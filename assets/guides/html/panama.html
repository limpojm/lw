Investor Entry Routes

  • Proinvex, the investment and export promotion agency of the Ministry of Commerce and Industries (MICI), provides facilitation and sector guidance for foreign investors
  • Law 54 of 1998 on legal stability of investments, offering registered investors protection against adverse changes in key legal, tax and customs conditions
  • Multinational Headquarters (SEM) and Multinational Manufacturing Services (EMMA) regimes offering preferential tax, migration and labour treatment for regional headquarters, logistics and manufacturing operations
  • Colón Free Zone and the Panamá Pacífico special economic area, with customs and tax incentives for distribution, logistics and light-industrial projects
  • Dollarised economy: the US dollar circulates as legal tender alongside the balboa, removing currency-conversion risk (S&P transfer and convertibility assessment: AAA)
  • Panama Canal Authority concessions and tenders, including new port terminals, a canal pipeline project, the Logistics Corridor and the Río Indio reservoir (tender planned for 2027)
  • International banking centre supervised by the Superintendency of Banks, strengthened by a 2026 economic substance law that led to Panama's removal from the EU tax blacklist on 9 October 2026

Market-Entry Observation

Panama in October 2026 is one of Latin America's fastest-growing economies, powered by its logistics platform. GDP expanded 5.5% in the first half of 2026, accelerating to 6.4% in the second quarter, after 4.4% growth in 2025, led by transport and logistics (+10.4%) and trade (+8.8%). Foreign direct investment recovered to $792.4 million in H1 2026, from a net outflow of $141.8 million a year earlier, with reinvested earnings accounting for 73%. Reform momentum is clear: the fiscal deficit fell to 3.7% of GDP in 2025, within the 4% legal ceiling, with a 3.5% target for 2026; a landmark pension reform passed in March 2025; S&P affirmed Panama's BBB- investment grade with a stable outlook in September 2026; and the EU removed Panama from its tax blacklist on 9 October 2026, following its exit from the FATF grey list in 2023.

Panama's principal structural task is rebuilding investment momentum and fiscal headroom after the closure of Cobre Panamá in late 2023, which the IMF estimates cost about 4.5% of GDP and contributed to a 63% fall in FDI to $905 million in 2025. Progress is now visible: the government has authorised sales of stockpiled concentrate and the mine's power plant has restarted, an environmental audit found 87.7% compliance, First Quantum has suspended its $20 billion arbitration claim, and the government is weighing three ownership and operating models for a possible long-term restart. Public debt stood at B/.61.9 billion at end-May 2026, and Moody's (Baa3, negative outlook) expects it to stabilise at around 66–67% of GDP as the deficit target falls to 3% in 2027. A durable mining settlement, continued fiscal consolidation and the Canal Authority's multi-billion-dollar project pipeline together offer a clear path to renewed investor confidence.

Investor Risk Note

Panama's risk profile is that of a strongly positioned, dollarised logistics hub working through a period of fiscal and investment rebuilding. Growth of 5.5% in H1 2026, record canal revenue, a recovery in FDI, S&P's stable investment-grade rating and removal from the EU tax blacklist all point to improving momentum. The main considerations are the unresolved long-term future of Cobre Panamá, a public debt stock that the government is stabilising through a legally binding deficit path, the transition of the Balboa and Cristóbal ports following the January 2026 Supreme Court ruling, and the canal's sensitivity to rainfall in El Niño years. Each is being actively managed: stockpile processing and formal talks at the mine, fiscal rules with falling targets, temporary operators guaranteeing port continuity, and long-term water investment through the Río Indio reservoir. Investors should structure contracts carefully, using legal stability agreements and international arbitration, and can take comfort from the absence of currency risk and the depth of Panama's trade and logistics franchise.

Market Access

Panama is a WTO member and a member of the Central American Integration System (SICA), and it trades through a broad network of agreements, including the US–Panama Trade Promotion Agreement, the EU–Central America Association Agreement and the UK–Central America Association Agreement. It became a Mercosur associate state in December 2024, and the National Assembly approved the Economic Complementation Agreement with Mercosur in October 2025, opening the way to bilateral deals beginning with Brazil. Its national port system handles around 9.4 million TEU a year, the canal links Atlantic and Pacific trade lanes, the Colón Free Zone serves as a re-export platform for Latin America, and Tocumen airport connects the Americas. This combination of trade agreements and physical connectivity makes Panama a natural regional hub for distribution, services and headquarters functions.

Current International Business Channels

The American Chamber of Commerce and Industry of Panama (AmCham Panama), the Panama Chamber of Commerce, Industries and Agriculture (CCIAP) and the Panamanian Association of Business Executives (APEDE) are the leading business associations, with the Industrialists' Union of Panama (SIP) representing manufacturers and the Panama Mining Chamber active on the Cobre Panamá debate. More than 100 US companies operate in Panama, and many multinationals run regional headquarters from Panama City under the SEM regime, providing a strong peer network. The Inter-American Development Bank, World Bank, IFC and CAF maintain active programmes in infrastructure, water and private-sector development, and the Panama Canal Authority's industry consultations on new ports and logistics projects are a valuable forum for prospective bidders.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Panama suits investors seeking a stable, dollarised base for regional operations: logistics, shipping and port investors positioned around the canal and its new concessions; multinationals establishing regional headquarters or distribution centres under the SEM, EMMA and free-zone regimes; financial-services firms building on the international banking centre and the improved standing that follows EU delisting; and infrastructure, water and energy investors targeting Canal Authority projects such as the Río Indio reservoir. Mining and mining-services investors should follow the Cobre Panamá negotiations closely, while tourism and aviation investors can build on strong growth at Tocumen.

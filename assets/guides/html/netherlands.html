The economy today

The Netherlands in November 2026 is one of Europe's most open and productive economies, combining a world-class port and logistics system, a globally important high-tech cluster and a predictable tax environment. GDP grew 1.8% in 2025, and growth has accelerated in 2026: the economy expanded 0.6% quarter-on-quarter and 2.1% year-on-year in the third quarter, with manufacturing the largest contributor and exports of machinery and transport equipment rising. Foreign companies and investors invested €72 billion in the Netherlands in the first half of 2026, mainly from the United States and Germany, and the NFIA supported 180 foreign investment projects in 2025, 30% of them focused on R&D. Public finances are sound, with a 2025 deficit of 1.6% of GDP, and the minority cabinet led by Prime Minister Rob Jetten, sworn in on 23 February 2026, has set out a 2027 deficit target of 2.2% of GDP alongside higher defence spending.

The Netherlands' main structural challenge is physical capacity: electricity grid congestion, labour shortages and housing are limiting how fast new industrial and technology investment can be connected and staffed. Many industrial firms are on waiting lists for stronger grid connections, and inflation rose to 3.7% in October 2026, driven by energy prices. The response is substantial and well-funded. Project Beethoven commits €2.5 billion to the Eindhoven region for 33,000 additional technicians by 2030, transport, nearly 20,000 homes and grid upgrades; the first 32 km section of the national hydrogen network is operating in Rotterdam; and grid operators are expanding capacity. For investors, this creates opportunities in grid technology, battery storage, on-site generation, housing and technical training, while sites with available grid capacity command a premium.

How do I get in?

Netherlands Foreign Investment Agency (NFIA / Invest in Holland), which supported 180 foreign investment projects in 2025, with R&D the largest category for the first time

Corporate income tax of 19% on profits up to €200,000 and 25.8% above, with an innovation box taxing qualifying intellectual-property profits at an effective 9%

WBSO R&D credit reducing payroll tax by 36% of the first €391,020 of eligible R&D costs (50% for start-ups), administered by the Netherlands Enterprise Agency (RVO)

Advance Tax Rulings and Advance Pricing Agreements giving up to five years of certainty, with a dedicated liaison for potential foreign investors

The 30% ruling for qualifying skilled migrants, capped at €78,600 in 2026 and reducing to 27% from 1 January 2027

Euro-area, EU single-market and Schengen membership, with free capital movement and around 100 bilateral tax treaties

Trade and market access

As an EU member, the Netherlands gives investors tariff-free access to the EU single market and to all EU free trade agreements, with WTO membership and the EU common trade policy governing trade with third countries. Euro membership removes currency risk across the euro area, and Schengen membership allows passport-free movement. The Port of Rotterdam, Schiphol airport and dense road, rail and inland-waterway links along the Rhine make the country Europe's leading gateway for goods, with deep-sea container volumes to and from North America rising strongly in 2026. Germany and Belgium are its closest markets, and the planned extension of the hydrogen network to both countries reinforces the Netherlands' role as an energy hub for north-west Europe.

The overall risk picture

The Netherlands has a low-risk profile as a founding EU member, euro-area economy and FATF member with sound public finances and strong legal institutions. The 2025 deficit of 1.6% of GDP and a 2027 target of 2.2% leave the country well inside EU fiscal rules, and growth accelerated to 2.1% year-on-year in the third quarter of 2026. The main points to monitor are the parliamentary arithmetic of the minority cabinet, which holds 66 of 150 seats and must negotiate its budget with opposition parties, and the effect of higher energy prices on inflation, which reached 3.7% in October 2026. Investors in semiconductors and other sensitive technologies should plan for national-security screening and export-control requirements, as the Nexperia case showed; these are a compliance consideration rather than a barrier for most sectors. Practical constraints such as grid congestion are best managed through early site due diligence.

Prime Minister's Office (Prime Minister Rob Jetten)

Leads the D66–VVD–CDA minority cabinet sworn in on 23 February 2026 and sets the government's overall direction

Primary reference point for national priorities and major strategic projects

Netherlands Foreign Investment Agency (NFIA / Invest in Holland)

National investment promotion agency working with regional development agencies to support foreign investors

Standard first point of contact for site selection, incentives and introductions

De Nederlandsche Bank (DNB)

National central bank within the Eurosystem and banking supervisor; the ECB deposit rate has been 2.50% since September 2026

Relevant for banking, payments and financial-sector licensing

Ministry of Finance (Minister Eelco Heinen)

Leads the budget and tax policy, including the 2027 budget and planned wealth-tax reform

Key counterpart on tax rulings policy and public finances

Ministry of Economic Affairs (Minister Heleen Herbert)

Oversees industrial policy, the NFIA, the Netherlands Enterprise Agency and economic-security measures

Relevant for incentives, innovation funding and investment screening

Minister for Foreign Trade and Development (Sjoerd Sjoerdsma)

Responsible for trade promotion, export policy and development cooperation

Counterpart for trade missions and international partnerships

Port of Rotterdam Authority and Brainport Development

Essential partners for logistics, energy and semiconductor investments

Business networks

The American Chamber of Commerce in the Netherlands, the Netherlands British Chamber of Commerce, the German-Dutch Chamber of Commerce and the employers' federation VNO-NCW are the main business associations, with regular dialogue with government on tax and regulation. The NFIA works with regional development agencies such as those covering Rotterdam, Amsterdam and Brainport Eindhoven to support new investors, and the Netherlands Enterprise Agency administers innovation, export and subsidy schemes. Invest-NL, the national promotional investment institution, and the European Investment Bank co-finance energy transition and innovation projects. Embassies of major partners maintain commercial sections that are a useful source of introductions.

Who this market suits

The Netherlands suits investors seeking a stable, well-connected European base: companies establishing European headquarters, distribution and logistics centres around Amsterdam, Schiphol and Rotterdam; semiconductor, photonics and high-tech firms joining the Brainport ecosystem and using the innovation box and WBSO credit; energy-transition investors in hydrogen, carbon capture, offshore wind, grid technology and storage; and R&D-intensive life sciences and technology companies. Investors in sensitive technologies should plan for national-security screening.

The economy today

Latvia in November 2026 offers investors euro-area and EU membership, a tax system that leaves reinvested profits untaxed and public debt well below the EU average. GDP grew 3.0% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, the strongest growth since early 2022, led by trade, manufacturing, finance and information and communication. Latvijas Banka forecasts growth of 2.0% in 2026, rising to 2.4% in 2027 and 3.0% in 2028, supported by defence, dual-use production, housing and large public investment. The investment agency LIAA attracted a record €1.01 billion of foreign investment across 31 projects in 2025, led by bioeconomy and smart-energy projects, and reports a pipeline of about 180 projects worth more than €17 billion. In the 3 October 2026 election, Prime Minister Andris Kulbergs' United List won 35.3% of the vote and 41 of 100 seats, giving centre-right parties the mandate to govern.

Latvia's main structural challenge is financing a large defence build-up while keeping public finances and costs under control. The general government deficit was 2.5% of GDP in 2025 and debt 46.9% of GDP, but defence spending of at least 5% of GDP from 2027 is pushing the deficit above 3%, and Latvijas Banka expects debt to rise to slightly above 50% of GDP. Inflation was 3.2% in September 2026, and labour costs are rising quickly. The starting position is sound: Fitch rates Latvia A- with a stable outlook, the Treasury plans to keep debt below 55% of GDP, and euro membership lowers funding costs. Defence, Rail Baltica and energy investment are creating sustained demand, and the stock of foreign direct investment, at €27 billion at the end of 2025, leaves considerable room for growth compared with Baltic neighbours.

How do I get in?

The Investment and Development Agency of Latvia (LIAA), through Invest in Latvia, supports foreign investors and helped attract a record €1.01 billion across 31 projects in 2025

A corporate income tax of 20% charged only on distributed profits, with reinvested profits taxed at 0%

Free ports at Riga and Ventspils and special economic zones at Liepāja, Rēzekne and Latgale offering corporate income tax and property tax rebates, expected to remain in place until 2035

Euro-area membership: the euro is the national currency, with free capital movement under EU rules and monetary policy set by the European Central Bank

EU single-market and Schengen membership, giving free movement of goods, services, capital and people and access to all EU trade agreements

Defence spending of 4.9% of GDP in 2026 and a legal minimum of 5% from 2027, opening supply routes for defence and dual-use manufacturers

Rail Baltica and other EU-co-financed infrastructure creating construction, engineering and logistics opportunities

Trade and market access

As a member of the EU, Latvia gives investors tariff-free access to the EU single market and to all the EU's free trade agreements, with WTO membership and the EU's common trade policy governing trade with third countries. Euro membership removes exchange-rate costs with euro-area partners, and Schengen membership allows passport-free travel. Latvia's Baltic ports at Riga, Ventspils and Liepāja connect it by sea with the Nordic countries and Western Europe, and Rail Baltica will link the Baltic states to the European standard-gauge network. Nordic and Baltic investors are prominent, with Lithuania, Finland and Sweden among the leading sources of new projects in 2025, and the defence build-up across the region is creating demand for local supply chains.

The overall risk picture

Latvia's risk profile is that of a stable EU, euro-area and NATO member with low public debt, a competitive tax regime that exempts reinvested profits and growing investor interest, reflected in record LIAA-supported investment in 2025. The economy grew 3.0% in the second quarter of 2026, and the October 2026 election gave the United List a clear lead in forming the next government. The main near-term issues are a deficit expected to exceed 3% of GDP as defence spending rises to at least 5% of GDP, and rising labour costs; both are manageable given debt of 46.9% of GDP and an A- rating. Latvia borders Russia and Belarus, faces hybrid threats including drone incursions, and applies EU sanctions on both countries in full, so investors should take sanctions and export-control advice for any related business and include security planning for eastern locations. Outside these areas, Latvia offers the legal certainty of EU law and the convenience of the euro.

Business networks

The Latvian Chamber of Commerce and Industry, national employers' organisations, foreign investors' associations and bilateral chambers of commerce are the main business networks, with regular dialogue with government. LIAA runs investor programmes, represents Latvia at international forums and manages a pipeline of about 180 projects, while the free port and special economic zone authorities work directly with incoming investors. EU funds and European development finance institutions are relevant co-financing partners for infrastructure, energy and defence-related projects. Embassies of major EU partners and the United States maintain commercial sections that are a useful source of introductions.

Who this market suits

Latvia suits investors seeking a cost-competitive base inside the EU and the euro area where reinvested profits are untaxed: bioeconomy, wood-processing and renewable-fuel investors using ports and special economic zones; smart-energy and grid investors; business-services and IT companies drawing on Riga's talent pool; defence and dual-use manufacturers serving a build-up to at least 5% of GDP; and construction and engineering firms targeting Rail Baltica. Investors with any links to Russia or Belarus should take sanctions advice before engagement.

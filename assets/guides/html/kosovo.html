The economy today

Kosovo in November 2026 is one of the faster-growing economies in Europe, with a young population, a large diaspora, low public debt and use of the euro. GDP grew 3.6% in 2025, according to the IMF, and accelerated to 5.27% year-on-year in the first quarter of 2026 and 4.06% in the second, with fixed investment up 11.91% and exports up 6.82%. Public debt was just 17.5% of GDP in 2025 and the deficit 0.8%. Foreign direct investment was projected by the central bank governor at about €950 million in 2025, roughly 20% higher than in 2024, and diaspora remittances at about €1.4 billion. After three elections since February 2025, Prime Minister Albin Kurti's fourth government was approved on 13 September 2026 and parliament elected Justina Shiroka Pula as president on 7 October 2026, ending a long period of political deadlock.

Kosovo's main structural challenges are its incomplete international recognition and the need to turn political stability into faster reform. Kosovo is not recognised by Serbia or by several EU member states, including Spain, Greece and Romania, which limits its membership of some international organisations and complicates its EU path; it applied for EU membership in December 2022. Relations with the EU have improved markedly: the restrictive measures imposed in 2023 were lifted after December 2025, freeing about €205 million in assistance, and Commission President von der Leyen visited Pristina on 1 October 2026 to confirm up to €882 million under the Growth Plan, of which €62 million has been received. Inflation of 7.8% in September 2026, driven by transport and energy costs, is a near-term pressure, but low debt, a sound banking sector and EU funding give room to invest in energy, transport and skills.

How do I get in?

Kosovo Investment and Enterprise Support Agency (KIESA) provides facilitation, information and aftercare for foreign investors

Law on Strategic Investments, under which large projects can be designated strategic and receive dedicated government support

A flat 10% corporate income tax, one of the lowest rates in Europe

Use of the euro as the domestic currency, removing exchange-rate risk for euro-based investors

Stabilisation and Association Agreement with the EU, giving preferential access for Kosovo products to the EU market, and full participation in CEFTA

Competitive renewable-energy auctions with 15-year contracts for difference, as used for the 105 MW Lindja Solar plant

EU Growth Plan funding of up to €882 million, linked to reforms that open parts of the EU single market to the region

Trade and market access

Kosovo's Stabilisation and Association Agreement with the EU provides preferential access for its products to the EU market, and Kosovo now represents itself fully in the Central European Free Trade Agreement, which covers trade with its Western Balkan neighbours. Use of the euro removes exchange-rate costs for trade with the euro area. The ban on Serbian finished goods was lifted in October 2024, easing regional supply chains. Kosovo is landlocked, with road links to the Albanian port of Durrës and road and rail links to North Macedonia and on to Thessaloniki. The EU Growth Plan is set to bring Kosovo closer to the single market, with SEPA payments membership and lower roaming charges among the steps tied to reform.

The overall risk picture

Kosovo's risk profile combines strong fundamentals with specific political and legal considerations. Public debt of 17.5% of GDP, a sound banking sector with non-performing loans of 1.8% and use of the euro provide a stable macroeconomic base, and Kosovo is not on the FATF grey list or subject to international sanctions. Investors should, however, factor in its incomplete recognition: Serbia and several EU member states do not recognise Kosovo, which limits access to some international bodies and slows its EU path. The 2025–2026 political deadlock has ended with a new government and president, and relations with the EU have improved, with restrictive measures lifted and Growth Plan funding flowing. Tensions in the north and weak contract enforcement are best managed through local counsel, international arbitration clauses and close engagement with EU and US partners, who remain Kosovo's main supporters.

Business networks

The Kosovo Chamber of Commerce, the American Chamber of Commerce in Kosovo, the German-Kosovar Chamber of Commerce and the Kosovo Business Alliance are the main business associations, with regular dialogue with government. KIESA organises investor and diaspora events. The European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, the European Investment Bank, the World Bank and the EU's Western Balkans Investment Framework are active in energy, transport and private-sector finance, and the EU Reform and Growth Facility provides additional funding linked to reforms. Embassies of EU member states, the United Kingdom, Switzerland and the United States maintain commercial sections that are a useful source of introductions, and diaspora networks in Germany and Switzerland are an important source of partners and capital.

Who this market suits

Kosovo suits investors seeking a low-cost, low-tax, euro-based market with a young workforce and strong diaspora links: renewable-energy and storage developers using competitive auctions; ICT and business-services firms drawing on young, multilingual talent in Pristina; construction, real-estate and building-materials companies serving diaspora demand; agri-food and beverage producers; and engineering firms working on EU- and DFI-financed energy and transport projects. Investors should be comfortable with recognition-related constraints and use international arbitration.

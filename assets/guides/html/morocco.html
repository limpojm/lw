The economy today

Morocco in October 2026 combines macroeconomic stability, investment-grade credit standing and a deep industrial export base. GDP grew close to 5% in 2025 and Bank Al-Maghrib projects 4.4% in 2026, with second-quarter growth of 4% supported by a 21.2% rebound in agriculture. Inflation was just 0.5% year-on-year in September 2026, allowing the central bank to hold its policy rate at 2.25%. FDI inflows rose 91% to $3.33 billion in 2025 (UNCTAD), lifting the FDI stock to $80.8 billion, while the National Investment Commission has approved 381 projects worth MAD 581 billion and around 245,000 jobs. S&P restored Morocco to investment grade (BBB-) in September 2025 and Moody's moved its outlook to positive in March 2026. A new coalition government under Fatima Ezzahra El Mansouri, Morocco's first woman Head of Government, was sworn in on 16 October 2026, with continuity in the key economic, industry and foreign affairs portfolios.

Morocco's main structural challenge is water. Seven consecutive years of drought weighed on agriculture and rural employment until heavy rainfall in winter 2025–26, which lifted dam reserves to 76% in May 2026 from 40% a year earlier. The state has responded with a long-term programme that reduces dependence on rainfall: desalination capacity of 345 million cubic metres a year is set to rise to 1.7 billion by 2030, supplying 60% of drinking water, led by the 300 million cubic metre Casablanca plant. This creates a substantial pipeline of opportunities in desalination, renewable power, water networks and efficient irrigation. Job creation for young people is the parallel priority: unemployment fell to 9.5% in Q2 2026 but stands at 27.2% for 15–24 year-olds, and the new government has made employment, education and health its central commitments.

How do I get in?

Moroccan Investment and Export Development Agency (AMDIE) as the national investment promotion body, working with 12 Regional Investment Centres (CRIs) that handle project facilitation in each region

Investment Charter (Law 03-22): job-linked grants of 5–10% of investment, territorial premiums of 10–15% for less-developed provinces, sectoral support of up to 5%, and negotiated State agreements for strategic projects of MAD 2 billion or more

Casablanca Finance City, Africa's top-ranked financial centre, offering a dedicated regulatory and tax framework for regional headquarters, banks, insurers and investment firms targeting West and Central Africa

Mohammed VI Investment Fund (state-owned, MAD 15 billion initial capital), which co-invests through sectoral funds with private managers such as A.P. Moller Capital's MAD 2.24 billion logistics fund

Bank Al-Maghrib manages the dirham against a euro–dollar basket and is moving gradually towards greater exchange-rate flexibility; the Office des Changes administers the rules on transfers of investment income and capital

Sector-entry routes: partnerships with OCP for phosphates, joint ventures with ONHYM (25% state stake) for hydrocarbons, and 99-year leases for agricultural land; foreign ownership is capped at 49% in air and maritime transport and fisheries

The government's green hydrogen offer, which has drawn more than 40 proposals and approved eight projects worth about $43 billion

Trade and market access

Morocco is the only African country with a free trade agreement with the United States, has a free-trade area with the European Union, and is a member of the African Continental Free Trade Area, giving manufacturers based in Morocco preferential access to markets on three continents. Exports reached MAD 470 billion in 2025, led by automotive (MAD 159.2 billion, the top export sector for the third year running), phosphates and derivatives (MAD 99.7 billion) and agri-food (MAD 87.5 billion), with aerospace growing by almost 10%. Tanger Med is the country's main gateway, connecting Morocco to European and global shipping lines on the Strait of Gibraltar, and will be complemented by Nador West Med from late 2026 and Dakhla Atlantic by 2028, the latter designed to open an Atlantic route for landlocked Sahel economies. This combination of trade agreements and port capacity makes Morocco a natural platform for nearshoring to Europe and for expansion into West Africa.

The overall risk picture

Morocco's risk profile is among the strongest in Africa: it is the continent's only investment-grade sovereign with S&P, Moody's has a positive outlook, the IMF confirmed in March 2026 that it continues to qualify for the Flexible Credit Line, and inflation is near zero. Political continuity is well established under the monarchy, and the October 2026 transition to a new coalition government kept the key economic and foreign affairs ministers in place. The main sensitivities are structural rather than cyclical: water availability, which a large desalination programme is steadily addressing, and youth employment, which is the new government's central priority. Investors with activities, suppliers or sourcing linked to the southern provinces should treat Western Sahara as a specific legal and compliance matter, taking advice on EU rules of origin and labelling and on applicable international law. With sound buffers, a clear incentive framework and growing reform momentum, Morocco offers a predictable base for long-term investment.

Business networks

The Confédération Générale des Entreprises du Maroc (CGEM) is the principal private-sector federation and the natural counterpart for domestic business liaison, alongside the American Chamber of Commerce in Morocco and European bilateral chambers of commerce. AMDIE and the Regional Investment Centres organise investor outreach and sector events, and Casablanca Finance City acts as a networking hub for financial services and Africa-focused investment. The Mohammed VI Investment Fund is an increasingly important co-investment partner through its sectoral funds. The World Bank Group, African Development Bank and EBRD maintain active programmes in Morocco covering infrastructure, water, energy transition and private-sector development, and the IMF's Flexible Credit Line underlines the country's standing with international institutions.

Who this market suits

Morocco suits investors seeking a stable, investment-grade base close to Europe with access to African markets: automotive, aerospace and battery-supply-chain manufacturers using the Tanger Med and Kenitra clusters and Morocco's FTA network; infrastructure, water and renewable-energy investors taking part in desalination, green hydrogen, ports and World Cup 2030 projects; financial-services and fintech firms using Casablanca Finance City as a regional hub; and tourism, agri-food and logistics investors building on record visitor numbers and export growth. Investors considering activity in the southern provinces should factor in specialist legal advice as a standard part of due diligence.

    Engage AMDIE and the relevant Regional Investment CentreOpen dialogue early to identify eligible Investment Charter premiums and, for projects of MAD 2 billion or more, the route to a negotiated State agreement.
  2. 2
    Build relationships with the new governmentBrief the incoming ministers for industry and investment, economy and finance, and energy on project plans, aligning with their focus on jobs and the World Cup 2030 programme.
  3. 3
    Evaluate Casablanca Finance City for regional structuringConsider CFC status for financial-services operations or an Africa regional headquarters to benefit from its dedicated regulatory and tax framework.
  4. 4
    Position for water, energy and infrastructure pipelinesTrack desalination, renewable-power, green hydrogen, port and rail tenders, and explore co-investment with the Mohammed VI Investment Fund or partnerships with OCP and other state companies.
  5. 5
    Complete legal and currency due diligenceConfirm sector ownership rules and fund-transfer arrangements with local counsel and banks, and take specialist advice on any activity linked to the southern provinces.

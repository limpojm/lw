The economy today

Trinidad and Tobago enters late 2026 with strengthening international standing and a clear growth catalyst ahead. S&P reaffirmed the country's investment-grade BBB- rating in July 2026, Moody's moved its Ba2 outlook to stable in June, and the EU removed Trinidad and Tobago from its list of non-cooperative tax jurisdictions in February 2026 after the Free Trade Zone regime was replaced by a modern Special Economic Zone framework. After a contraction of 0.5% in 2025 and a projected 0.2% in 2026, the October 2026 budget projects a return to 2.5% growth in 2027 as new gas fields come on stream. Inflation is low at 0.6% (July 2026), the repo rate has been held at 3.50%, net reserves stood at $5.3 billion (6.1 months of import cover) in August 2026, and the Heritage and Stabilisation Fund held about $6.1 billion. Net FDI flows have been negative as energy companies restructured portfolios, but announced greenfield investment rose to $1.76 billion across seven projects in 2025, and a 2025 shallow-water bid round secured $1.08 billion in commitments.

The country's main structural challenge is natural gas supply. Output from mature fields has declined, leaving Point Lisas with tight supply: Methanex announced in June 2026 the idling of its 860,000-tonne Titan methanol plant, with its Atlas plant already preserved, and Nutrien closed its ammonia and urea operations, reducing export and foreign-exchange earnings. Trinidad and Tobago is meeting this with a concentrated pipeline of new supply: Shell's Manatee (peak about 104,000 boe/d) and Aphrodite, and bp's Ginger and Coconut, all target first gas in 2027, while US licences issued in October 2025 and February 2026, together with a Shell framework agreement with Venezuela in March 2026, have reopened cross-border gas, with Dragon expected to add about 350 million cubic feet a day from late 2027. Idled plants have been preserved for restart, so returning gas can translate quickly into higher exports, and investors entering now can position for the 2027–28 upswing in gas, petrochemicals and energy services.

How do I get in?

Global Trinidad and Tobago (Global TT), formed in April 2025 by consolidating InvesTT, exporTT and CreativeTT, is the single trade and investment promotion agency for investor facilitation and sector guidance

Foreign Investment Act 1990 allowing full foreign ownership of private companies (licensing above 30% in public companies), with October 2026 budget amendments simplifying land and share acquisitions up to $100,000

Special Economic Zones Act 2022, administered by the Special Economic Zones Authority, offering 15% corporation tax, a 150% enhanced initial capital allowance and zero customs duty and VAT on qualifying supplies

Competitive upstream bid rounds run by the Ministry of Energy and Energy Industries, supported by a 30% petroleum investment tax credit and 140% deepwater allowances

Central Bank of Trinidad and Tobago oversight of a managed TT dollar; there are no legal restrictions on repatriating earnings or capital, although bank foreign-exchange allocations can involve waiting times

Investor protection through 12 bilateral investment treaties, 17 double taxation treaties and membership of ICSID, MIGA and the New York Convention

Sector incentive packages, including tourism tax holidays of up to seven years and a 90% initial capital allowance on manufacturing plant and machinery

Trade and market access

Trinidad and Tobago is a member of CARICOM and its common market, giving preferential access to Caribbean partners, with further CARICOM preferences in Cuba, the Dominican Republic, Venezuela, Colombia and Costa Rica, and is a WTO member. It trades with Europe under the CARIFORUM-EU Economic Partnership Agreement and the UK-CARIFORUM agreement, with Canada under CARIBCAN and with the United States under the Caribbean Basin Economic Recovery Act, and is negotiating agreements with Chile and Curaçao. Energy and petrochemical exports such as LNG, ammonia, urea and methanol account for around 80% of export earnings, while agricultural and food exports go chiefly to the United States, Jamaica, Guyana, Barbados and Suriname. Growing energy cooperation with Guyana, Suriname and Venezuela reinforces the country's role as the Caribbean's gas-processing and energy-services hub.

The overall risk picture

Trinidad and Tobago's risk profile is that of an established, investment-grade energy economy at a turning point. Gas supply has tightened as mature fields decline, which has idled some Point Lisas capacity and placed pressure on foreign-exchange availability, but the response is concrete: several major fields are scheduled for first gas in 2027, cross-border Venezuelan gas has been reopened under specific US licences, and the budget projects growth of 2.5% in 2027. Strong buffers, including $5.3 billion in net reserves and the Heritage and Stabilisation Fund, the S&P reaffirmation, Moody's stable outlook and removal from the EU tax list all support confidence. Investors should treat any Venezuela-linked activity as a compliance matter, proceeding only within issued OFAC licences and with sanctions counsel, and should plan foreign-exchange needs early; those that do are well placed to benefit from the coming recovery in gas output.

Who should I talk to first?

Office of the Prime Minister (Kamla Persad-Bissessar)

Leads the government elected in April 2025 and sets economic policy, including energy diplomacy with Venezuela and the United States

Primary reference point for strategic direction and major project priorities

Global Trinidad and Tobago (Global TT)

Consolidated trade and investment promotion agency combining the former InvesTT, exporTT and CreativeTT

Standard first point of contact for market entry and incentive guidance

Central Bank of Trinidad and Tobago

Sets the repo rate (3.50%) and manages the TT dollar and foreign-exchange support to banks

Relevant for currency, banking and foreign-exchange planning

Ministry of Finance (Davendranath Tancoo)

Leads fiscal policy, the Heritage and Stabilisation Fund and amendments to the Foreign Investment and Exchange Control Acts

Key counterpart on tax, incentives and investment-law changes

Ministry of Energy and Energy Industries (Roodal Moonilal)

Runs upstream bid rounds and leads cross-border gas negotiations with Venezuela

Essential for upstream licensing and energy-sector engagement

National Gas Company (NGC)

State gas aggregator and partner in the Dragon project, central to gas supply contracts for Point Lisas

Core counterpart for gas supply and downstream investment

Special Economic Zones Authority (SEZA)

Licenses and regulates SEZ operators and enterprises under the 2022 Act

Gateway for manufacturing, logistics and ICT projects seeking SEZ incentives

Business networks

The Trinidad and Tobago Chamber of Industry and Commerce and the Energy Chamber of Trinidad and Tobago are the principal business associations, with the Energy Chamber the leading voice for operators, contractors and service providers at Point Lisas and offshore. Global TT runs investor outreach and sector programmes, and the Ministry of Trade, Investment and Tourism's investments team supports project facilitation. International energy companies including Shell, bp and EOG Resources maintain major in-country operations and are a useful source of practical intelligence on the gas outlook. The Inter-American Development Bank and the IMF engage closely on economic reform, while the EU Delegation supports trade under the CARIFORUM-EU agreement.

Who this market suits

Trinidad and Tobago suits investors positioning for the 2027–28 recovery in gas output: upstream and oilfield-services companies, subsea engineering firms and petrochemical investors able to take advantage of preserved, restart-ready capacity at Point Lisas. Nearshore manufacturers, logistics and bunkering operators, and ICT and business process outsourcing firms can benefit from the 15% SEZ tax rate, an English-speaking workforce with 98.8% literacy and proximity to North American markets. Tourism and hospitality investors can draw on generous incentives in Tobago. Export-oriented businesses earning hard currency are especially well placed, while investors with Venezuela-linked interests should work within OFAC licences and with specialist counsel.

Investor Entry Routes

  • Ghana Investment Promotion Authority (GIPA), which replaced the GIPC under the GIPA Act, 2026 (Act 1173), provides a one-stop investment shop and an Investor Grievance Mechanism
  • GIPA Act, 2026 removes minimum capital requirements for joint ventures and wholly foreign-owned enterprises; foreign-owned trading companies require $500,000 and 75% Ghanaian skilled staff, and certain activities remain reserved for Ghanaians
  • Free zone licensing under the Free Zones Act, 1995 (Act 504): 10-year corporate tax holiday and import exemptions for enterprises exporting at least 70% of output
  • Upstream petroleum agreements with GNPC and mining leases ratified by Parliament (the Ewoyaa lithium lease carries a 13% state interest) for extractive-sector entry
  • Bank of Ghana oversight of the foreign-exchange market; remittances flow through licensed banks, and technology-transfer payments require GIPA registration of the underlying agreement
  • Reopened local capital markets, including a GH¢3.8 billion seven-year government bond in April 2026 and COCOBOD's proposed Cocoa Notes Programme
  • Concessions for private operators in electricity distribution (ECG and NEDCo), with a transaction adviser appointed and private participation targeted for early 2027

Market-Entry Observation

Ghana in October 2026 is one of Africa's clearest turnaround stories. GDP grew 6.0% in 2025, up from 5.8% in 2024, and 6.2% in the first half of 2026, led by services and an ICT sector expanding by more than 30%. Inflation has fallen from 23.8% in December 2024 to 5.2% in September 2026, the Bank of Ghana's policy rate stands at 14%, and gross reserves reached $12 billion (4.5 months of imports) in September. Public debt has dropped from 61.8% of GDP at end-2024 to around 45%. Ghana completed its $3 billion IMF programme in July 2026, with debt distress risk upgraded to moderate two years earlier than expected, and moved to a non-financing Policy Coordination Instrument. Fitch (B, positive outlook) and Moody's (B3) have upgraded the sovereign in 2026. Provisional GIPC data show foreign direct investment of $2.61 billion in 2025, against $652 million in 2024, and the new GIPA Act, 2026 has removed most minimum capital requirements for foreign investors.

Ghana's main structural task is to put the power sector on a sound commercial footing while broadening an export base in which gold accounts for around 67% of goods exports. Electricity distribution losses of 28–31% and foreign-exchange demand from the energy sector have contributed to a 9.5% cedi depreciation in the first nine months of 2026, after a 40.7% appreciation in 2025. The government is addressing this directly: it cleared $1.47 billion of legacy energy debts in 2025, restored the World Bank guarantee that supports nearly $8 billion of private gas investment, renegotiated power purchase agreements, and is bringing private operators into ECG and NEDCo through concessions from early 2027. Rising Jubilee output and domestic gas supply are cutting fuel import costs, while lithium, cocoa processing and AfCFTA-oriented manufacturing are widening the export base. For investors, these reforms create openings in power distribution, gas infrastructure, energy efficiency and export manufacturing.

Investor Risk Note

Ghana's risk profile has improved markedly since its 2022–23 debt restructuring. Successful completion of the IMF programme, a fall in public debt to around 45% of GDP, single-digit inflation, reserves of $12 billion and rating upgrades from Fitch and Moody's in 2026 show a credible and sustained policy reset, now anchored by a non-financing IMF Policy Coordination Instrument. The remaining points for investors are well defined and being actively managed: power-sector finances, a currency that responds to energy-sector and dividend demand for dollars, and refinancing of domestic debt maturing in February 2027, for which a dedicated sinking fund is in place. Ghana is a stable multiparty democracy with no international sanctions affecting mainstream investment. Investors should apply standard currency hedging, conduct counterparty due diligence on state-linked utilities, and can take confidence from the depth of reform and the broad base of current growth.

Market Access

Ghana is a member of ECOWAS, a founding member of the WTO and host of the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) Secretariat in Accra, giving investors a base for a continental market. It trades with the EU under an interim Economic Partnership Agreement and with the UK under the UK–Ghana Trade Partnership Agreement, with UK–Ghana trade at about £1.6 billion in the year to Q1 2026. US access under AGOA was extended to 31 December 2026, the US has removed a 15% tariff on a range of Ghanaian agricultural products, and Ghana plans to negotiate a bilateral trade agreement with the US. Exports reached $31.1 billion in 2025, led by gold ($20.9 billion), cocoa ($3.8 billion) and oil ($2.6 billion), and the ports of Tema and Takoradi serve as gateways to the West African coast and the landlocked Sahel.

Current International Business Channels

The UK–Ghana Chamber of Commerce is an active bilateral channel, running the UK–Ghana Trade Resource Centre and convening the UK–Ghana Trade and Investment Summit 2026 in Accra with the UK Department for Business and Trade. The Association of Ghana Industries and the Ghana National Chamber of Commerce and Industry are the main domestic business bodies, and the Ghana Export Promotion Authority supports export-oriented investors. GIPA runs investor outreach and aftercare, while the AfCFTA Secretariat in Accra makes the capital a natural venue for continental trade events. The IMF, now engaged through the Policy Coordination Instrument, and the World Bank, which backs gas-supply guarantees and education investment, are useful sources of policy insight, and international mining and oil companies with long-standing Ghana operations offer practical intelligence on operating conditions.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Ghana suits investors seeking exposure to a reforming, fast-growing West African economy with a strong rule-of-law tradition and an English-speaking business culture: energy investors and utility operators positioned for the ECG and NEDCo concessions and gas-to-power projects; mining and critical-minerals investors in gold and lithium; manufacturing and agro-processing investors using free zone incentives and AfCFTA access; technology and financial-services investors drawn by rapid ICT growth; and infrastructure investors following the Big Push programme. Investors with local-currency revenues should build standard hedging into project economics.

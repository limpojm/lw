The economy today

Anguilla in October 2026 is a small, high-value economy with strong public finances and a clear investment pipeline. Real GDP grew 3.7% in 2024, taking output above its pre-pandemic level, and the government projects 2.7% growth in 2026. Visitor arrivals reached an all-time high of 229,734 in 2025, with ten of twelve months setting records, while revenue from the .ai internet domain rose to about EC$230 million in 2025, more than double the 2024 figure. These revenues delivered a 2025 fiscal surplus of EC$87.3 million and cut public debt to an estimated 19.9% of GDP, against a regional benchmark of 60%, with no new borrowing planned over the medium term. The government is reinvesting through a EC$233 million capital programme in 2026, led by the airport runway extension, and private investment is following: net FDI was EC$50.9 million (3.8% of GDP) in 2024 and the Port Nimara superyacht marina and Equinox resort launched in 2026.

Anguilla's main structural challenge is concentration: the economy depends on luxury tourism, with the United States supplying over 70% of stayover visitors in peak months, and public revenue now relies heavily on .ai domain income. The territory is building resilience on several fronts. The government plans a Sovereign Wealth Fund to save part of the domain windfall and is using current surpluses to fund long-term assets such as the runway extension, a 12-megawatt solar farm and water and road upgrades. The longer runway, due in early 2029, will allow larger aircraft and new routes, broadening air access, while the solar farm reduces reliance on imported fuel. For investors, this combination of low debt, a stable dollar peg and well-funded infrastructure creates opportunity in hospitality, marine tourism, construction, renewable energy and services linked to the digital economy.

How do I get in?

Direct engagement with the Government of Anguilla, which offers development agreements and targeted incentives for large-scale projects such as resorts, marinas and branded residences

A simple tax regime with no corporate income, personal income, capital gains, inheritance or withholding taxes; a 9% Goods Tax on imports and a 13% General Services Tax apply from 1 August 2025

Alien Land Holding Licence for non-Anguillian property purchasers, a well-established step in transactions, with stamp duty of up to 12.5% for non-belongers and a 5% transfer tax payable by the seller

Membership of the Eastern Caribbean Currency Union: the EC dollar has been pegged at EC$2.70 to US$1 since 1976, there are no exchange controls and the US dollar is widely used

English common law framework with company, LLC, limited partnership and trust structures supervised by the Anguilla Financial Services Commission, backed by economic substance, beneficial ownership and AML/CFT rules

Public capital programme of EC$233 million in 2026, covering the airport, port, water network and roads, alongside CDB-financed energy projects, creating openings for contractors and suppliers

Work permits under the Labour Act where local skills are limited, with social security contributions of 5.75% each for employer and employee

Trade and market access

Anguilla is a member of the Eastern Caribbean Currency Union, sharing the EC dollar and the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank with seven other OECS economies, and is an associate member of both the Organisation of Eastern Caribbean States and CARICOM (since 1999). As a British Overseas Territory it benefits from the UK constitutional link and close ties with the UK government. The United States is the dominant market for its main export, tourism, supplying over 70% of stayover visitors in peak months, and sea arrivals through Blowing Point account for most visitors in the busiest months. Anguilla has no double tax treaties, so cross-border structuring requires specialist advice. The runway extension and the Port Nimara port of entry for superyachts will widen direct air and sea access in the coming years.

The overall risk picture

Anguilla's risk profile is that of a well-governed, low-debt British Overseas Territory with an English common-law system, a currency pegged to the US dollar for five decades and public finances in surplus. The principal considerations are concentration and compliance rather than macroeconomic stability. Domain revenue has transformed the fiscal position, and the government is using it prudently: debt has fallen to about 19.9% of GDP, no new borrowing is planned and a Sovereign Wealth Fund is proposed. Anguilla's continued presence on the EU's Annex I list is a material compliance point for financial and holding structures, and investors should take tax advice for any EU-linked flows; it has little bearing on direct investment in hotels, real estate, construction or energy. Public safety has received focused attention, with UK support and a 25% rise in police funding. Investors in tourism should plan for seasonal demand and for the airport works through 2029, after which improved air access should support further growth.

Business networks

The Anguilla Hotel and Tourism Association represents the island's core hospitality industry and is a practical first contact for tourism investors, while the Anguilla Tourist Board provides market data and promotion. The Caribbean Development Bank is an active lender, financing the Corito solar farm, and the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank's regional forums and Monetary Council meetings, attended by the Premier, offer insight into ECCU policy. UK engagement runs through the Governor's Office and the FCDO's Minister for the Overseas Territories. Developers and partners in flagship projects such as Port Nimara, together with established resort operators, are useful sources of practical intelligence on approvals, construction and the luxury market.

Who this market suits

Anguilla best suits investors in luxury and boutique hospitality, branded residences and marine tourism who value a stable, low-density, high-value destination with a US-dollar-linked currency and no income or corporate taxes. Construction, engineering and aviation-services firms can target the airport runway extension and the public capital programme, while renewable-energy and utility investors can build on the solar programme. Digital and technology businesses may find opportunities linked to the .ai profile. Financial and holding-structure investors should factor in the EU Annex I listing and take specialist tax advice.

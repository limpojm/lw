The economy today

Ivory Coast in October 2026 stands out as one of Africa's fastest-growing and most creditworthy economies. GDP grew 6.5% in 2025, with the IMF projecting around 6% in 2026 and about 6.7% a year over the medium term. Inflation was close to zero in 2025, the fiscal deficit was brought back to the WAEMU ceiling of 3% of GDP and public debt fell to 57.1% of GDP, its first decline in more than a decade. In June 2026 the IMF completed its $3.5 billion EFF/ECF and $1.3 billion RSF programmes and moved the country to low risk of debt distress, the only sub-Saharan African country with that status. Fitch upgraded the sovereign to BB in December 2025, and a $1.3 billion 15-year Eurobond in February 2026 drew $6.3 billion of orders. FDI reached $3.8 billion in 2024, its highest since 1990, and the 2026–2030 National Development Plan looks to the private sector for 70.2% of $205 billion in planned investment.

The economy's main structural challenge is its reliance on cocoa, where world price swings pass through to farm incomes and public revenues. After setting a record CFA 2,800 per kg, the government reset the farmgate price to CFA 1,200 in March 2026 and held it for the 2026/27 main crop as world prices retreated, while the Middle East conflict since February 2026 has raised fuel and fertiliser costs. Ivory Coast is mitigating this through the Coffee-Cocoa Council's forward-sales system, which gives early price visibility, and through rapid diversification. Baleine Phase 3 will more than double offshore oil output and supply gas to domestic power, Saipem has won contracts worth about $930 million on the project, and new gold mines such as Koné are coming on stream. Combined with record port volumes and expanding industrial zones, this broadening base creates opportunities across energy, mining, agro-processing and logistics.

How do I get in?

CEPICI (Centre de Promotion des Investissements en Côte d'Ivoire), the national investment agency and one-stop shop, which registered 26,948 new companies and approved CFA 812.67 billion of private investment in 2025

Investment Code (Ordinance No 2018-646, amended in 2019 and 2024) offering equal treatment of foreign and domestic investors, protection against expropriation, and declaration and approval regimes with incentives graded by zone (A, B, C)

Industrial zones (eleven main zones, including the new Akoupé-Zeudji PK24) and the VITIB technology free zone in Grand-Bassam

CFA franc (XOF) pegged to the euro and managed by the BCEAO; the Investment Code guarantees access to foreign exchange and profit repatriation, with FDI above 10% reported to the BCEAO and generally no prior authorisation required

Public-private partnership framework under Law No 2018-575, overseen by the National Steering Committee for PPPs (CNP-PPP)

Production-sharing contracts with Petroci for hydrocarbons and permits under the Mining Code for gold and other minerals

OHADA uniform business law, with arbitration through the CCJA or the CACI arbitration centre and commercial courts in Abidjan, Bouaké and San-Pédro

Trade and market access

Ivory Coast is the largest economy in the West African Economic and Monetary Union (WAEMU), whose eight members share the CFA franc, and a member of ECOWAS, giving tariff-preferential access across the wider West African market. It has been a WTO member since 1995, has ratified the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) and has an Economic Partnership Agreement with the EU, its main destination for cocoa. Abidjan is the primary sea gateway for landlocked Sahel economies: in 2025 transit through the port reached 2.4 million tonnes to Burkina Faso and 1.47 million tonnes to Mali, served by road and the Abidjan–Ouagadougou railway. Container traffic of almost 1.7 million TEU, a second deep-water port at San-Pédro and growing offshore energy output give the country a strong platform for both regional and global trade.

The overall risk picture

Ivory Coast's risk profile is that of a stable, fast-growing economy with improving public finances and strong market access. The peaceful 2025 presidential election, which Fitch said showed the country has moved beyond its history of election-related unrest, a new government focused on the 2026–2030 National Development Plan, the successful completion of IMF programmes and the move to low risk of debt distress all point to sustained policy continuity. The principal sensitivities are external: cocoa price swings, higher energy and fertiliser costs linked to the Middle East conflict, and security developments in the neighbouring Sahel. These are cushioned by the CFA franc's euro peg, regional reserves above eight months of imports, a fiscal path back to 3% of GDP by 2028 and a diversifying production base in oil, gas and gold. Investors in cocoa-linked businesses should plan for price cycles and EU traceability requirements, while those in northern regions should include standard security assessments.

Who should I talk to first?

Presidency of the Republic (Alassane Ouattara)

Re-elected in October 2025; sets national economic strategy, including the 2026–2030 National Development Plan

Primary reference point for Ivory Coast's strategic direction and flagship projects

CEPICI (Centre de Promotion des Investissements en Côte d'Ivoire)

National investment agency and one-stop shop for company formation and Investment Code approvals

Standard first point of contact for market entry and incentive guidance

Central Bank of West African States (BCEAO)

Regional central bank managing the euro-pegged CFA franc; held its policy rate at 3.00% in September 2026

Relevant for currency, banking-sector and capital-transfer matters

Ministry of Economy, Finance and Budget (Adama Coulibaly)

Leads fiscal policy, debt management and relations with the IMF and Eurobond investors

Key interlocutor on fiscal incentives, PPPs and public financing

Ministry of Mines, Petroleum and Energy and Petroci

Regulate and partner in oil, gas, mining and power, including Baleine with Eni and Vitol

Essential counterparts for any energy, mining or power-sector engagement

Coffee-Cocoa Council (Conseil du Café-Cacao)

Regulates the cocoa sector, sets the farmgate price and manages forward sales of the crop

Central counterpart for cocoa sourcing, processing and traceability investment

Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Crafts (Ibrahim Khalil Konaté)

Oversees industrial policy and the development of industrial zones such as Akoupé-Zeudji PK24

Relevant for manufacturing, agro-processing and industrial land access

Business networks

The Confédération Générale des Entreprises de Côte d'Ivoire (CGECI) is the leading private-sector employers' body and works with CEPICI on investor outreach, including briefings on the Investment Code. The Chamber of Commerce and Industry of Côte d'Ivoire provides business liaison and hosts the CACI arbitration centre. Abidjan is home to the African Development Bank's headquarters and the regional stock exchange (BRVM), and the World Bank Group and IMF maintain active country programmes. International energy and mining companies, including Eni, provide useful practical intelligence on the hydrocarbons and gold sectors, while the major cocoa traders and processors are a valuable channel for agribusiness insight.

Who this market suits

Ivory Coast suits investors seeking exposure to a large, stable and diversifying West African economy with a euro-anchored currency: oil, gas and power investors building on the Baleine expansion and domestic gas supply; mining companies and service providers in the growing gold sector; agribusiness and food-processing investors adding value to cocoa and other export crops; logistics and manufacturing investors using Abidjan's port and the new industrial zones to serve WAEMU and Sahel markets; and financial-services and fintech investors drawn by a deepening regional capital market. Cocoa-linked investors should plan for price cycles and EU traceability requirements.

The economy today

The US Virgin Islands in October 2026 combines record tourism with an unprecedented federally funded rebuilding programme. Gross territorial product has risen to about $5.2 billion from $3.9 billion in 2018, and public debt has been reduced by around 25% over seven years to $1.6 billion, about 30% of GDP. Visitor arrivals reached 2.6 million in 2025, and 2026 is setting new records: first-quarter arrivals rose 12% to 303,388, air arrivals grew 9.7% and cruise passengers 40.2% in January to June, and hotel occupancy reached 73.8% with revenue per available room of $415, well above the regional average. More than $24 billion in federal disaster recovery funding has been obligated, over 1,000 projects are complete and more than 30 major projects are due to start construction in 2026–2027, alongside hotel investment backed by EDC and Hotel Development Act incentives.

The territory's main structural challenge is the cost and reliability of essential infrastructure and public finances in a small island economy. Inflation reached 7.6% in 2025, led by housing costs, power has historically been expensive, and the government's cash position is tight, with a projected deficit of about $59 million in FY2026 and a government pension system that actuaries expect to face insolvency between 2033 and 2039. The response is substantial: new solar farms with battery storage are expected to save the power utility around $10–11 million a year, Congress made the $13.25 rum cover-over rate permanent from the end of 2025, and federal recovery funds are rebuilding schools, hospitals, roads and housing. Each of these creates openings for contractors, energy developers, housing and hospitality investors, and the potential restart of the St Croix refinery would add a further industrial dimension.

How do I get in?

US Virgin Islands Economic Development Authority (USVIEDA) is the principal investment agency, administering incentive programmes, Enterprise Zones and business lending

Economic Development Commission (EDC) programme: a 90% credit against income tax, exemption from the 5% gross receipts tax and from property tax on approved facilities, and excise exemptions on building materials and raw materials

Hotel Development Act incentives for new and refurbished hotels, used for projects such as the 126-room Hampton by Hilton on St Thomas

Mirror tax code: the US Internal Revenue Code applies with "USVI" substituted for "United States", with sourcing and residence rules under Internal Revenue Code Sections 934 and 937

US dollar currency, FDIC-insured banks, a federal district court within the Third Circuit and US bilateral investment treaty coverage; there are no currency or repatriation restrictions

SSBCI small-business finance: $57.8 million awarded to the territory, used for loan guarantees and direct lending through the Economic Development Bank

Public-private partnerships and federally funded recovery contracts in airports, ports, energy, schools and hospitals

Trade and market access

The US Virgin Islands is an unincorporated territory of the United States. Unlike Puerto Rico, it lies outside the US customs territory: US Customs and Border Protection administers customs on behalf of the US Treasury, but import tariffs are set by the territory's legislature, and goods wholly or largely produced in the islands can qualify for duty-free entry to the US mainland. This allows locally produced rum to reach the US market duty-free, with federal excise taxes returned to the territory through the cover-over programme. The US mainland is the main source of visitors and the principal market, supported by new and expanded routes from Newark and Tampa and new regional services to Puerto Rico and St Maarten. Goods and services exports were $2.58 billion in 2024. The territory is covered by US bilateral investment treaties but not by US tax treaties, and it is a short hop from Puerto Rico and the British Virgin Islands, giving it a natural role in the north-eastern Caribbean.

The overall risk picture

The US Virgin Islands offers a risk profile anchored by US sovereignty: the US dollar, US federal courts, FDIC-insured banking and a mirror of the US tax code give investors a familiar legal and financial framework. Tourism is at record levels, more than $24 billion in federal recovery funding is being spent through 2035, and EDC incentives can reduce the effective income tax rate on eligible income to around 2.3%. The main considerations are the territory's tight public finances and pension liabilities, high energy and living costs, and the EU's listing of the territory as a non-cooperative tax jurisdiction, which matters for EU-based investors. Each is being addressed through debt reduction, a permanent rum cover-over rate, new solar and storage capacity and continued federal support. A gubernatorial election on 3 November 2026 will bring a new administration as Governor Albert Bryan Jr. completes his final term, so investors should plan for a short transition period while incentive frameworks continue to operate under existing law.

Business networks

USVIEDA is the main gateway for investors, and its board and staff work directly with incentive applicants and hotel developers. The St Thomas–St John and St Croix chambers of commerce provide local business networks, while the Department of Tourism works with airlines and hotel groups on route and product development. The Virgin Islands Bureau of Economic Research publishes regular economic data and testimony that provide useful market intelligence. US federal channels, including SelectUSA and the US Treasury's State Small Business Credit Initiative, are available to investors. Hotel operators, cruise lines and the established rum distillers are a practical source of intelligence on operating conditions.

Who this market suits

The US Virgin Islands suits investors who value a US legal and currency framework in a Caribbean setting: hospitality and real-estate investors drawn by record arrivals, high occupancy and Hotel Development Act incentives; engineering and construction firms able to deliver federally funded recovery projects; renewable energy, storage and utility-services investors supporting the energy transition; and professional, technology and financial-services businesses that can benefit from EDC incentives with a genuine local presence. Energy and logistics investors may also follow the potential restart of the St Croix refinery and the airport and harbour upgrades.

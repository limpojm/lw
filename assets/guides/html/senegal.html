Investor Entry Routes

  • APIX (Agency for the Promotion of Investments and Major Works) is the one-stop entry point for investors, incentive approvals and the Invest in Senegal forum
  • New Investment Code adopted in September 2025, lowering the eligibility threshold from CFA 100 million to CFA 15 million and offering VAT suspension and customs exemptions during the build phase (three years in Dakar and Thiès, up to five elsewhere), with a digital single window targeting decisions within ten working days
  • Special economic zone regime covering seven zones, including Diamniadio, Sandiara, Diass and Bargny-Sendou, with serviced land on long-term leases
  • CFA franc (XOF) issued by the BCEAO and pegged to the euro, with foreign-exchange operations governed by common UEMOA regulations; the regional policy rate stands at 3.00%
  • Hydrocarbons entry through licences and partnerships with the national oil company Petrosen, including the gas-to-power agenda around Yakaar-Teranga
  • Large infrastructure and public-private projects in a pipeline of over $15 billion of foreign investment for 2026–2030, including the Grand Transfert d'Eau (about $8 billion) and Dakar–Bamako rail
  • OHADA uniform business law and regional arbitration, with the UEMOA regional bond market and BRVM exchange providing local-currency financing

Market-Entry Observation

Senegal enters October 2026 as West Africa's newest oil and gas producer, with growth momentum and a clear route back to market confidence. GDP grew 6.7% in 2025, the first full year of hydrocarbon output, with Sangomar producing 36.1 million barrels against an initial forecast of 30.5 million, and non-hydrocarbon growth recovering to 4.7% year-on-year in Q1 2026. Inflation was 1.4% in 2025, within the regional target, and the BCEAO held its policy rate at 3.00% in September 2026. FDI reached a record $3.3 billion in 2024 before falling sharply in 2025 as Sangomar and GTA moved from construction into production, lifting the FDI stock to about $25 billion. A new Investment Code (2025) with lower thresholds and a ten-day digital approval window, the Ndayane deep-water port and a pipeline of more than $15 billion in planned foreign investment for 2026–2030 underpin the next phase.

Senegal's central challenge is restoring debt sustainability after a 2025 audit found that borrowing between 2019 and early 2024 had been under-reported by more than $11 billion; the IMF's revised figures put public debt at about 132% of GDP, and Moody's (Caa2) and S&P (CC) have downgraded the sovereign. The authorities have responded decisively: the deficit was cut from 13.4% of GDP in 2024 to 6.4% in 2025, and on 1 September 2026 IMF staff agreed a new three-year Extended Credit Facility of about $2.2 billion, subject to corrective action on misreporting and creditor financing assurances. The government has launched a Debt Treatment Plan under an "enhanced" G20 Common Framework covering external debt, including bilateral, commercial and Eurobond debt, while excluding CFA franc regional debt, and targets IMF Board approval in November 2026 and creditor agreements in principle in December. A successful treatment would return Senegal to a sustainable footing, with hydrocarbon revenues and World Bank backing behind it.

Investor Risk Note

Senegal's risk profile is shaped by a transparent reckoning with past debt misreporting, set against strong underlying assets: new oil and gas output that beat forecasts, a stable euro-pegged currency within UEMOA, low inflation and a long democratic tradition. The material fact for investors is that Senegal is restructuring its external debt, including Eurobonds and commercial loans, under an enhanced G20 Common Framework, and ratings agencies treat a distressed exchange as likely; CFA franc regional debt is excluded, and coupons continued to be paid in September 2026. Holders of sovereign or sovereign-linked external claims should take specialist advice and expect changed terms. For project and corporate investors, the IMF staff-level agreement of about $2.2 billion, the targeted November 2026 Board approval and World Bank support for a faster process provide a clear route to stability. Practical mitigation includes DFI co-financing, political-risk cover and payment-security structures, and the post-treatment period should offer a cleaner, better-monitored fiscal framework in which to invest.

Market Access

Senegal is a member of the West African Economic and Monetary Union (UEMOA), sharing the CFA franc and BCEAO with seven other states, of the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS), and of the African Continental Free Trade Area, and has been a WTO member since 1995. Dakar is the natural Atlantic port for landlocked Mali, its leading regional market, and the Ndayane port will add deep-water capacity from 2028. Exports are led by gold, crude oil, refined petroleum products and phosphoric acid; in December 2025 the main destinations were Switzerland (19%), Belgium (16%), Mali (10%), Spain (9%) and the United Kingdom (7%), with LNG from GTA now adding a gas export stream. This mix of regional monetary integration, Atlantic access and European and Swiss market links gives investors a gateway to a wider West African market.

Current International Business Channels

APIX's Invest in Senegal forum is the principal investment showcase; the 2025 edition drew 11,772 participants from 70 countries and announced $23.5 billion of investment intentions. The Dakar Chamber of Commerce, Industry and Agriculture and the national employers' bodies provide domestic business liaison. The World Bank Group, including IFC and MIGA, the African Development Bank and the IMF are closely engaged, and World Bank President Ajay Banga has pledged to help Senegal move through the Common Framework faster than earlier cases. International operators such as Woodside at Sangomar, Kosmos on GTA and DP World at Ndayane are a valuable source of practical insight on operating conditions.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Senegal suits investors with a medium- to long-term horizon who can build on its democratic stability and new energy base: energy and gas-to-power investors drawn by Yakaar-Teranga and domestic gas supply; logistics, port and industrial investors positioned around Ndayane, the special economic zones and the Mali corridor; infrastructure and water investors working alongside DFIs; and agro-processing and manufacturing firms using the lower Investment Code thresholds. Investors holding or considering sovereign external debt should factor in the ongoing debt treatment, while project and corporate investors are best served by structures with DFI participation, political-risk cover and secure payment mechanisms.

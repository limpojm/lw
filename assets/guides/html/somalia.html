Investor Entry Routes

  • SOMINVEST (Somalia Investment Promotion Office), within the Ministry of Planning, Investment and Economic Development, provides investor facilitation, registration support and sector guidance
  • The Investment Law passed by Parliament in November 2025, which establishes a National Investment Council, one-stop-shop procedures and priority sectors, with implementing regulations being drafted in 2026
  • Petroleum sector entry through the Ministry of Petroleum and Mineral Resources, following Türkiye's first offshore deepwater drilling campaign
  • Central Bank of Somalia supervision of banks and payment providers; the US dollar is widely used, and currency reform to restore the Somali shilling is a national priority being prepared with IMF advice
  • Regional market access through East African Community membership and ratification of the African Continental Free Trade Area, coordinated by the Ministry of Commerce and Industry
  • Planned designated industrial zones and a 'Made in Somalia' export brand under the Ministry of Commerce and Industry's AfCFTA implementation agenda

Market-Entry Observation

Somalia in October 2026 is an early-stage frontier market with a decade of recovery behind it and a strengthening institutional base. The World Bank estimates growth of about 3% in 2025, with inflation at 3.7%, and the IMF's October 2026 Article IV mission projects 2.3% growth in 2026, 2.7% in 2027 and around 4% over the medium term, with a contained fiscal deficit of 0.9% of GDP. FDI inflows reached an estimated $834 million in 2025 according to UNCTAD data. Reaching the HIPC completion point in December 2023 delivered $4.5 billion of debt relief and cut external debt from 64% of GDP in 2018 to under 6%, normalising relations with international lenders. A new Investment Law, East African Community membership, AfCFTA ratification, Türkiye's first offshore deepwater well and plans to restore a national currency mark a clear direction of travel toward a more formal, investable economy.

Somalia's main structural challenge is security, together with a political settlement still being completed. Al-Shabaab still holds large rural areas and can strike population centres, and the national army, backed by an African Union mission, continues operations against it, while the electoral commission has set parliamentary elections for April 2027 and a presidential election for 15 May 2027 after a contested one-year term extension. The private sector has shown considerable adaptability in this setting: Somali firms built widely used mobile-money and money-transfer networks, livestock exports serve large Gulf markets, and resilient remittances remain an important source of household income and foreign exchange. For investors, structured security planning, careful counterparty screening and partnership with established Somali firms or development-finance institutions provide a practical route to participate in reconstruction, infrastructure, energy and trade opportunities as stability consolidates.

Investor Risk Note

Somalia's risk profile is that of a recovering frontier economy with improving institutions and genuine first-mover opportunities. HIPC debt relief, an IMF-supported reform programme, a new Investment Law and membership of the EAC and AfCFTA are concrete reforms that have reconnected Somalia with international finance and regional markets. Legal realities should be stated plainly: al-Shabaab is subject to a UN sanctions regime renewed in December 2025 under Resolution 2806, and to national counter-terrorism designations, so any investment requires rigorous sanctions and anti-money-laundering screening of partners, agents, logistics providers and payment channels, with specialist legal counsel. The UN arms embargo exempts deliveries to the Federal Government and its security forces, while restrictions aimed at al-Shabaab remain. Somaliland's disputed status also requires careful legal structuring for any project there. Security risk is real but geographically uneven, and investors who concentrate initially on Mogadishu and the main ports, use professional security support and partner with established Somali firms or development-finance institutions are well positioned to benefit as stability and the political settlement consolidate.

Market Access

Somalia signed the East African Community accession treaty in December 2023, becoming the bloc's eighth member, and in 2026 became the 50th country to ratify the African Continental Free Trade Area, with plans for industrial zones and a 'Made in Somalia' export brand. Somalia is also a member of the Arab League and is pursuing WTO accession. Its exports are led by livestock, shipped mainly to Gulf markets through the northern ports, alongside sesame, fish and other agricultural produce. With more than 3,000 km of coastline facing the Gulf of Aden and the Indian Ocean, close to the Bab el-Mandeb shipping lane, Somalia is well placed to develop port, transit and logistics services serving the Horn of Africa, including landlocked Ethiopia, following the Ankara Declaration that restored Somalia–Ethiopia relations.

Current International Business Channels

The Somali Chamber of Commerce and Industry is the main domestic business association, and Somalia's leading private groups in telecoms, banking, money transfer and trade are the most valuable sources of practical market intelligence. Business forums in Mogadishu with EU, Chinese and Italian partners provide structured networking with officials and companies. Türkiye, the UAE, Qatar and other Gulf partners are active in ports, infrastructure and energy. The World Bank, the African Development Bank and the IFC run programmes in infrastructure, financial inclusion and private-sector development, and the AfCFTA Secretariat has begun implementation cooperation with Mogadishu.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Somalia best suits investors with frontier-market experience, a strong compliance function and a long-term horizon: telecoms, fintech and payments investors building on very high mobile-money usage; logistics and port investors positioned for Horn of Africa trade; agribusiness, livestock and fisheries investors serving Gulf and East African demand; and energy investors able to engage in frontier offshore exploration alongside established partners. Diaspora investors and development-finance co-investors are especially well placed, while construction, renewable power and water projects linked to DFI programmes offer lower-risk entry points.

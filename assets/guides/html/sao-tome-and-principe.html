Investor Entry Routes

  • Trade and Investment Promotion Agency (APCI), housed in the Ministry of Planning, Finance and Blue Economy, provides facilitation and co-hosts investor forums with partners such as Afreximbank
  • Investment Code and Code of Fiscal Benefits and Incentives (both 2016), with implementing regulations adopted in 2017, set out investor guarantees and tax incentives
  • Public-Private Partnership Law (2018) provides the framework for concessions in infrastructure such as the airport, port and power generation
  • National Petroleum Agency (ANP-STP) licensing rounds and production-sharing contracts, with the 2026 round for Blocks 7, 8 and 9 reportedly allowing operator stakes of up to 85%
  • The dobra has been pegged to the euro since 2010 at about 24.5 dobras per euro, giving euro-based investors stable currency exposure; the Central Bank of São Tomé and Príncipe manages the peg
  • Co-investment alongside development finance institutions active in the country, including the African Development Bank, World Bank, European Investment Bank, BADEA and Afreximbank
  • National Sustainable Development Strategy (ENDS) 2026–2040, presented to investors in December 2025, sets out priority projects in ports, airports, water, energy and tourism

Market-Entry Observation

São Tomé and Príncipe in October 2026 is a small, stable island democracy that has made measurable progress on macroeconomic stabilisation. Under a 52-month IMF Extended Credit Facility, with most third-review targets met, public debt fell from 65.2% of GDP in 2024 to an estimated 54% in 2025, gross reserves rose from $38.9 million to about $60 million (3.7 months of imports), and the country recorded a zero primary fiscal balance in 2024, the first in several decades. Inflation has eased to single digits, at 9.7% year-on-year in São Tomé city in June 2026, from 11.6% at end-2024. Growth remains modest, about 1% in 2025 with the IMF projecting 0.4% in 2026 and around 2.75% over the medium term, but the drivers are clear: tourism reached a record 42,000 visitors in 2025 and is estimated at 11% of GDP, the country graduated from Least Developed Country status in December 2024, and UNCTAD lists it among the leading small-island recipients of infrastructure-related investment projects.

The main structural challenge is energy. Around 95% of the country's 106.3 GWh of annual electricity comes from diesel plants running on imported fuel, at a cost of about $0.34/kWh, and less than half of the 38 MW installed capacity is reliably available. Utility arrears to the fuel importer ENCO, estimated at 18% of GDP in 2025, also weigh on public finances. Mitigation is under way: the utility EMAE has added about 6 MW of new generators, the African Development Bank approved the $30 million ETREEP programme in April 2026 including a 4 MWp solar plant with battery storage, the Papagaio hydropower plant is being rehabilitated, and the IMF programme places energy-sector reform at its centre. With a target of universal electricity access by 2030, some of the highest power costs in sub-Saharan Africa and strong donor backing, the energy transition is the clearest near-term opportunity for investors in solar, hydropower, storage and efficiency.

Investor Risk Note

São Tomé and Príncipe's risk profile is that of a small, peaceful island democracy with a credible reform anchor. The IMF-supported programme has delivered falling public debt, rebuilt reserves and single-digit inflation, and the euro peg provides a stable currency framework for European and international investors. The key operating constraint is the cost and reliability of electricity, which is being tackled through donor-financed solar, hydropower and utility reforms that themselves create investment opportunities. Following peaceful presidential and legislative elections in 2026, a new government is being formed from a closely divided Assembly; investors should allow for a short transition period and confirm the incoming economic team's priorities. Offshore oil remains a long-term option rather than a near-term revenue source, so investment cases are best built on tourism, energy, agribusiness, fisheries and infrastructure. Large concessions should be structured with robust contracts, arbitration clauses and, where possible, development finance partners.

Market Access

São Tomé and Príncipe is a member of the Economic Community of Central African States (ECCAS) and the Community of Portuguese Language Countries (CPLP), and ratified the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) in June 2019, giving access to a continental market. The country is not yet a WTO member: it holds observer status, with an accession working party established in 2005. Having graduated from Least Developed Country status in December 2024, it is moving through a transition in which some LDC-specific trade preferences and concessional terms will phase out. Exports are concentrated in cocoa, with primary commodities making up 69% of merchandise exports in 2021–2023, and palm oil increasingly important. Portugal is the leading source of tourists, followed by France, Angola, Germany and the United Kingdom, and the country's position in the Gulf of Guinea links it to the Nigerian, Gabonese and Equatorial Guinean energy and trade economies.

Current International Business Channels

The Trade and Investment Promotion Agency (APCI) co-hosts investment forums with partners, including the Afreximbank-backed forum in São Tomé and a December 2025 investor forum in Brussels that drew more than 200 participants and interest from BADEA, the World Bank and the European Investment Bank in financing the port, airport and water systems. The African Development Bank, World Bank and IMF maintain active country programmes, and Afreximbank has extended a $12 million credit facility through the central bank. Portugal's trade and investment agency AICEP is a useful source of market intelligence given Portugal's long-standing commercial ties, and international oil companies with offshore interests, including Shell, Galp and Petrobras, provide practical insight into the energy sector. The US Embassy in Libreville covers the country for US investors.

Investor Profile Best Suited

São Tomé and Príncipe suits investors comfortable with a small but stable market and a long-term horizon: renewable-energy developers and independent power producers, given diesel-dependent generation, very high power costs and strong donor co-financing; eco-tourism and boutique hospitality investors building on record visitor numbers; premium cocoa, agribusiness and fisheries investors; and infrastructure and PPP investors working alongside development finance institutions on the port, airport and utilities. Oil and gas companies seeking frontier Gulf of Guinea acreage at an early stage will find an active licensing agency and established majors already present.

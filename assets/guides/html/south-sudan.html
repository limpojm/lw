Investor Entry Routes

  • Investment Promotion Act 2009, which established the national Investment Authority, issues investment certificates carrying tax incentives, and guarantees against expropriation without fair compensation
  • Statutory right to transfer investment capital, loan servicing and sale proceeds in and out of the country, with investor–state disputes open to international arbitration, including ICSID, at the investor's option
  • Ministry of Petroleum licensing: 14 open exploration blocks, five of them offered in the country's first licensing round, plus refinery, storage and distribution projects being advanced through MoUs
  • Currency regime: the South Sudanese pound trades at official and parallel rates, and unifying them is a core goal of the IMF Staff-Monitored Program agreed in 2025; investors should confirm dollar availability and repatriation mechanics with their banks
  • East African Community membership (since 2016), giving access to regional trade arrangements and the Mombasa and Dar es Salaam port corridors
  • Co-investment alongside the World Bank, AfDB and IMF-supported reform programmes, which anchor fiscal transparency, oil-sector governance and payment of salary arrears
  • Sanctions-compliant structuring: all counterparties and payment routes screened against UN, US and UK South Sudan and Sudan sanctions lists before commitment

Market-Entry Observation

South Sudan in October 2026 is in an oil-led recovery. After the 2024 rupture of the export pipeline through Sudan cut production from about 160,000 to around 60,000 barrels per day, flows resumed in April 2025 and national output has since climbed to around 170,000–174,000 barrels per day, with the Ministry of Petroleum targeting 230,000 barrels per day in the short term and 450,000 over the longer term. The World Bank projects a 48.8% rebound in 2026 after a 23.8% contraction in FY2025, while the IMF's current projection is a more measured 4.1%, with inflation forecast to ease to 14% from around 143% in FY2024/25. Public debt is about 58% of GDP. A nine-month IMF Staff-Monitored Program agreed in 2025 targets exchange-rate unification, tight monetary policy and oil-sector transparency, and the country's first oil licensing round, five of 14 open blocks, and plans for 60,000 barrels per day of new refining capacity mark a clear opening of the energy sector to new investors.

The main structural challenge is security, at home and along the export route. Domestically, fighting between government forces and the SPLA-IO since 2025 has left the 2018 peace agreement largely unimplemented; opposition leader Riek Machar remains under house arrest facing treason charges, clashes have been concentrated in Jonglei and Upper Nile, and the UN Security Council renewed its arms embargo, asset freeze and travel ban on 29 May 2026 until 31 May 2027. Elections are scheduled for December 2026, though preparations remain behind schedule. Externally, all crude exports run through Sudan, where the RSF has controlled the Heglig area since December 2025 and now collects part of the transit fee. Mitigation lies in careful site selection, professional security and sanctions advice, and partnership with established operators and development-finance institutions. The opportunity is substantial: a resource base nearly 90% untapped, a government actively seeking upstream, refining and power partners, and investors positioned now will be best placed as security and governance improve.

Investor Risk Note

South Sudan offers frontier exposure to one of Africa's least-explored oil provinces, with output back above 170,000 barrels per day, a first licensing round under way and growth forecast to rebound strongly in 2026. Investors must, however, factor in legal realities plainly: the UN arms embargo, asset freeze and travel ban were renewed on 29 May 2026 until 31 May 2027; the US and UK operate their own South Sudan sanctions regimes; and the crude export route crosses RSF-held territory in Sudan, where transit-fee payments raise distinct sanctions questions. Domestically, the 2018 peace agreement has stalled amid renewed fighting, and December 2026 elections remain uncertain. None of these measures bars civilian commercial investment, but each requires sanctions counsel, rigorous counterparty screening, professional security planning and close engagement with development-finance partners. Investors who build that compliance and security discipline in from the outset can participate lawfully today and will be well positioned for the gains that would follow progress on the UN benchmarks and a credible political transition.

Market Access

South Sudan joined the East African Community in 2016, linking it to the regional market and to the ports of Mombasa and Dar es Salaam, with the Nimule–Juba axis forming the backbone of trade with Uganda and Kenya and Sudan also among its main sources of consumer goods. The country is pursuing WTO accession and has endorsed a national AfCFTA implementation strategy with the UN Economic Commission for Africa. Crude oil, which accounts for the large majority of exports, travels via two pipeline systems through Sudan to Port Sudan on the Red Sea, carrying a combined 160,000 barrels per day or more in 2025. Planned domestic refining capacity and deeper East African integration offer complementary routes to market as the economy diversifies beyond oil.

Current International Business Channels

South Sudan Oil & Power 2026, the seventh edition, held in Juba on 24–25 November in official partnership with the Ministry of Petroleum, is the principal investor forum and the main platform for the licensing round, refinery projects and power opportunities. The IMF resident representative office, the World Bank and the African Development Bank are active in Juba and provide authoritative economic and reform intelligence, while operators in the GPOC-led Unity State fields are a practical source of insight into on-the-ground logistics. Regional business links run through the East African Community and the Kenyan and Ugandan trading networks along the Nimule–Juba corridor, and UNMISS and humanitarian agencies are valuable sources of security and access information.

Investor Profile Best Suited

South Sudan best suits experienced frontier and specialist investors with strong compliance and security capability: independent oil companies and oilfield-service firms targeting the licensing round and field redevelopment; refining, storage and fuel-distribution investors responding to planned new capacity; logistics and trading firms operating on the Nimule–Juba corridor; and agriculture, power and telecoms investors co-investing alongside development-finance institutions. All investors should operate with sanctions counsel and a clear framework for engagement within lawfully permitted civilian sectors.

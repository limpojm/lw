The economy today

Malawi in October 2026 is a reform-minded frontier market with a young population, abundant land, water and minerals, and a government focused on restoring macroeconomic stability. Inflation has fallen steadily from 28.7% in September 2025 to 19.4% in September 2026, allowing the Reserve Bank to cut its policy rate by 200 basis points to 24% in March 2026, its first move since February 2024. The IMF projects growth of 2.6% in 2026 on an economy of about $18.3 billion, while the government's budget targets 3.8%. An IMF mission that ended on 6 October 2026 reported considerable progress towards a new Extended Credit Facility, citing strong reforms to tighten fiscal discipline. FDI inflows reached $220 million in 2024, and the government's agriculture, tourism, mining and manufacturing strategy, the 2024 SEZ Act and new critical-minerals projects such as Kasiya rutile and Kangankunde rare earths give investors clear entry points.

Malawi's main structural challenge is foreign-exchange availability. Exports of $936 million in 2025, led by tobacco, covered about a quarter of imports of $3.6 billion, reserves stood at 2.4 months of import cover in July 2026, and public debt is high, with external commercial-creditor restructuring still being negotiated. The policy response is under way: a deficit target cut from 11.9% to 9.0% of GDP in the 2026/27 budget, progress with bilateral creditors including China and India, a shorter 90-day export-proceeds repatriation rule, and gold purchases to build reserves. A new IMF programme would unlock wider donor and DFI support. For investors, this creates opportunity in exactly the areas the economy needs most: export-oriented agro-processing, mineral beneficiation, and power, where the 218 km Mozambique–Malawi interconnector and plans to lift capacity from under 600 MW to over 1,000 MW by 2030 open space for independent producers.

How do I get in?

Malawi Investment and Trade Centre (MITC), the national investment and export promotion agency, which guides investors through registration, permits and incentives and organises investor forums

Investment framework under the Investment and Export Promotion Act, giving investors the right to establish, acquire and dispose of business interests, with priority given to agriculture, tourism, mining and manufacturing

Special Economic Zones Act (in force March 2024), designating MITC as the SEZ Authority and offering a more liberal regime for qualifying zone investors, with implementing regulations being finalised

Reserve Bank of Malawi oversight of the managed kwacha (about K1,734 per dollar in October 2026); export proceeds must be repatriated within 90 days, and investors should plan for foreign-exchange availability when remitting profits

Mining entry through the Ministry responsible for mining and the state Malawi Mining Company (MAMICO), with the current policy favouring in-country processing and value addition before export

Public-private partnership opportunities in energy, aviation and road networks, alongside regional power trade once Malawi joins the Southern African Power Pool

Trade and market access

Malawi is a member of the Southern African Development Community (SADC), the Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa (COMESA) and the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), giving preferential access to a large regional market that includes neighbours Tanzania, Zambia and Mozambique. It is a WTO member and, as a least developed country, qualifies for preferential access to major markets including the European Union and the United Kingdom. Tobacco, tea and sugar are the leading exports. Trade moves mainly through neighbouring ports, including Nacala in Mozambique by rail, and ties with Tanzania are deepening, with MITC holding the third Malawi–Tanzania Investment and Trade Forum in Dar es Salaam in September 2026. Regional connectivity is improving: the Nacala rail corridor is being planned for extension to Zambia and the DRC, and the interconnector with Mozambique will link Malawi to the Southern African Power Pool.

The overall risk picture

Malawi's risk profile is that of a stable, peaceful democracy working through a period of macroeconomic adjustment. Political risk is low: power has changed hands peacefully through elections, most recently with President Peter Mutharika taking office in October 2025, and ethnic and religious tensions are minimal. The main risks are economic: limited foreign exchange, high public debt with commercial restructuring still in progress, and dependence on tobacco and rain-fed farming. The direction of travel is positive. Inflation has fallen by more than nine percentage points in a year, the central bank has begun easing, the 2026/27 budget cuts the deficit, and the IMF reports considerable progress towards a new programme. Investors should plan for currency and remittance timing, favour projects that earn or save foreign exchange, and use DFI partners and political-risk insurance for larger commitments. Those entering now are positioned ahead of the recovery that a completed IMF programme and debt restructuring would support.

Who should I talk to first?

Office of the President (Peter Mutharika)

Sets economic policy, including the mineral beneficiation drive, austerity measures and the planned mineral Sovereign Wealth Fund

Primary reference point for strategic direction and flagship investments

Malawi Investment and Trade Centre (MITC)

National investment and export promotion agency and statutory Special Economic Zones Authority

First point of contact for market entry, incentives and SEZ sites

Reserve Bank of Malawi (Governor Dr McDonald Mwale)

Sets monetary policy and manages foreign-exchange regulations, reserves and the kwacha

Essential for capital registration, remittances and currency planning

Ministry of Finance, Economic Planning and Decentralisation

Leads the budget, fiscal consolidation, IMF negotiations and debt restructuring

Key for understanding the reform programme and public-investment pipeline

Ministry of Industrialization, Business, Trade and Tourism

Leads trade, industrial and tourism policy, including SEZ regulations and regional trade promotion

Relevant for manufacturing, export and tourism projects

Ministry responsible for Mining and Malawi Mining Company (MAMICO)

Regulate and participate in the minerals sector, including licensing, the mining-law review and beneficiation policy

Essential counterparts for any mining or processing investment

Export Development Fund (EDF)

State-backed export finance institution supporting exporters and analysing investment trends

Possible co-financing and trade-finance partner for export projects

Business networks

The Malawi Confederation of Chambers of Commerce and Industry (MCCCI) is the main private-sector voice and a useful partner for introductions, alongside MITC, which runs investment forums such as the China–Malawi series and the Malawi–Tanzania Investment and Trade Forum held in Dar es Salaam in September 2026. The World Bank is a leading development partner, supporting infrastructure such as the Mozambique–Malawi interconnector and publishing the regular Malawi Economic Monitor, while other DFIs are active in agriculture, energy and transport. Established mining developers, including those at Kasiya and Kangankunde, and the commercial banks in Blantyre and Lilongwe are practical sources of on-the-ground intelligence on licensing, foreign exchange and logistics.

Who this market suits

Malawi best suits investors with a long-term horizon and a tolerance for frontier-market conditions: agribusiness and agro-processing investors building export value chains beyond tobacco; critical-minerals developers prepared to process in country, in line with government policy; renewable-energy and independent power producers responding to the drive to exceed 1,000 MW by 2030; and tourism investors drawn to Lake Malawi and the national parks. Projects that earn or save foreign exchange, and those backed by DFIs or impact capital, are best placed to manage currency conditions and to benefit as stabilisation takes hold.

The economy today

Libya in October 2026 is Africa's largest holder of proven oil reserves, at about 48.4 billion barrels, and is rebuilding its energy sector at pace. Crude and condensate output reached 1.48 million barrels a day in June 2026, the highest since 2013, and the NOC is targeting 2 million barrels a day by 2030. Oil revenue rose by more than 15% to $22 billion in 2025, and the IMF projected double-digit growth of 16.1% for 2025 as production recovered from a 2024 slowdown. International investor interest has returned: the NOC's 2025 licensing round, the first in nearly two decades, brought Chevron back to Libya and led to production-sharing agreements with Eni, QatarEnergy, Repsol, TPAO and MOL in 2026. In April 2026 the rival legislatures agreed Libya's first unified national budget since 2013, and gross official reserves of $82.9 billion at end-2024, equal to about 29.6 months of imports, provide a substantial buffer.

Libya's main structural challenge is institutional: the country has had rival administrations in the east and west since 2014, the UN arms embargo and asset freeze remain in force, and the dinar has been adjusted twice since April 2025 as divided spending pressured the currency. The direction of travel is encouraging. National institutions such as the NOC and the Central Bank of Libya operate across the country, the 2026 unified budget is the clearest sign of fiscal co-operation in over a decade, and the UN Security Council has allowed the Libyan Investment Authority limited, supervised reinvestment of frozen funds. For investors, the practical approach is to work through these national institutions, take sanctions and contracting-authority advice at the outset, and position for an energy-led recovery that is already under way.

How do I get in?

Sanctions compliance is the starting point: the UN arms embargo and asset freeze under Security Council resolution 1970 (2011), renewed and refined by resolution 2819 in April 2026, apply to all investors, alongside UK, EU and US listings

National Oil Corporation (NOC) licensing: the 2025 bid round, Libya's first in nearly two decades, offered 22 onshore and offshore blocks and led to production-sharing agreements in 2026, with bidding for more than 40 marginal fields in preparation

Privatisation and Investment Board, which administers Law No. 9 of 2010 on Promoting Investment, as the formal registration and incentive route for non-oil foreign investment

Misrata Free Zone, Libya's principal free zone and port, now the subject of a $2.7 billion public-private partnership to develop a Mediterranean transhipment hub

Central Bank of Libya currency regime: the dinar is set officially at 6.37 per dollar after a 14.7% adjustment in January 2026, with the IMF recommending further steps to unify official and parallel rates

Commercial Arbitration Law No. 10 of 2023, providing a modern statutory framework for resolving commercial disputes by arbitration

Public and reconstruction contracting with national institutions in Tripoli and with reconstruction programmes in the east, subject to confirming the contracting authority and its legal mandate

Trade and market access

Libya's Mediterranean coastline places it close to southern European markets, including Italy, which receives Libyan gas by pipeline, and its economy is overwhelmingly export-oriented, with hydrocarbons providing about 95% of export earnings through terminals across the oil crescent and the western coast. The country is not yet a WTO member, although membership talks have been opened, so trade terms are set largely through bilateral relationships with its main energy buyers. Expansion of the Misrata Free Zone port as a transhipment hub, rising oil and gas output, new offshore exploration and planned bidding for more than 40 marginal fields are strengthening Libya's role as an energy supplier across the Mediterranean and as a logistics gateway linking Europe with North Africa and the Sahel.

The overall risk picture

Libya is a legally restricted market, and investors should treat sanctions compliance as the starting point of any engagement. The UN arms embargo and asset freeze imposed under resolution 1970 in 2011 remain in force, were renewed with refinements in April 2026, and keep the Libyan Investment Authority's assets frozen; UN measures against illicit petroleum exports run to August 2027. Libya also has rival administrations in the east and west, so the authority of each counterparty must be confirmed. Within these boundaries, lawful commercial activity is substantial and growing: oil output is at a 13-year high, the NOC has signed its first production-sharing agreements in nearly two decades with major international companies, and the April 2026 unified budget shows that the rival institutions can co-operate on core economic matters. Investors who take specialist legal and sanctions advice, work through national institutions and maintain contingency plans for local disruptions can participate in the recovery now and are well placed for further normalisation.

Who should I talk to first?

Office of the Prime Minister, Government of National Unity (Abdul Hamid Dbeibah)

Internationally recognised government in Tripoli; oversees national policy, public contracting and the energy-sector licensing launch

Primary reference point for national economic direction and government contracting

Privatisation and Investment Board

Administers Law No. 9 of 2010 on Promoting Investment and registers foreign investment projects

Standard first contact for non-oil market entry and incentive guidance

Central Bank of Libya (leadership contested since October 2026)

Monetary authority managing the dinar and foreign reserves; brokered the 2026 unified budget; rival bodies disagree over a successor to Governor Naji Issa

Key counterpart on currency, payments and banking-sector matters

National Oil Corporation (Chairman Masoud Suleiman)

State oil company operating nationwide; runs licensing rounds and production-sharing agreements

Essential partner for any energy-sector engagement

House of Representatives (Benghazi) and High Council of State (Tripoli)

Rival legislative bodies whose agreement is needed for budgets and major laws; they jointly approved the April 2026 budget

Relevant for understanding legislative and political direction

Eastern authorities and Khalifa Haftar's forces

Control much of the east and south, including major export terminals and fields, and oversee eastern reconstruction

Relevant for operational planning in the east, subject to sanctions screening and legal advice

Libyan Investment Authority

Sovereign wealth fund whose assets are frozen under UN sanctions, with limited reinvestment approved by the 1970 Committee

Relevant to any co-investment discussion; all engagement requires sanctions counsel

Business networks

International energy companies already active in Libya, including Eni, Repsol, TotalEnergies, OMV, Equinor and, since 2026, Chevron, are the most valuable source of practical intelligence on operating conditions and partner selection. The NOC's licensing programme and the Misrata Free Zone Authority provide structured routes to engage on energy and logistics opportunities. The UN Support Mission in Libya and the Security Council's 1970 Sanctions Committee are the reference points for the political process and sanctions framework, while the IMF and the World Bank maintain active engagement on fiscal, currency and private-sector reform. National embassies and trade promotion bodies in Tripoli can advise on bilateral support and current security arrangements.

Who this market suits

Libya best suits experienced investors with a high tolerance for complexity and strong compliance capabilities: international oil and gas companies and oilfield-service providers participating in licensing rounds, marginal-field bidding and production growth; port, logistics and infrastructure investors drawn by the Misrata Free Zone expansion; and construction and building-materials firms positioned for reconstruction in both the west and the east. All investors should engage specialist legal and sanctions counsel, contract through clearly mandated national institutions, and treat Libya as a market where well-prepared early movers can build positions ahead of further normalisation.

The economy today

The Gambia in October 2026 offers a decade of steady growth, falling inflation and an active reform agenda. The economy grew by around 6% in 2025 (5.7–6.0% according to the central bank and the IMF), extending an average of 5% a year since the 2017 democratic transition, and growth of 4.7–5.8% is projected for 2026. Inflation fell from 10.2% in December 2024 to 6.4% in January 2026, allowing the central bank to cut its policy rate from 16% to 14%; it rose to 7.0% in July 2026 as global oil prices rose, before easing to 6.1% in September. Gross reserves reached $563.9 million at end-July 2026, equal to 4.3 months of imports, and remittances rose 12.4% to a record $872 million in 2025. FDI inflows were $232 million in 2024, with an inward stock of about $1.9 billion. The banking sector is well capitalised (capital adequacy of 25.5%), and the IMF completed its fifth ECF review in July 2026, noting that the economy "continues to grow strongly".

The Gambia's main structural challenge is public debt, estimated at 72–76% of GDP in 2025, which the IMF and World Bank assess as carrying a high risk of debt distress, alongside a narrow tax base averaging 10.3% of GDP. The direction of travel is positive: the debt ratio fell from 73.6% in 2024 to 71.8% at end-2025 according to the Ministry of Finance, and is projected at 68.8% in 2026. The overall fiscal deficit narrowed to 1.7% of GDP in the first half of 2026 from 3.1% a year earlier, state-owned enterprises returned a combined net profit of D2.5 billion in 2025, and the World Bank estimates that closing tax gaps could raise 3–4% of GDP in revenue. With IMF, World Bank and EU support anchoring reform, the need to finance infrastructure through private capital rather than public borrowing is creating concrete opportunities for investors in energy, ports and tourism.

Where are the opportunities?

Greater Banjul Area (Banjul, Kanifing)

Capital and main commercial centre; seat of government, the central bank and the banking sector, with leasehold land titles more established than elsewhere

Financial services, trade, real estate, professional services

Port of Banjul and Half-Die container terminal

The country's only seaport and a regional gateway for re-exports to Mali, Guinea-Bissau and southern Senegal; rehabilitation under a 30-year concession is complete and a new Half-Die container terminal is in use

Port logistics, warehousing, re-export trade

Sanyang (West Coast Region)

Site of a planned deep-sea port under the same concession, with site studies now under way following assignment of the land lease in March 2026

Port development, construction, marine services

Atlantic coast tourism strip (Kololi, Kotu, Bijilo)

Heart of a tourism sector worth an estimated 18–20% of GDP, with arrivals rising to 233,113 and a further 15% increase forecast for the 2026/27 season

Hotels, hospitality, aviation services, leisure real estate

Brikama and Jambur (West Coast Region)

Location of the 23 MWp Jambur solar plant with 8 MWh of battery storage and an OMVG regional grid substation

Renewable energy, agro-processing, light manufacturing

Soma and Farafenni (Trans-Gambia corridor)

Central river crossing on the corridor between northern and southern Senegal, with a 225 kV OMVG substation importing power from the regional grid

Transit logistics, energy, cross-border trade

Kaur and Basse (River Gambia, east)

Agricultural heartland and planned inland logistics platforms under the port concession, connecting river trade with the eastern regions

Groundnuts, cashew, horticulture, river logistics

How do I get in?

Gambia Investment and Export Promotion Agency (GIEPA), the national one-stop agency for foreign investors, issuing Special Investment Certificates (SICs) for qualifying projects under the GIEPA Act 2015

GIEPA Amendment Bill 2025, backed by the National Assembly at Second Reading in March 2026, which modernises the investment framework, aligns it with the AfCFTA investment protocol and makes incentive administration clearer and more transparent

Special Export Promotion Zone licences from GIEPA for export-oriented operations, alongside SICs held by 73 companies with a combined investment portfolio of $268 million in 2023

Market-determined dalasi exchange rate overseen by the Central Bank of The Gambia, with profits, dividends and capital repatriated through commercial banks

Broadly open foreign ownership, with restrictions limited to a few sectors such as foreign exchange bureaux, television broadcasting and defence, and an English common-law legal system with ICSID membership

Concession and public-private partnership route, illustrated by the 30-year Banjul and Sanyang port concession effective February 2025 and power purchase arrangements with the national utility NAWEC

Co-investment alongside development partners, including the IMF-supported ECF and RSF programmes, World Bank budget and energy financing, and EIB and EU co-financing of the $165 million Jambur solar project

Trade and market access

The Gambia is a member of the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS), a State Party to the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), and a member of the WTO. Its position on the River Gambia and the Atlantic coast makes the Port of Banjul a natural gateway for re-export trade: in 2024, Mali (25.2%), Guinea-Bissau (14.8%) and Senegal (11.7%) together took more than half of exports, with China (36.3%) the largest single market, principally for groundnuts. The Trans-Gambia corridor through Soma and Farafenni connects northern and southern Senegal, while the OMVG interconnection links the country to the regional power grid. Trade with the UK totalled £174 million in 2025, and English as the official business language, together with long-standing tourism links to the UK and Northern Europe, gives British and European investors a familiar operating environment.

The overall risk picture

The Gambia's risk profile is that of a small, open and reform-minded economy with a consistent growth record, improving macroeconomic management and strong backing from international partners. Inflation has fallen from double digits, the central bank has begun easing, reserves cover more than four months of imports and record remittances provide a dependable source of foreign exchange. Public debt is the principal constraint, and the IMF and World Bank continue to rate the risk of debt distress as high; however, the debt ratio is declining, the fiscal deficit is narrowing and SOE finances have turned to profit. The presidential election on 5 December 2026 is a key milestone, and the country's record of peaceful elections since 2017 supports continuity in its partnerships with the IMF, World Bank and EU. Investors should plan for some exposure to imported-fuel prices and project-delivery timelines, while taking comfort from the market-determined exchange rate, open foreign-ownership regime and growing pipeline of private-sector opportunities in energy, logistics and tourism.

Who should I talk to first?

Office of the President (Adama Barrow)

Sets national economic priorities under the Recovery-Focused National Development Plan 2023–2027 (YIRIWAA) and leads relations with key international partners

Primary reference point for strategic direction, particularly through the December 2026 election cycle

Gambia Investment and Export Promotion Agency (GIEPA)

National investment and export promotion agency; issues Special Investment Certificates and export licences, and will implement the updated investment framework

Standard first point of contact for market entry, incentives and site facilitation

Central Bank of The Gambia

Monetary authority; sets the policy rate (14%), manages reserves and supervises a well-capitalised banking sector

Relevant for currency, repatriation and banking-sector matters

Ministry of Finance and Economic Affairs

Manages the budget, debt strategy and the IMF and World Bank programmes; Minister Seedy Keita chairs the ECOWAS Bank for Investment and Development (EBID) Board of Governors

Key counterpart for PPP structuring, fiscal incentives and DFI co-financing

Gambia Ports Authority (GPA)

Guarantor and shareholder in the Banjul and Sanyang port concession, and steward of the country's re-export gateway

Essential partner for logistics, shipping and port-related investment

National Water and Electricity Company (NAWEC)

State utility and offtaker for power generation, operating the Jambur solar plant and OMVG regional power imports

Key counterpart for independent power, solar and energy-efficiency projects

Gambia Tourism Board

Promotes and regulates the tourism sector, estimated at 18–20% of GDP, and forecasts a 15% rise in arrivals for 2026/27

Relevant for hotel, aviation and leisure investment and destination marketing

Business networks

The Gambia Chamber of Commerce and Industry (GCCI) is the main private-sector body, alongside sector associations such as the Gambia Hotel Association. GIEPA hosts investor forums and stakeholder briefings for certificate holders and has partnered with the OIC's Islamic Centre for Development of Trade on the Banjul Investment Forum. The World Bank, IMF, European Investment Bank, European Union and African Development Bank all run active programmes covering budget support, energy, climate resilience and private-sector development, and the ECOWAS Bank for Investment and Development offers a regional financing channel. The diaspora is an important investment partner, with diaspora direct investment estimated at $300 million in 2024 — around 30% higher than FDI — measured through a new Diaspora Direct Investment Index that The Gambia piloted in 2026.

Who this market suits

The Gambia suits investors who value a stable, English-speaking, common-law environment and a strong development-partner presence: tourism and hospitality investors building on a sector worth around a fifth of GDP and a growing season; renewable-energy and independent power investors responding to rising demand and donor-backed grid upgrades; logistics and trade investors positioned around the Port of Banjul and regional re-export flows; and agribusiness investors in groundnuts, cashew, horticulture and fisheries. Financial-services and fintech firms can serve record remittance flows of $872 million, while diaspora investors and impact and DFI-backed funds are well placed to partner on infrastructure and SME growth.

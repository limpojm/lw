The economy today

The Palestinian economy is recovering slowly from the shock of the Gaza war. According to the Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics (PCBS), GDP rose 4% in 2025, with the West Bank up 4.4% and Gaza down a further 8.7%, but output remained 24% below its 2023 level. Activity contracted 4.8% year-on-year in Q1 2026 and 1.0% in Q2, although exports rose 18.2% and household spending 7.0% in Q2. Unemployment averaged 46% in 2025 (28% in the West Bank, 78% in Gaza). The private sector has proved adaptable: the Palestine Exchange's Al-Quds index rose 11.6% year-on-year to Q2 2026, and leading groups such as APIC have grown regionally. Reform steps include the 2022 Companies Law, the end of the prisoner-payment system in August 2025, recognition of the State of Palestine by the UK, Canada and Australia in September 2025, and legislative elections called for 28 November 2026.

The main structural challenge is the Palestinian Authority's fiscal and financial position. Israel has suspended transfers of clearance revenues, about two-thirds of PA income, since May 2025, withholding over ILS 13 billion in total; the PA pays partial salaries and has accumulated arrears of about ILS 20.5 billion, alongside debt of ILS 14.2 billion owed mainly to local banks. Palestinian banks also hold ILS 17–18 billion in excess shekel cash against an annual transfer ceiling of ILS 18 billion, and the two Israeli banks providing correspondent services, handling about ILS 51 billion a year, have agreed to continue only until the end of 2026. Mitigation comes from EU budget support (€310 million committed for 2026–27), a PMA anti-money-laundering framework built to international standards, and €883.6 million in early-recovery funding under the Team Gaza Initiative. Investors who establish compliant structures and DFI partnerships now will be positioned for recovery and reconstruction when conditions allow.

How do I get in?

Palestinian Investment Promotion Agency (PIPA), which approves qualifying projects for incentives under the Law on the Encouragement of Investment: 5% income tax for up to five years, then 10% for three years

Companies Law (in force since April 2022), which removed foreign-equity limits and local-partner requirements and introduced online incorporation and limited liability companies

Palestinian Industrial Estates and Free Zones Authority (PIEFZA) sites, including the Jericho Agro-Industrial Park developed with Japanese support

Palestine Monetary Authority (PMA) supervision of banking; there is no national currency and the economy operates mainly in the Israeli shekel, alongside the Jordanian dinar and US dollar

Co-investment with development finance institutions, following the model of the 2024 IFC, EBRD and PROPARCO package of up to $100 million for Bank of Palestine

Donor-backed recovery platforms, including the World Bank-administered Palestinian Fund for Reconstruction and Development and UNDP's proposed Gaza Economic Recovery Programme for small and medium enterprises

Listed-equity participation through the Palestine Exchange, where established companies such as APIC reported record 2025 sales of $1.31 billion across six regional markets

Trade and market access

Palestine's trade operates under the 1994 Paris Protocol, which aligns its trade policy with Israel's and means that about 90% of Palestinian trade, including food, fuel and medicines, passes through Israel; Israel took 89% of exports and supplied 59% of imports in December 2025. The EU-PLO Interim Association Agreement (1997) gives Palestinian industrial goods duty-free access to the EU, with agricultural liberalisation in force since 2012 and extended in 2021, and Palestine has belonged to the pan-Euro-Mediterranean rules-of-origin convention since 2014. EU-Palestine goods trade totalled €403 million in 2025, mostly EU exports. Jordan provides the main regional link via the Jericho area, and Palestinian groups such as APIC already operate in Jordan, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Iraq and Turkey. Greater recognition of the State of Palestine since September 2025 has widened diplomatic channels, although the practical gains for trade depend on access and on arrangements with Israel.

The overall risk picture

Investment in Palestine is subject to conditions that set it apart from most markets in this series. The Gaza ceasefire of October 2025 has reduced the intensity of the conflict but not ended violence; no international stabilisation force has deployed, the National Committee for the Administration of Gaza operates from Cairo, and humanitarian funding for 2026 was 37% secured by late July. In the West Bank, movement restrictions, the suspension of clearance revenues and the uncertain future of correspondent banking are the key operating constraints. Investors must comply with counter-terrorism financing laws, including UK, US and EU designations of Hamas, and should take legal and sanctions advice, screen all counterparties and transact only through regulated channels. Mitigation is available: DFIs and donors offer co-investment, guarantees and political-risk cover; the PMA maintains an anti-money-laundering framework built to international standards; and the private sector has shown resilience. The opportunity lies in lawful, well-structured activity in the West Bank today and in readiness for Gaza reconstruction as access, governance and financing arrangements are established.

Key reference point for Gaza reconstruction planning, procurement and eligibility

Business networks

The Palestinian Federation of Industries and the Gaza Chamber of Commerce and Industry are the main channels to local enterprises and are partners in UNDP's Gaza recovery planning, while the Association of Banks in Palestine represents the banking sector. Among development finance institutions, the IFC, EBRD and PROPARCO are shareholders in or lenders to Bank of Palestine, and the World Bank's Palestinian Fund for Reconstruction and Development channels donor co-financing. The EU, through PEGASE and the Team Gaza Initiative, is the leading source of budget and recovery support, and Japan sponsors the Corridor for Peace and Prosperity. UNDP has identified diaspora bonds and solidarity funds as channels for regional and diaspora capital.

Who this market suits

Palestine suits investors with patient capital, a high tolerance for operating constraints and robust compliance capabilities: development finance institutions and impact funds using blended finance and guarantees; diaspora and Gulf or Jordanian investors with established regional networks; strategic investors in banking, telecoms, consumer goods and ICT services in the West Bank; and suppliers of water, energy, agri-food and construction services prepared to work within donor-funded recovery programmes in Gaza. Conventional short-horizon capital is less suited to current conditions.

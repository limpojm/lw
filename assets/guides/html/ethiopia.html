Investor Entry Routes

  • Ethiopian Investment Commission (EIC), reporting to the Prime Minister, provides one-stop investor services, investment permits, land and utility facilitation and aftercare
  • Investment framework updated in 2020, including Investment Regulation No. 474/2020, which opened a wider range of sectors to foreign investors
  • Special Economic Zones and industrial parks offering incentives such as duty-free machinery imports and tax holidays, with foreign-exchange restrictions in the zones eased under recent reforms
  • Market-based exchange-rate regime since July 2024; in July 2026 the National Bank of Ethiopia cut the export surrender requirement on goods from 50% to 30% and its foreign-exchange commission from 2.5% to 1.5%
  • Ethiopian Securities Exchange (launched January 2025) and the Ethiopian Capital Market Authority, offering listings, investment-bank services and a platform for state-enterprise share sales
  • Partnership with Ethiopian Investment Holdings, the sovereign wealth fund overseeing around 40 state-owned enterprises, as reforms open telecoms and other sectors to private participation
  • The annual Invest in Ethiopia forum, which secured $13 billion of investment commitments in March 2026

Market-Entry Observation

Ethiopia in October 2026 is one of the world's fastest-growing large economies and is two years into an ambitious, IMF-supported reform programme. Officially reported GDP growth was 9.2% in FY2024/25, and the IMF estimates growth of around 9% in FY2025/26, projecting 7.7–8.2% a year through 2031. FDI rose 8% to $4.32 billion in FY2025/26, and the Invest in Ethiopia 2026 forum secured $13 billion of commitments. Since the birr was floated in July 2024, exports have strengthened markedly — up 53% to $5.0 billion in July–December 2025, led by gold and coffee — while gross reserves rose from $3.3 billion in September 2024 to around $5.5 billion in early 2026 and tax revenue has climbed from 6.2% to an estimated 9.5% of GDP. The Ethiopian Securities Exchange, a sovereign wealth fund restructuring state assets, and the 5,150 MW GERD add new depth to the investment case.

Ethiopia's main structural task is completing its macro-financial transition: restoring external debt sustainability, consolidating the new exchange-rate regime and bringing inflation down. Progress is tangible. After the 2023 Eurobond default, Ethiopia reached an agreement in principle with bondholders on 29 June 2026 to exchange the $1 billion bond for an $880 million instrument at 6.15%, which official creditors confirmed as comparable in August 2026, clearing the path back to international markets. The IMF completed its fifth programme review in July 2026, releasing about $464 million and providing around $200 million more to absorb higher fuel costs from the Middle East war. Inflation of 14.9% in September 2026 is easing from its July peak, supported by a policy rate raised to 16%, while public debt is projected to fall from 50.5% of GDP in 2024/25 toward 28.6% by 2031. For investors, the reform path is creating early-mover openings in export sectors, capital markets and privatisation.

Investor Risk Note

Ethiopia's risk profile reflects a large, fast-growing economy midway through a far-reaching reform programme with strong international backing. The float of the birr, revenue mobilisation and monetary tightening have delivered higher exports, rising reserves and a narrowing current account deficit, and the IMF has completed five programme reviews. The external debt default of December 2023 is a material fact for any investor; its resolution is now well advanced, with a bondholder agreement endorsed by official creditors in August 2026. Inflation remains in double digits and the Middle East war has added to import costs, but the IMF judges its effects on growth and prices so far as modest. Security conditions differ by region, and investors should commission site-specific assessments outside the main economic centres. Investors focused on export earnings, energy-linked industry and the opening of capital markets are best placed to benefit as reforms mature.

Market Access

Ethiopia, Africa's second most populous country with more than 120 million people, is a member of the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), giving long-term access to a continent-wide market. Its leading export markets in 2024 were Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Germany, China and the Netherlands, and export growth is now led by gold, coffee and electricity, with power exports expanding to neighbours including a long-term agreement of up to 400 MW with Kenya. Trade flows mainly through Djibouti via the electric Addis Ababa–Djibouti railway, while Ethiopian Airlines' network of more than 140 international destinations makes Addis Ababa a major continental air hub for passengers and cargo. As host of the African Union, Ethiopia also offers diplomatic convening power that supports its regional positioning.

Current International Business Channels

The annual Invest in Ethiopia forum, organised with the Ethiopian Investment Commission, is the principal platform for meeting government and deal-making, and secured $13 billion of commitments in 2026. The Ethiopian Chamber of Commerce and Sectoral Associations, the Addis Ababa Chamber of Commerce and the American Chamber of Commerce in Ethiopia provide business liaison, and the Ethiopian Capital Market Authority's regional capital market summits offer access to the emerging financial sector. The IMF, the World Bank — which is expected to provide $3.75 billion in support of the reform programme — and the African Development Bank are active partners, and Addis Ababa's role as host of the African Union and the UN Economic Commission for Africa makes it a natural centre for continental networking.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Ethiopia suits investors with a medium- to long-term horizon who can benefit from early positioning in a large, liberalising market: export-oriented manufacturers using Special Economic Zones and industrial parks; agribusiness and coffee investors; energy and power-intensive industry building on GERD; logistics and aviation-linked investors; and financial-services and portfolio investors positioning ahead of capital-market deepening and state-enterprise share sales. Investors with export earnings or hard-currency revenue are best placed to manage the foreign-exchange transition.

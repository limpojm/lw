Investor Entry Routes

  • Ministry of Investment (MISA) registration under the Investment Law in force since February 2025, which replaced foreign-investment licensing with registration and gives Saudi and foreign investors equal treatment
  • Regional Headquarters Programme, under which more than 500 multinationals have established regional headquarters in the Kingdom, strengthening access to government contracts
  • Four Special Economic Zones launched in 2023 (King Abdullah Economic City, Jazan, Ras Al Khair and the Cloud Computing SEZ), overseen by the Economic Cities and Special Zones Authority, offering tax and customs incentives
  • Saudi Exchange (Tadawul) Main Market, open since 1 February 2026 to all foreign investors directly, within a 49% aggregate foreign-ownership limit and a 10% cap per non-resident investor, with strategic investors exempt
  • Saudi Central Bank (SAMA) oversight of a riyal pegged to the US dollar; the repo rate stands at 4.50% after a 25 basis-point rise in September 2026 in line with the US Federal Reserve
  • Co-investment with the Public Investment Fund (PIF), whose 2026–2030 strategy seeks greater private and foreign participation across six priority sectors
  • Sector partnerships with Saudi Aramco and PIF portfolio companies in energy, petrochemicals, industry, logistics and renewables

Market-Entry Observation

Saudi Arabia enters the final quarter of 2026 with a decade of Vision 2030 reform behind it and a strengthening record as an investment destination. The IMF estimates real GDP grew 4.6% in 2025, with non-oil activity up 4.2%, inflation stood at just 1.9% in September 2026, and the non-oil PMI rose to 55.3 in September, its highest since February, as new orders and hiring picked up. Foreign direct investment inflows rose 33.5% in 2025 to SR159.1 billion (about $42.3 billion), led by construction and manufacturing, and the FDI stock reached SR1.06 trillion. The Investment Law in force since February 2025, the opening of the Main Market to all foreign investors in February 2026 and the Public Investment Fund's 2026–2030 strategy, which invites greater private and foreign participation, are steadily broadening the opportunity set.

Saudi Arabia's main structural challenge in 2026 is the disruption to Gulf shipping since the Iran war began on 28 February, with the Strait of Hormuz largely closed. Oil activity fell 24.7% in the second quarter and GDP by 4.8%, while non-oil activity still grew 0.6%. Saudi Arabia's strategic investment in the 7 million barrel-a-day East-West pipeline has proved decisive: around 4 million barrels a day were rerouted to Red Sea ports, and after a precautionary shutdown following a drone attack in September the line was back to 5.8 million barrels a day within days. The Energy Minister now plans to raise pipeline capacity to destinations outside the Gulf to 10 million barrels a day within two to three years. The IMF projects a 3.3% contraction in 2026 followed by 7.9% growth in 2027, assuming a gradual reopening from March 2027, and S&P affirmed the Kingdom's A+ rating with a stable outlook in September, citing its diversified export infrastructure and foreign-exchange reserves of around $462 billion.

Investor Risk Note

Saudi Arabia's risk profile combines strong sovereign fundamentals with a significant but temporary external shock. An A+ stable rating from S&P, public debt of around 32% of GDP, foreign-exchange reserves of about $462 billion, inflation below 2% and a banking sector the IMF describes as having strong capital and liquidity buffers give the Kingdom considerable capacity to absorb the costs of the 2026 conflict. The principal risk is the continued closure of the Strait of Hormuz, together with periodic attacks on energy infrastructure; the East-West pipeline, rapid repairs and plans for further bypass capacity have substantially mitigated both, and the IMF and S&P expect a strong rebound in 2027 once Gulf shipping normalises. Investors should plan for elevated security and logistics costs in the near term and build insurance and continuity arrangements into project structures, while recognising that the reform programme, deepening capital markets and record FDI inflows in 2025 position the Kingdom for a sharp recovery.

Market Access

Saudi Arabia is a member of the Gulf Cooperation Council customs union and has been a WTO member since 2005, and its exports are dominated by crude oil, refined products and petrochemicals sold mainly to Asian markets. The UK–GCC free trade agreement, concluded in May 2026 and awaiting ratification, will remove tariffs on around 93% of current UK exports to the Gulf and includes investment protection and services commitments. Saudi Arabia is unusual among Gulf producers in having coastlines on both the Gulf and the Red Sea: the East-West pipeline links the Eastern Province oilfields to Yanbu, while Jeddah Islamic Port and King Abdullah Port serve Red Sea trade routes towards Europe and Africa. Its scale, with the largest economy in the Middle East, makes the Kingdom both a major market in its own right and a natural regional hub.

Current International Business Channels

The Federation of Saudi Chambers and the regional chambers of commerce in Riyadh, Jeddah and the Eastern Province are the main domestic business channels, alongside bilateral bodies such as the Saudi-British Joint Business Council and the US-Saudi Business Council. MISA runs investor outreach and sector roadshows, and the annual Future Investment Initiative conference in Riyadh is the Kingdom's flagship forum for meeting PIF, ministries and major corporates. The Islamic Development Bank, headquartered in Jeddah, and international banks and advisers with regional headquarters in Riyadh are useful sources of deal flow and market intelligence, while the UK–GCC free trade agreement is expected to deepen government-to-government commercial engagement once ratified.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Saudi Arabia suits investors seeking scale and long-term exposure to the Gulf's largest economy: infrastructure, construction and engineering firms positioned for transport, housing and Expo 2030-related programmes; energy, pipeline, storage and port investors drawn by the expansion of Red Sea export capacity; industrial and logistics investors using the Special Economic Zones and national manufacturing push; tourism and hospitality investors partnering with PIF; and institutional portfolio investors now able to access the Main Market directly. Investors with Gulf-coast logistics exposure should build Hormuz-related scenarios into near-term planning, while those taking a longer view are well placed for the expected recovery in 2027.

The economy today

Germany in November 2026 is Europe's largest economy and its biggest market, now returning to growth on the back of a historic increase in public investment. After GDP rose 0.2% in 2025, the first annual growth since 2022, the government raised its 2026 forecast in October to 1.3% and expects 1.1% in 2027, driven by exports and state investment in defence and infrastructure; GDP grew 0.2% in the third quarter of 2026, 1.3% higher than a year earlier. The 2027 draft budget raises investment to €117.5 billion and core defence spending to €109 billion, supported by a €500 billion infrastructure fund. GTAI recorded 1,564 foreign investment projects in 2025, led by China (228) and the US (206), and flagship projects such as the ESMC chip plant in Dresden underline Germany's industrial depth.

Germany's main structural challenge is restoring industrial competitiveness amid high energy costs, an ageing workforce and shifting trade patterns. Higher energy prices linked to the Middle East conflict lifted inflation to 3.3% in September 2026 before it eased to 2.8% in October, and the government expects private consumption and investment to remain subdued. The response is substantial: tax incentives for investment, a phased cut in corporate tax from 2028, and record public spending, financed from a strong starting position with general government debt of 63.5% of GDP in 2025, among the lowest of the large euro-area economies. For investors, this creates sustained demand in defence, infrastructure, energy transition, automation and digitalisation, alongside opportunities to acquire and modernise high-quality industrial assets.

How do I get in?

Germany Trade & Invest (GTAI), the federal economic development agency, provides free support on market entry, sites, incentives and financing, working with state-level agencies

The growth booster package allows 30% declining-balance depreciation on equipment bought from July 2025 to the end of 2027, and cuts the 15% corporate income tax by one point a year from 2028, bringing the total burden to just under 25% by 2032

A €500 billion special fund for infrastructure and a reformed debt brake that excludes defence spending from the borrowing cap, creating a long pipeline of public contracts

Research and investment incentives, including an expanded research allowance from 2026 and regional and EU grants for projects in eligible areas

Euro-area membership: the euro is the national currency, with free movement of capital under EU rules

EU single-market and Schengen membership, giving goods, services, capital and people free movement across the EU

Foreign investment screening by the Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs applies to acquisitions in defence, critical infrastructure and key technologies

Trade and market access

As a founding member of the EU, Germany gives investors tariff-free access to the EU single market and to all the EU's free trade agreements, with WTO membership and the EU's common trade policy governing trade with third countries. Euro-area and Schengen membership remove currency and border frictions with its nine neighbours, and Germany is at the centre of European road, rail, river and air networks, with the port of Hamburg and North Sea and Baltic ports linking it to global markets. The European Union, the United States and China are its main trading partners, and its export industries in vehicles, machinery, chemicals and electrical equipment are embedded in supply chains across Central and Eastern Europe. Frankfurt's financial centre and the ECB's presence make it a natural base for euro-area finance.

The overall risk picture

Germany's risk profile is that of a large, stable and highly developed EU and euro-area economy with strong institutions, the rule of law and low public debt of 63.5% of GDP in 2025. As a member of the EU and of the Financial Action Task Force, it offers legal certainty and a mature financial system, and the ECB, which held its deposit rate at 2.50% on 29 October 2026, sets monetary policy for the euro area. The main issues are economic rather than legal: growth is recovering only gradually, energy prices are elevated, and inflation was 2.8% in October 2026. A large increase in public borrowing will fund infrastructure and defence, and the government plans to borrow €838.2 billion over 2027–2030, which the low debt starting point accommodates. Politically, the strong AfD result in Saxony-Anhalt adds pressure on mainstream parties, but federal institutions and policy remain stable. None of these issues restricts lawful investment, and investors can manage them through energy contracts, tax planning and early engagement.

Primary reference point for strategic direction and flagship projects

Standard first point of contact for market entry

Relevant for banking, payments and financial-sector matters

Key counterpart on tax and fiscal matters

Relevant for incentives, energy and approval matters

Co-financing partner for qualifying investments

Relevant for licensing and financial-services entry

Business networks

The German Chambers of Industry and Commerce (DIHK) and their global network of bilateral chambers abroad (AHKs), the Federation of German Industries (BDI) and sector associations such as VDMA and ZVEI are the main business channels, with regular dialogue with government. GTAI and state economic development agencies host investor events, and major trade fairs in Frankfurt, Munich, Hanover, Düsseldorf and Berlin are key meeting points for industry. KfW and the European Investment Bank provide co-financing, and federal and state funding programmes support research, innovation and regional development. The American Chamber of Commerce in Germany and the British Chamber of Commerce in Germany offer peer networks, and embassies of major partners maintain commercial sections that are a useful source of introductions.

Who this market suits

Germany suits investors seeking scale, engineering depth and stability at the heart of Europe: defence, infrastructure and construction firms positioned for record public investment; semiconductor, electronics and automation companies around Dresden, Munich and Stuttgart; energy-transition investors in grids, offshore wind, hydrogen and efficiency; financial and fintech firms in Frankfurt; and acquirers of quality Mittelstand businesses. Energy-intensive investors should plan carefully for power costs, and acquisitions in sensitive sectors require foreign investment screening.

The economy today

Honduras in October 2026 offers steady growth, sound macroeconomic management and a new government that has made investment attraction a central priority. GDP grew 3.8% in 2025, the central bank projects 3–4% growth in 2026, and the monthly activity index rose 4.4% year-on-year in July. Foreign direct investment reached $675.7 million in the first half of 2026, up 67.4% year-on-year and described by the central bank as the highest flow in 11 years. Since taking office in January 2026, the Asfura administration has restored ICSID membership, extended the Temporary Import Regime for five years, passed a Part-Time Employment Law and enacted an electricity-sector reform. The IMF completed its fourth and fifth programme reviews on 29 June 2026, bringing disbursements to about $725 million; public debt fell to 45.4% of GDP in 2025, and net international reserves of $11.5 billion cover 6.6 months of imports.

The country's main structural challenge is its energy sector. Electricity costs are high, supply can be unreliable and the state utility ENEE carries losses and arrears, while dependence on imported fuel has transmitted higher global energy prices into domestic inflation, which reached 6.3% in September 2026 and prompted the central bank to raise its policy rate to 6.0% in September. The government is addressing this directly: the new electricity law unbundles ENEE, creates a reform steering committee supported by the IDB, and opens the way for 1,600 MW of new capacity tenders being prepared with the World Bank, IDB and CABEI. Remittances of around 30% of GDP and comfortable reserves provide a strong external cushion in the meantime, and the reform programme creates one of the region's most significant opportunities in generation, renewables, storage and grid investment.

How do I get in?

National Investment Council (CNI), the state investment promotion body, offers guidance and facilitation for foreign investors and opened an office in Miami in April 2026 to attract US capital

Investor protection through restored ICSID membership (Convention re-signed in March 2026, in force from 16 August 2026) and the CAFTA-DR investment chapter with investor-state dispute settlement

Free Trade Zones (ZOLI) and the Temporary Import Regime (RIT), extended for five years under Decree 2-2026, offering tax and duty exemptions for export-oriented manufacturing and services

Electricity sector opening under the new electricity law, which unbundles state utility ENEE into generation, transmission and distribution companies and provides for tenders of 600 MW (planned for January 2027) and 1,000 MW (early 2028), open to all technologies

Banco Central de Honduras foreign-exchange framework: auction rules have been updated for more predictable dollar access and documentation requirements reduced, with a gradual move toward private-sector-led allocation of foreign exchange

Labour and trade reforms in 2026, including a new Part-Time Employment Law and active negotiation of a bilateral trade agreement with the United States to expand preferential access

Trade and market access

Honduras has been a WTO member since 1995 and is a party to CAFTA-DR, which provides preferential access to the United States and the Central American and Dominican markets together with strong investment protections. It is part of the Central American integration system, including a customs union with Guatemala and El Salvador, and trades with Europe under the EU–Central America Association Agreement and the equivalent UK agreement. Goods exports reached $12.2 billion in 2025, up 10.4%, with North America taking 47.2% and coffee generating a trade surplus with Europe. Puerto Cortés, the main Caribbean deep-water port, links the Sula Valley manufacturing base to the US East Coast, and talks on a bilateral trade agreement with the United States aim to deepen this access further.

The overall risk picture

Honduras's risk profile reflects an economy with consistent growth, disciplined public finances and a government actively improving the investment climate. A deficit of 0.7% of GDP in 2025, declining public debt, reserves equal to 6.6 months of imports and an IMF programme on track provide a stable macroeconomic base, while the return to ICSID, the renewal of the Temporary Import Regime and the electricity reform address long-standing investor concerns. Energy remains the main constraint: high power costs and imported-fuel exposure have pushed inflation above target in 2026, and the central bank has responded promptly with a modest rate increase. Security and governance have historically weighed on investment decisions; homicides have fallen to two-decade lows and AML and beneficial-ownership reforms are advancing, and the State Department reports that investor perceptions have improved since the new government took office. Investors should apply standard due diligence on counterparties and land titles, use established free zones and international arbitration clauses, and plan for energy-cost and climate scenarios.

Business networks

The Honduran Private Enterprise Council (COHEP) is the principal national business federation, and the Honduran-American Chamber of Commerce (AmCham Honduras) represents the more than 200 US companies operating in the country, mainly in manufacturing, textiles and auto parts. The Honduran Maquila Association and the Chamber of Commerce and Industry of Cortés provide practical insight into the Sula Valley manufacturing and logistics cluster. The National Investment Council runs investor outreach, including through its Miami office and Honduran embassies abroad. The IDB, World Bank and CABEI maintain active programmes in energy, infrastructure and private-sector development, and the IMF programme provides a regular, transparent check on macroeconomic policy.

Who this market suits

Honduras suits investors seeking a cost-competitive, US-proximate production and export base: nearshoring manufacturers in apparel, textiles and automotive wire harnesses using the free-zone and RIT regimes; energy investors and developers of solar, hybrid and storage projects positioned for the 1,600 MW of planned tenders; and port, logistics and warehousing investors around Puerto Cortés and the Sula Valley. Agribusiness investors in coffee, shrimp and fresh produce, and tourism and hospitality investors in the Bay Islands, can build on established export and visitor markets. Investors with experience in Central American markets, and those partnering with development finance institutions, are best placed to manage energy, security and governance considerations.

    Position early for the electricity tendersTrack the 600 MW and 1,000 MW tender bases being prepared with the World Bank, IDB and CABEI, and engage ENEE, the Secretariat of Energy and CREE ahead of publication.

    Build legal and financial safeguards into project structuresUse ICSID and CAFTA-DR arbitration clauses, conduct thorough counterparty and land-title due diligence, and agree foreign-exchange and repatriation procedures with the central bank and banks at the outset.
  5. 5
    Monitor US trade developments and reform milestonesFollow the bilateral trade-agreement talks with the United States, the FATF evaluation and IMF programme reviews, which will shape market access and investor confidence.

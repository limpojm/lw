The economy today

Guinea-Bissau enters late 2026 with solid growth, low inflation and an established IMF reform track record. Real GDP grew an estimated 5.8% in 2025, driven by a strong cashew harvest, good farmgate prices and private investment, and is projected at 4.8–4.9% in 2026 by the World Bank and IMF. Inflation fell to 0.9% in 2025, and the WAEMU-wide rate was just 1.3% in August 2026, with the BCEAO holding its policy rate at 3.00% in September 2026. The IMF reached staff-level agreement on the eleventh review of its Extended Credit Facility in May 2026, noting that all end-March 2026 targets had been met, with cumulative disbursements of about $52.8 million. FDI inflows remain modest at an estimated $34 million in 2025, leaving early movers room to establish positions in cashew processing, fisheries, renewable energy, mining and eco-tourism.

The country's main structural challenge is concentration: cashew nuts account for about nine-tenths of export revenues, leaving receipts sensitive to prices, weather and freight costs, while around $50 million a campaign is lost to informal cross-border trade. Mitigation is under way. The transitional government held the 2026 producer reference price at 410 CFA francs per kg under a 'zero tolerance' smuggling campaign; the 2024–2029 EU fisheries protocol provides €17 million a year; the OMVG grid and World Bank solar programme are expanding power supply from a 33% national access rate; and the IMF has prioritised diversification into fisheries and extractives such as phosphate and bauxite. Each of these creates a direct opportunity, above all in local processing, which would capture more of the cashew value chain at home.

How do I get in?

Investment Code (Law No. 13/2011) guaranteeing equal treatment, protection against expropriation without fair compensation, and recourse to conciliation and ICSID arbitration

Code incentives for investments of $34,000 or more, including customs and sales-tax exemptions on equipment and corporate-tax reductions for up to seven years; projects of $80 million or more can negotiate an investment contract approved by the Council of Ministers

Applications lodged with the Ministry of Economy, with the Directorate General for Promotion of Private Investment overseeing incentives; company formation via the Centro de Formalização de Empresas, where fees were halved to 180,000 CFA francs in 2025

BCEAO and WAEMU monetary framework: the CFA franc is pegged to the euro, and the Investment Code guarantees currency conversion and the transfer abroad of profits, dividends and capital through the banking system

Bilateral investment treaties, including with Portugal and China, providing additional treaty protection

Sector-entry routes through the EU fisheries protocol and licensing, mining concessions such as Farim phosphate, and cashew export and processing licences

Co-financing alongside development partners, including the World Bank's $35 million Solar Energy Scale-up and Access Project and the OMVG regional interconnection

Trade and market access

Guinea-Bissau is a member of the West African Economic and Monetary Union (WAEMU), sharing the BCEAO-managed CFA franc with seven other states, and of ECOWAS, whose regional market offers wider West African access, although its participation in ECOWAS decision-making bodies is suspended during the transition. It is also a WTO member and part of the Community of Portuguese Language Countries, which supports commercial ties with Portugal, Brazil and the wider Lusophone world. India is by far the largest buyer of its exports, overwhelmingly raw cashew nuts, while Portugal, Senegal and China are leading import sources. The EU fisheries partnership gives European fleets licensed access to Bissau-Guinean waters, and the OMVG interconnection links the country's grid to The Gambia, Guinea and Senegal. Its Atlantic coastline and border with Senegal and Guinea position it as a natural supply point for coastal West Africa.

The overall risk picture

Guinea-Bissau's risk profile combines sound recent macroeconomic performance with a political transition that investors should follow closely. Growth of 5.8% in 2025, inflation below 1%, membership of the euro-pegged WAEMU currency union and a sustained record of meeting IMF programme targets, including after the change of government, give a stable monetary base. The principal consideration is the return to constitutional order: Guinea-Bissau remains suspended from ECOWAS and the African Union, a new constitution expanding presidential powers was approved in August 2026, and the timing of the presidential and legislative elections set for 6 December 2026 is now under review. ECOWAS has not imposed economic sanctions and has appointed Senegal as facilitator. Investors should take sanctions-screening and governance advice as standard, build election-timing scenarios into plans, and use the Investment Code's arbitration and repatriation guarantees. Those who prepare now will be well placed for the wider re-engagement by partners that typically follows a completed transition.

Who should I talk to first?

Transitional Presidency (General Horta N'Tam)

Leads the transition, sets the electoral timetable and is barred from standing in the coming elections under the transitional charter

Primary reference point for transition timing and strategic direction

Prime Minister and Minister of Finance (Ilídio Vieira Té)

Leads economic policy and IMF programme dialogue, and opened the 2026 cashew campaign

Key counterpart for fiscal, tax and cashew-sector matters

Ministry of Economy and Directorate General for Promotion of Private Investment

Receives Investment Code applications and oversees incentive approvals and monitoring

Standard first point of contact for incentives and investment contracts

Regional central bank managing the CFA franc, monetary policy and bank supervision

Relevant for currency, transfers and banking matters

ECOWAS and Senegal (appointed facilitator)

IMF and World Bank

Business networks

Development partners are the most active channels for investors: the World Bank, whose 2026 Economic Update sets out a private-sector growth agenda covering tax simplification, credit access, customs modernisation and the national fibre backbone; the IMF country team; the African Development Bank, which approved a $20.6 million health-sector grant in December 2025; and the regional development bank BOAD, active in port infrastructure. The European Union is a major partner through its fisheries protocol and sectoral support. Portuguese-speaking networks, notably through Portugal, Guinea-Bissau's largest import source, and the Community of Portuguese Language Countries, provide practical market intelligence. In-country, the Centro de Formalização de Empresas and the Ministry of Economy are the main entry points for company set-up and incentives.

Who this market suits

Guinea-Bissau best suits patient, risk-aware investors with West African experience: agri-processors and commodity traders able to add value to the cashew crop locally; fisheries and seafood investors building on the EU partnership; renewable-energy and off-grid developers aligned with World Bank and OMVG programmes; mining investors, notably in phosphate; and eco-tourism operators focused on the newly inscribed Bijagós World Heritage site. DFI-backed and impact investors are particularly well placed, and all investors should phase commitments around the completion of the political transition.

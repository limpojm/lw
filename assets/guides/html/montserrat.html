The economy today

Montserrat in October 2026 is in a sustained construction-led expansion. According to the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank, real GDP grew 5.5% in 2025, following 4.4% in 2024 and 7.3% in 2023, comfortably above the island's long-run average of 2.4%; growth of 3.0% is projected for 2026 and 2.2% for 2027. Public finances are exceptionally conservative, with public debt at about 2.9% of GDP in 2024, inflation of 2.9% and a primary surplus of 2.9% of GDP forecast for 2026 and 2027. The 2026/27 budget of EC$345.95 million includes EC$162.2 million of capital spending, funded mainly by the UK, the EU and the Caribbean Development Bank, for a pipeline that includes the Little Bay port, the New National Hospital and utility upgrades. Foreign direct investment was 4.4% of GDP in 2024, supported by this infrastructure programme.

Montserrat's main structural challenge is scale and access, a legacy of the Soufrière Hills eruptions that began in 1995 and led to the evacuation of about two-thirds of the island. The population, around 10,500 before the eruption, has recovered to about 5,000, and air and sea links remain limited and relatively costly. The volcano has not erupted since 2010 and is continuously monitored by the Montserrat Volcano Observatory, which supports clear land-use zoning for investors. The government is tackling access directly, with the new Little Bay port, a new air traffic control tower and renewed ferry procurement. The same geology also offers opportunity: volcanic sand and aggregate exports, geothermal power that could reduce electricity costs of about $0.50 per kWh, and a growing geotourism and research niche anchored by the buried former capital of Plymouth.

How do I get in?

Direct engagement with the Government of Montserrat through the Office of the Premier and Ministry of Finance and Economic Management, which runs a 2026/27 capital programme of EC$162.2 million

Donor-funded public procurement: capital works are financed mainly by the UK (FCDO and the UK Caribbean Infrastructure Fund), the EU and the Caribbean Development Bank, with contracts open to regional and international firms

Company registration under the Companies Regulations 2024, with the Financial Services Commission overseeing financial regulation and compliance

Micro and Small Business Act incentives: up to five years' income tax exemption, import duty and consumption tax waivers on equipment and building materials, and stamp duty relief for qualifying businesses

Membership of the Eastern Caribbean Currency Union: the EC dollar has been pegged at EC$2.70 to US$1 since 1976, under the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank

Energy-sector entry through Montserrat Utilities Ltd and government renewable programmes, supported by geothermal legislation passed in 2023 and a planned 2 MW solar expansion

English common law framework under a stable constitutional relationship with the United Kingdom

Trade and market access

Montserrat is a full member of both CARICOM and the Organisation of Eastern Caribbean States (OECS), and a member of the Eastern Caribbean Currency Union, giving businesses a shared currency and central bank with seven other Eastern Caribbean economies and access to regional integration arrangements. As a British Overseas Territory, it benefits from close ties with the United Kingdom, its main development partner, while the EU and the Caribbean Development Bank also fund its capital programme. Merchandise exports are modest and led by volcanic sand and aggregate for regional construction, while tourism earned travel receipts equivalent to 10.8% of GDP in 2024. A submarine fibre-optic connection links the island to regional networks, and the new Little Bay port will strengthen cargo and passenger links with Antigua and the wider region.

The overall risk picture

Montserrat's risk profile combines strong macro-fiscal stability with the practical constraints of a small island. As a British Overseas Territory using the EC dollar, pegged to the US dollar since 1976, with public debt of around 2.9% of GDP and steady UK, EU and Caribbean Development Bank funding for capital works, the island offers a predictable legal and financial environment under English common law. The principal considerations are physical: access by air and sea, the cost of electricity and the presence of an active but closely monitored volcano. Each is being addressed through funded projects, including the new port, energy upgrades and continuous scientific monitoring that underpins clear zoning. The 2024 port suspension and its 2025 restart show that delivery timelines can move, so investors should build schedule contingency into projects linked to public infrastructure, while recognising that the pipeline itself is well funded and progressing.

Business networks

The Montserrat Chamber of Commerce represents local business, and the Montserrat Business Awards and the government-run Produced in Montserrat Expo (PRIME) provide visible platforms for meeting local enterprises and producers. The locally based Bank of Montserrat reported a year of growth in 2025, while the Eastern Caribbean Partial Credit Guarantee Corporation offers loan guarantees for smaller businesses. Among development partners, the UK FCDO, the European Union and the Caribbean Development Bank finance most capital projects and are useful sources of insight on the procurement pipeline. Research partnerships, including University of Oxford-linked work on volcanic risk and the RESET geothermal-minerals project, offer a further channel for investors interested in energy and scientific tourism.

Who this market suits

Montserrat suits focused, niche investors who value stability and early positioning: construction, engineering and marine contractors able to compete for UK-, EU- and CDB-funded tenders; renewable energy and storage developers, including those with geothermal expertise; boutique hospitality, dive and eco-tourism operators building on the island's unique volcanic landscape and planned geopark; and aggregates and building-materials firms supplying regional construction markets. Diaspora entrepreneurs and small businesses can benefit from the Micro and Small Business Act incentives, while digital and remote-service businesses can use the island's fibre connectivity.

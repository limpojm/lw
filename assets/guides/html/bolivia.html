The economy today

Bolivia in November 2026 is in the midst of its most significant economic opening in a generation. Since President Rodrigo Paz took office in November 2025, the government has ended the 15-year dollar peg (the boliviano now trades around 12 per dollar against the former 6.96), removed the diesel subsidy, secured an IMF Extended Fund Facility of about $1.9 billion approved on 2 October with more than two-thirds support in both chambers of the legislature, and restored diplomatic relations with the United States. Early results are encouraging: annual inflation has fallen from 20.4% at end-2025 to 7.08% in October 2026, exports rose 55% to $6.4 billion in the first half of 2026 on strong gold, silver and zinc sales, net international reserves have risen to $3.93 billion, and Moody's upgraded Bolivia to Caa2 with a positive outlook on 30 September. Foreign direct investment reached $1.02 billion gross ($620 million net) in 2025, led by mining (41.9%) and manufacturing (26.8%).

Bolivia's central task is completing the adjustment from a gas-and-subsidy model to a market-based, export-diversified economy. Gas output has fallen from over 60 million cubic metres a day in 2014 to about 24 million in 2025, and the transition is weighing on activity, with the World Bank projecting a 3.2% contraction in 2026 before 4% growth in 2027. The government is managing this through a sequenced programme: an IMF-anchored package of over $5 billion with the World Bank and IDB, fuel prices moving to cost recovery from January 2027 with more resources for social protection, and planned investment, hydrocarbons and mining legislation to bring in private capital. For investors, this creates an early-mover window in lithium, mining, gas, agribusiness and the infrastructure needed to support them, at a point where reforms are being locked in.

How do I get in?

IMF 36-month Extended Fund Facility of about $1.9 billion, approved on 2 October 2026, anchoring a reform programme expected to catalyse over $5 billion including World Bank and IDB support

Investment Promotion Law 516 (2014) as the current framework, with a new investment law in preparation that would limit unilateral nationalisation, open access to international arbitration and create a National Investment Agency

Banco Central de Bolivia (BCB) management of a more flexible, market-based exchange rate following the end of the 15-year dollar peg in June 2026; the IMF programme targets a market-based foreign-exchange system

Lithium partnerships through Yacimientos de Litio Bolivianos (YLB), including direct-lithium-extraction calls and a negotiation agreement with EAU Lithium, subject to a new legal framework

Hydrocarbons participation via YPFB, with planned legislation to reopen the sector to foreign investment through lower royalties and reduced required state participation

Mining sector entry through partnerships and contracts in silver, zinc and gold, the largest recipient of foreign direct investment in 2025

Trade and market access

Bolivia became a full member of Mercosur in July 2024 and is phasing in its rules, giving it a foothold in the bloc with Brazil, Argentina, Paraguay and Uruguay; it is also a member of the Andean Community and the WTO. Bolivia is not party to the EU–Mercosur agreement provisionally applied since May 2026, but its government is seeking to join through specific negotiations, which would open a significant new route to European markets. Exports reached $9.7 billion in 2025 and are rising strongly in 2026, led by gold, silver, zinc, soy derivatives and specialty foods such as quinoa, chia and Brazil nuts. As a landlocked country at the heart of South America, Bolivia moves trade through Pacific ports in Chile and Peru and eastward via the Paraguay–Paraná river system and road links to Brazil, positioning it as a natural bridge between Mercosur and the Pacific.

The overall risk picture

Bolivia's risk profile is that of a country part-way through a decisive, internationally backed reform programme. The facts are clear: growth is negative in 2025–26, reserves remain modest at $3.93 billion and largely held in gold, the boliviano has moved from 6.96 to around 12 per dollar since June, and reforms have met protest. Equally clear is the direction of travel: inflation has fallen from 20.4% to 7.08% within the year, exports and reserves are rising, the IMF Executive Board has approved a $1.9 billion programme with broad legislative backing, and Moody's has upgraded the sovereign with a positive outlook. The principal considerations for investors are the pace of legal reform in lithium, hydrocarbons and investment protection, and continued access to foreign exchange. Investors who engage now, with sound legal structuring, currency hedging and DFI partners, can secure positions ahead of the improved framework expected during 2027.

Business networks

The Confederation of Private Entrepreneurs of Bolivia (CEPB) and the Santa Cruz chamber of industry and commerce (CAINCO) are the leading domestic business bodies, and the Bolivian Institute of Foreign Trade (IBCE) is a valuable source of trade data and export-sector contacts. The American Chamber of Commerce of Bolivia and bilateral chambers, including the British-Bolivian Chamber of Commerce, provide links for international investors, a role strengthened by the restoration of US–Bolivia diplomatic relations. The IMF programme is catalysing financing from the World Bank, the Inter-American Development Bank and Fonplata, whose country programmes offer co-financing and project-preparation support. Mining and energy companies already operating in Bolivia, including Petrobras and TotalEnergies in gas, are a practical source of sector intelligence.

Who this market suits

Bolivia suits investors with a medium- to long-term horizon who are comfortable entering during a reform cycle: mining and battery-materials investors drawn by silver, zinc, gold and the lithium resources of the Uyuni salt flat; energy companies ready to participate in upstream gas and fuel-supply investment as the hydrocarbons framework opens; agribusiness and food investors building on Santa Cruz's soy, beef and specialty-crop exports; and infrastructure and logistics investors, often alongside development finance institutions, linking Bolivia with Mercosur and Pacific ports. Export-oriented projects that earn foreign currency are particularly well placed during the exchange-rate transition.

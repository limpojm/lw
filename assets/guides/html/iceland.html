The economy today

Iceland in November 2026 offers investors a high-income, well-governed economy with renewable energy, an educated workforce and rapidly improving public finances. GDP in the first half of 2026 was 1.3% above a year earlier, and the IMF projects growth of 1.8% in 2026 and 2.1% in 2027. Business investment rose 10.1% year-on-year in the second quarter, with data-centre-related investment reaching a record level, and Landsvirkjun is adding new hydro, geothermal and wind capacity. Tourism remains a core export, with about 2.25 million foreign visitors in 2025. The 2027 budget bill presented in September 2026 forecasts a treasury surplus of ISK 5 billion, ending eight consecutive years of deficits, and S&P revised Iceland's A+ rating outlook to positive in March 2026, citing a strengthening fiscal position.

Iceland's main structural challenge is bringing inflation down while keeping growth on track in a small, open economy. Inflation was 5.9% in October 2026, driven by energy prices linked to the Middle East conflict and higher public levies, and the Central Bank of Iceland raised its policy rate three times in 2026 to 8% before holding in October. GDP fell 1.1% year-on-year in the second quarter as a wider goods-trade deficit, partly due to data-centre imports, weighed on output. The mitigation is clear: the IMF expects inflation to fall to about 3.2% in 2027 and reach the 2.5% target by early 2028, fiscal policy is tightening, and treasury debt is projected at just under 38% of GDP by end-2027. In August 2026 voters rejected reopening EU membership talks, so Iceland's EEA membership remains the stable basis for access to European markets.

How do I get in?

Business Iceland (Íslandsstofa), the public-private promotion agency, supports exporters and foreign investors through its export and investment division

A 20% corporate income tax for limited companies, with a 24% standard VAT rate

EEA membership through EFTA, giving access to the EU single market for goods, services, capital and people, together with Schengen membership; Iceland is not an EU member

The Icelandic króna, with an independent Central Bank of Iceland and capital movement within the EEA framework

Act No 34/1991 on non-resident investment caps foreign ownership in fisheries, reserves energy rights to Icelandic and EEA persons, and limits non-EEA ownership of airlines to 49%

Renewable hydro and geothermal power, supplied by the state-owned Landsvirkjun and others, attracting data centres and energy-intensive industry

The government's Growth Plan to 2035, with an action plan of 35 measures for 2026–27 on permitting, regulation, energy and innovation

Trade and market access

Through the EEA Agreement and EFTA membership, Iceland gives investors access to the EU single market for goods, services, capital and people, and it is a member of Schengen and the WTO. EFTA's network of free trade agreements extends preferential access to many markets beyond Europe. Following the 29 August 2026 referendum, in which 52.8% of voters opposed reopening EU accession talks, the government has said it will not resume negotiations, so the EEA remains the long-term framework. Seafood and tourism are central to Iceland's exports, and its position between Europe and North America, served by Keflavík airport and transatlantic sea routes, together with abundant renewable power, makes it a natural location for data centres, green industry and Arctic logistics.

The overall risk picture

Iceland's risk profile is that of a stable, high-income Nordic democracy and NATO member with strong institutions, renewable energy and rapidly improving public finances. The 2027 budget targets the first treasury surplus in eight years, S&P has placed Iceland's A+ rating on positive outlook, and business investment, particularly in data centres, is at record levels. The main near-term issues are inflation of 5.9% and a policy rate of 8%, which raise financing costs, and a slowdown in output in the second quarter of 2026; both are being addressed through tight monetary and fiscal policy, with the IMF expecting inflation to fall in 2027. Investors should note the legal limits on foreign ownership in fisheries, energy rights and aviation, and that Iceland is not an EU member: following the August 2026 referendum, its relationship with the EU continues through the EEA Agreement, which provides access to the single market.

Business networks

The Iceland Chamber of Commerce and Iceland's sector federations of employers and industry are the main business associations, alongside bilateral chambers in key partner countries. Business Iceland organises trade and investment promotion, and Iceland's embassies in key markets, including London and Helsinki, run dedicated business and trade services. Landsvirkjun and municipalities hosting industrial sites are important early partners for energy-intensive projects, and the Growth Plan to 2035 provides a channel for dialogue on permitting and regulation. Embassies of major European partners and the United States in Reykjavík maintain commercial sections that are a useful source of introductions.

Who this market suits

Iceland suits investors seeking a stable, renewable-powered base with EEA access: data-centre and AI infrastructure operators drawn by geothermal and hydro power and a cool climate; energy and engineering firms supplying Landsvirkjun's hydro, geothermal and wind projects; seafood technology, aquaculture and marine-services companies, within ownership limits; tourism and hospitality investors targeting higher-value, year-round demand; and technology and life-sciences companies drawing on a highly educated workforce. Investors in fisheries, energy rights or aviation should take legal advice on ownership restrictions before engagement.

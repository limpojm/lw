Investor Entry Routes

  • Agence pour la Promotion des Investissements au Mali (API-Mali), the national investment promotion agency and first point of contact for registration, incentives and investor facilitation
  • Investment Code incentive regimes, administered through API-Mali, for qualifying projects in priority sectors
  • 2023 Mining Code, which increases state participation and now governs major gold and lithium operations; the Loulo-Gounkoto licence was renewed for 10 years under it in February 2026
  • Partnership with the state on mining assets, including state-held gold mines such as Morila and Yatéla and a state company created in February 2026 to manage mining holdings
  • West African Economic and Monetary Union (WAEMU) membership: the CFA franc is pegged to the euro and managed by the BCEAO, with transfers made through authorised banks under WAEMU foreign-exchange rules
  • OHADA uniform business law, giving a common regional framework for company, security and commercial-arbitration matters

Market-Entry Observation

Mali enters late 2026 with a macroeconomic recovery under way and solid policy credentials. Real GDP grew 9.3% year-on-year in Q1 2026, up from 2.7% a year earlier, as gold output rebounded. The IMF projects 5.5% growth for 2026, after 4.9% in 2025, with inflation at 2.2%, a fiscal deficit of 2.4% of GDP and public debt falling to about 41% of GDP, rated a moderate risk of debt distress. Mali met all targets and structural benchmarks under its IMF Staff-Monitored Program, completed in March 2026. Foreign direct investment was $844 million in 2025. The November 2025 settlement with Barrick, which included a CFAF 244 billion payment to the state and the return of Loulo-Gounkoto to production, and the arrival of lithium exports from Goulamina and Bougouni have broadened the export base. The government is targeting average growth of 6.5% over 2027–2029.

Mali's main structural challenge is security. Since September 2025, the al-Qaeda-linked JNIM has attacked fuel convoys to the capital. On 25 April 2026, coordinated attacks by JNIM and the separatist FLA killed the defence minister and led to the loss of Kidal in the north. The response has been practical: military-escorted convoys delivered more than 900 tankers to Bamako on 30 August 2026, and fuel supply and electricity in the capital had largely normalised by early September, although supply in parts of the centre remains irregular. Industrial gold output fell 22.9% to 42.2 tonnes in 2025 during the Barrick dispute, but is now recovering. Investors who site operations in the south and west, plan logistics and security carefully and partner closely with the state can benefit from Mali's large gold and lithium resources at a time of strong prices for both.

Investor Risk Note

Mali's risk profile combines sound macroeconomic management with a challenging security and political environment. On the positive side, inflation is low, the deficit is within the WAEMU 3% ceiling, public debt is falling and rated a moderate risk of distress, and every IMF Staff-Monitored Program target was met. Investors should, however, note plainly that the April 2026 insurgent offensive, the loss of Kidal and the JNIM fuel blockade show that security in much of the country is contested. the US and UK have urged their citizens to leave, and the military-led transition has no fixed end date. Investment is not restricted by broad sanctions, but EU targeted listings and the sanctioned status of Russian security contractors require rigorous counterparty screening with sanctions counsel. The 2023 Mining Code and the Barrick dispute show that the state expects a larger share of resource value, so agreements should include clear approvals, arbitration and political-risk cover. Against this, the resolution of the Barrick dispute, recovering gold output, new lithium production and improving US engagement point to real opportunities for well-advised, security-conscious investors.

Market Access

Mali is a member of the West African Economic and Monetary Union (WAEMU), sharing the euro-pegged CFA franc and the BCEAO with seven other states, and of the OHADA business-law area. It left ECOWAS in January 2025 and joined Burkina Faso and Niger in the Alliance of Sahel States (AES) confederation, which applies a common 0.5% import levy. Mali is a WTO member. Gold is the dominant export, and lithium concentrate has been shipped to China since 2025. As a landlocked country, Mali trades mainly through the Abidjan–Bamako and Dakar–Bamako corridors, with fuel imports now protected by military escorts. Together, WAEMU monetary integration, OHADA law and two coastal corridors give Mali workable access to regional and global markets.

Current International Business Channels

The Chambre de Commerce et d'Industrie du Mali (CCIM) is the main domestic business body, and API-Mali publishes regular investor newsletters and organises outreach on priority sectors. The large gold and lithium operators, including Barrick, Ganfeng and Kodal Minerals, are useful sources of practical intelligence on permitting, logistics and security in the mining regions. The IMF and World Bank remain engaged, with the IMF's Staff-Monitored Program setting a path towards a future funded arrangement. Renewed US diplomatic engagement in 2026, including talks on minerals, together with Chinese, Russian, Turkish and Gulf partners, is widening the range of financing and partnership channels.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Mali best suits experienced, risk-tolerant investors with strong security and compliance capabilities: mining companies and mining-services firms ready to work under the 2023 Mining Code in partnership with the state; lithium and battery-materials investors seeking long-term supply; logistics, fuel-storage and power providers addressing pressing infrastructure needs; and agribusiness investors in the cotton and irrigated-rice belts of the south and centre. Development finance institutions and investors with political-risk insurance are best placed to lead, with others entering through established operators.

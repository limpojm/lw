The economy today

The United States in November 2026 remains the world's largest destination for foreign direct investment, combining unmatched market scale, deep capital markets and technology leadership. Real GDP grew at an annual rate of 3.4% in the third quarter of 2026, up from 2.2% in the second, led by investment, consumer spending and government spending. New foreign direct investment rose 49.5% to $232.2 billion in 2025, led by investors from Japan ($50.5 billion), Germany ($26.7 billion) and Canada ($23.5 billion). Strategic partners are adding further capital: Japan's $550 billion investment pledge launched its first $36 billion tranche in February 2026, focused on energy and industrial projects. Federal incentives for semiconductors, nuclear, storage and advanced manufacturing, together with competitive state packages, continue to support large-scale industrial investment.

The main challenge for investors is policy volatility, particularly in trade and monetary conditions. The Supreme Court ruled in February 2026 that tariffs could not be imposed under emergency powers, and the administration has since rebuilt its tariff regime under other trade laws, including Section 301 tariffs of 10–12.5% on 60 economies from July 2026 alongside sector tariffs on steel, aluminium, autos and other goods. Inflation rose to 3.7% in September 2026, driven by energy prices up 20.4%, and the Federal Reserve under Chair Kevin Warsh raised rates in September to a 3.75–4% range. Investors can mitigate these pressures by producing locally, which avoids import tariffs and positions firms to benefit from strong domestic demand, and by securing incentives early. The Democratic victory in both chambers of Congress in November 2026 is likely to bring more legislative scrutiny of trade policy from January 2027, adding a further check on future changes.

How do I get in?

SelectUSA, the federal investment promotion programme within the Department of Commerce, connects investors with federal agencies and state economic development organisations; its May 2026 Summit drew more than 5,500 attendees and over $56 billion in investment commitments

CFIUS national-security review of foreign acquisitions in critical technology, infrastructure, sensitive data and real estate; Treasury is designing a Known Investor Program to speed reviews for trusted allied investors

Semiconductor incentives: the advanced manufacturing investment credit rose to 35% for property placed in service after 2025, for projects where construction begins by 31 December 2026

Energy tax credits for nuclear, storage, geothermal, carbon capture and clean fuels continue on phased timetables, while wind and solar credits now require construction to have begun by 4 July 2026 or service by end-2027

State-level incentives such as tax abatements, tax increment financing, enterprise zones, grants and workforce training, negotiated directly with state and local development agencies

Fully convertible US dollar with no capital controls, open capital markets and strong legal protection for investors

Foreign-Trade Zones, allowing duties to be deferred or reduced on imported components used in US production

Trade and market access

The United States is a founding member of the WTO and is linked to Canada and Mexico through the United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA), which remains in force until 2036 after the first joint review in July 2026, with annual reviews to follow; USMCA-qualifying goods are exempt from the July 2026 Section 301 tariffs. Since 2025 the administration has concluded investment and trade arrangements with partners including Japan, whose $550 billion pledge directs capital into US energy, infrastructure and manufacturing, while the EU, Taiwan, Japan, South Korea and Switzerland have capped Section 301 rates. A US base gives investors direct access to the world's largest consumer market, an integrated North American supply chain and an extensive network of ports, rail and air links.

The overall risk picture

The United States offers investors the strongest rule of law, the deepest capital markets and the largest consumer market in the world, with growth of 3.4% in the third quarter of 2026. The main risks are policy-driven. After the Supreme Court struck down emergency tariffs in February 2026, about $100 billion has been refunded and replacement tariffs under Sections 301 and 232 now apply, with further legal challenges pending; investors should plan for tariff scenarios and favour local production. Inflation of 3.7% and the Federal Reserve's September rate rise point to higher financing costs in the near term. CFIUS reviews and expanding outbound-investment rules are legal requirements that call for early specialist advice, although allied investors are explicitly welcomed. The Democratic majorities elected to Congress in November 2026 will take their seats in January 2027, which may increase oversight of trade and economic policy and create a more balanced legislative environment.

Central counterparts for incentive packages and local permitting

Business networks

SelectUSA's annual Investment Summit, held in National Harbor, Maryland, is the main federal forum for foreign investors, bringing together economic development organisations from all 55 US states and territories. Bilateral chambers such as the British American Business Council, the German American Chambers of Commerce and the Japan External Trade Organization's US offices support investors from their home countries, while the US Chamber of Commerce and the National Association of Manufacturers represent business at federal level. State development agencies and regional bodies provide direct site-selection support, and foreign embassies' commercial sections in Washington offer introductions.

Who this market suits

The United States suits investors seeking scale and long-term growth: manufacturers localising production to serve the US market and reduce tariff exposure; semiconductor, energy and advanced-manufacturing investors able to secure federal credits and state packages before deadlines; infrastructure and power investors meeting rising demand from data centres and industry; and technology, life sciences and financial-services firms drawing on US talent and capital markets. Investors in sensitive technologies should plan for CFIUS review from the outset.

The economy today

Uganda in October 2026 is one of Africa's steadiest growth stories, on the threshold of becoming an oil producer. The Uganda Bureau of Statistics estimates growth of 6.4% in FY2025/26, following 6.3% in FY2024/25, while inflation averaged just 3.4% over FY2025/26. The UNCTAD World Investment Report 2026 ranks Uganda as Africa's second-largest recipient of announced greenfield investment, lifted by the $4 billion Hoima refinery agreement, and FDI inflows reached $3.12 billion in 2024. The oil programme is well advanced: 1,414 km of the 1,443 km EACOP pipeline has been laid, the line is expected to receive crude by mid-December 2026, and first commercial oil and exports are now targeted for June 2027. The IMF expects growth to strengthen further as production begins, and the Bank of Uganda continues to project medium-term growth of about 8%.

Uganda's main near-term challenge is managing external pressures while oil revenues come on stream. Higher global fuel prices linked to the Middle East conflict widened the current account deficit to $4.6 billion and weakened the shilling, prompting the Bank of Uganda to raise its policy rate decisively from 9.75% to 12% at a special meeting on 14 October 2026; the shilling strengthened by about 4.5% on the day. The authorities have buffers to work with: gross reserves stood at $6.1 billion at end-May 2026, public debt is around 55% of GDP, and the 2026 Ebola outbreak was contained and declared over on 28 July. The IMF's reform agenda, including a Domestic Revenue Mobilisation Strategy and a transparent oil-revenue framework, provides a clear path, and oil exports from 2027 are expected to narrow the external deficit and diversify a merchandise export base currently led by gold and coffee.

How do I get in?

Uganda Investment Authority (UIA), the national investment promotion agency, issuing investment licences and incentives and running a One-Stop Centre for company registration

Investment Code Act 2019, under which foreign investors in priority sectors can access incentives subject to local-sourcing and export conditions

Free zones regime (Free Zones Act 2014), now overseen by the Uganda Free Zones and Export Promotion Authority, offering duty-free import of machinery and inputs for producers exporting at least 80% of output

Industrial and business parks offering tax incentives, customs-duty exemptions on equipment and raw materials, and land on favourable terms

Bank of Uganda oversight of a liberalised foreign-exchange market with a market-determined shilling

Oil and gas participation through licensing by the Petroleum Authority of Uganda and partnership with the Uganda National Oil Company across upstream, pipeline and refinery projects

Regional market access through the East African Community (EAC) Customs Union, COMESA and the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA)

Trade and market access

Uganda is a member of the East African Community (EAC) Customs Union, providing duty-free access to a regional market of more than 200 million people, and of COMESA; it ratified the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) agreement in 2018 and is a WTO member. Its leading export destinations in 2024 were the UAE ($2.2 billion), Kenya ($583 million) and the Democratic Republic of Congo ($489 million), while China, India and the UAE were the main sources of imports. Goods move mainly via the Kenyan and Tanzanian ports, and from 2027 crude exports will flow through the EACOP pipeline to the Tanzanian port of Tanga. Strong trade links with neighbouring markets, notably Kenya and the DRC, make Uganda a natural regional production and distribution base.

The overall risk picture

Uganda's risk profile is that of a fast-growing frontier economy approaching a transformational oil milestone. Growth has held above 6% for consecutive years with low average inflation, and the Bank of Uganda's swift response to the October 2026 fuel and currency pressures demonstrates a credible commitment to macroeconomic stability. The principal considerations are the timing of first oil, now targeted for June 2027, exchange-rate movements while the external deficit remains elevated, and a fiscal position that the IMF expects to strengthen as revenue reforms take hold. Governance and administrative challenges are real but manageable with sound due diligence and partnerships. Investors in oil-linked sectors should plan around milestone-based timelines, while investors in agriculture, manufacturing and services can benefit from strong domestic demand and growing regional markets.

Sets national economic strategy; President Museveni began a seventh term on 12 May 2026, giving policy continuity

Primary reference point for strategic direction and flagship projects

Uganda Investment Authority (UIA)

National investment promotion agency; issues investment licences and incentives and is developing industrial and business parks

Standard first point of contact for market entry and incentive guidance

Bank of Uganda (Governor Michael Atingi-Ego)

Monetary authority; raised the policy rate to 12% in October 2026 to anchor inflation and the shilling

Relevant for currency, banking and macroeconomic stability matters

Ministry of Finance, Planning and Economic Development

Leads budget, debt and revenue policy, including the IMF-recommended domestic revenue and oil-revenue frameworks

Key counterpart for fiscal incentives, PPPs and public investment

Ministry of Energy and Mineral Development and the Petroleum Authority of Uganda

Set policy for and regulate the oil, gas and minerals sectors

Essential for licensing and regulatory engagement in extractives

Uganda National Oil Company (UNOC)

State oil company holding Uganda's commercial interests in the upstream projects, EACOP and the planned refinery

Central partner for oil and gas and refinery-linked investment

Uganda Free Zones and Export Promotion Authority

Merged body overseeing free zones and export promotion

Relevant for export-oriented manufacturing and free-zone licensing

Business networks

The Private Sector Foundation Uganda, the Uganda Manufacturers Association and the Uganda National Chamber of Commerce and Industry are the main domestic business bodies, alongside the American Chamber of Commerce in Uganda for international firms. The Uganda Investment Authority holds regular investor engagements, including its Investors Baraza, and maintains sector-specific outreach. TotalEnergies, CNOOC and their contractors maintain a substantial in-country presence and are a useful source of intelligence on oil-sector supply chains. The World Bank Group, African Development Bank and IMF maintain active programmes, and development finance institutions are important co-financing partners for infrastructure, energy and agribusiness.

Who this market suits

Uganda suits investors with a medium- to long-term horizon seeking exposure to a fast-growing economy: oilfield services, engineering and logistics firms positioned for the production phase; agribusiness and food-processing investors building on Uganda's strength in coffee and other crops; manufacturers using industrial parks and free zones to serve EAC and wider regional markets; and infrastructure, energy and financial-services investors, often alongside development finance institutions. Investors should apply currency hedging and milestone-based planning for oil-linked projects.

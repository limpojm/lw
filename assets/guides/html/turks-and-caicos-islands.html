The economy today

The Turks and Caicos Islands in October 2026 combine one of the Caribbean's strongest growth records with an exceptionally strong public balance sheet. Real GDP grew 5.6% in 2024 after three years of double-digit expansion, and the 2026/27 budget forecasts growth of 3–4% in 2026 and around 4% a year through 2029, with GDP per capita of about $36,400. Tourism set records in 2025, with 640,754 stayover visitors and around 1.3 million cruise passengers, and stayover arrivals rose a further 6% to 384,590 in the first half of 2026. S&P upgraded the territory to A- in February 2025 and affirmed the rating in February 2026. Public debt stood at just $288,000 at the end of May 2026, against cash balances of $420.8 million and a National Wealth Fund of $97.5 million, while development agreements worth $1.27 billion are set to add over 1,000 hotel rooms and 2,000 permanent jobs.

The islands' main structural feature is their concentration on tourism, which accounts indirectly for about 65% of GDP, with over 90% of visitors coming from the United States, together with exposure to Atlantic hurricanes. The territory has built strong buffers against both. Cash reserves equal to roughly 23% of GDP meant that Hurricane Irma in 2017 had a limited fiscal impact, and Hurricane Melissa in October 2025 caused no significant damage, with airports reopening the day after closure. The government is broadening the economic base through new airlift, including United's Denver service from December 2026 and BermudAir routes from six US airports, along with port and airport upgrades. It also plans to borrow on capital markets for infrastructure, digitisation, solid waste and housing, using its credit rating to fund the next phase of growth.

How do I get in?

Invest Turks and Caicos (Invest TCI), the investment promotion agency operating under the Encouragement of Development Ordinance and the National Investment Policy revised in December 2023, advises on customs duty and stamp duty concessions, permits and post-establishment support

Development Agreements leading to Development Orders, the usual route for concessions on larger projects, subject to Invest TCI due diligence and with Immigration Protocol Documents to streamline expatriate work permits

No corporate, income, capital gains, withholding or wealth taxes; government revenue comes mainly from accommodation tax, import duties, stamp duty and work permit fees

US dollar as the official currency, with no central bank and no exchange controls, allowing straightforward movement of capital and profits

English-influenced common-law legal system with the Privy Council in London as the final court of appeal, and no general legislation restricting foreign direct investment

Business licensing under the Business Licensing Act, with most sectors open and certain categories reserved for majority Islander ownership; a revised framework (Exclusive, Reserved at 60% Islander ownership, Restricted and General) went to public consultation in March 2026

Permanent Residence Certificate by investment for qualifying investments of $750,000 in the Family Islands or $1.5 million on Providenciales, with at least 60% of employees Turks and Caicos Islanders

Trade and market access

The Turks and Caicos Islands have been an associate member of CARICOM since 2 July 1991. They benefit from duty-free access to Canada under CARIBCAN for goods produced in the islands, and from the UK's commitment of 9 November 2020 to keep tariff-free access for goods originating in the territory after Brexit. The economy is services-led, and its main external market is the United States, which supplies over 90% of visitors. Nonstop flights link Providenciales with Miami, Fort Lauderdale, New York, Boston, Atlanta, Charlotte and Philadelphia, and United's Denver route from December 2026 and BermudAir's six-airport expansion from October 2026 will widen that reach. Grand Turk is an established cruise port, with roughly 350–400 ship calls scheduled for 2026. The US dollar currency and English-language common-law system make the islands a straightforward base for North American and European investors.

The overall risk picture

The Turks and Caicos Islands present a low-debt, high-income risk profile underpinned by an A- rating from S&P, near-zero public debt, cash balances above $420 million and a National Wealth Fund approaching $100 million. As a British Overseas Territory, the islands benefit from the stability of UK oversight: the Governor is responsible for security, external affairs, law and order, and key aspects of public finance, and S&P views these ties as a positive factor for stability. UK and UN sanctions apply automatically, so investors should apply standard sanctions and AML screening. The main sensitivities are dependence on US tourism and hurricane exposure, both mitigated by strong fiscal buffers and a track record of quick recovery. Investors should also follow the 2026 business-licensing reform, which may change ownership rules in some local-service categories, and note that there is currently no data protection law or merger control regime. Fully dollarised and free of exchange controls, the islands carry no currency or repatriation risk for US-dollar investors.

Business networks

Invest TCI is the main gateway for investors, offering free, confidential advisory services from offices in Providenciales and Grand Turk and referrals to vetted local service providers. The Turks and Caicos Hotel and Tourism Association, a member of the Caribbean Hotel and Tourism Association, is the leading industry body for hospitality investors, and Experience Turks and Caicos, the destination marketing organisation, works closely with airlines and resort operators. The Caribbean Development Bank is the territory's established development lender. Locally qualified law firms and the major international hotel brands active on Grace Bay are useful sources of practical intelligence on development agreements, licensing and labour.

Who this market suits

The Turks and Caicos Islands best suit hospitality and real estate investors targeting the luxury segment, where record arrivals, new airlift and $1.27 billion of development agreements point to sustained demand. Branded residence developers and high-net-worth individuals can benefit from unrestricted foreign property ownership and residence-by-investment options, while construction, engineering and aviation-services firms can pursue opportunities in the airport and port programmes. Infrastructure, utilities and digital investors may find openings as the government finances projects in fibre, solid waste and housing. The territory is less suited to manufacturing or export-led strategies, given its small domestic market and import-dependent cost base.

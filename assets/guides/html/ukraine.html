The economy today

Ukraine in November 2026 is a wartime economy with deep international backing and one of the largest reconstruction pipelines in the world. Real GDP grew 1.8% in 2025, and the IMF approved a new $8.1 billion, 48-month Extended Fund Facility in February 2026, completing its first review in July with $2.2 billion disbursed so far. International reserves rose to $49.1 billion at the end of October 2026, helped by $3.3 billion of EU support that month, and the EU's €90 billion Ukraine Support Loan covers 2026–27, with the Commission proposing the remaining €45 billion for 2027 on 30 October. Foreign direct investment continues despite the war, at $640 million in the first quarter of 2026 and $487 million in the second. EU accession talks formally opened their first cluster on 15 June 2026, and the World Bank Group used the Ukraine Recovery Conference in Gdańsk in June to launch new risk-sharing and co-financing platforms for private investors.

Ukraine's defining challenge is the continuing Russian invasion, which drives security, energy and financing conditions. Martial law has been renewed by parliament in 90-day periods, most recently for the 20th time, and attacks on energy infrastructure led the National Bank to forecast a 0.4% fall in GDP in 2026 and to raise its key rate to 16.5% in October, with inflation at 10% in September. Public debt reached 98.4% of GDP at the end of 2025, and the draft 2027 budget allocates about two-thirds of spending to security and defence. Mitigation is substantial: concessional EU, IMF and World Bank finance, a growing market for war-risk insurance, and US-brokered talks, including a local ceasefire around Oleshky from late October and technical meetings planned for November. For investors able to manage the risk, early positioning in energy, logistics, agri-processing, defence technology and reconstruction offers a strong first-mover advantage.

How do I get in?

UkraineInvest, the national investment promotion office, supports foreign investors with market entry, project matching and government liaison

Law on state support for significant investments: projects of at least €20 million creating at least 80 jobs can sign a special investment agreement giving support worth up to 30% of the investment, including import-duty exemption on new equipment, land and infrastructure

Industrial parks with state co-financing for infrastructure, and the Diia City regime for IT companies, which may pay a 9% tax on distributed profits instead of the standard 18% corporate income tax

War-risk and political-risk cover from MIGA (which has issued $948 million since February 2022), the US DFC, the Export Credit Agency of Ukraine and private insurers

The US–Ukraine Reconstruction Investment Fund, which approved its first investment in March 2026 and has assembled an initial eight-project portfolio worth $1.2 billion

Co-investment with the EBRD, IFC, EIB and the World Bank, and EU programmes funded under the €90 billion Ukraine Support Loan and the Ukraine Facility

EU candidate route: accession negotiations, with the first cluster opened in June 2026, are steadily aligning Ukrainian law with the EU single market

Trade and market access

Ukraine trades with the EU under its Association Agreement and Deep and Comprehensive Free Trade Area, and is a WTO member with free trade agreements with partners including the UK, Canada and Turkey. EU accession negotiations opened their first cluster in June 2026, and continuing alignment with EU rules is bringing Ukrainian standards closer to the single market. Exports of grain, oilseeds, metals and iron ore move through the Odesa-region Black Sea ports, the Danube ports and road and rail crossings into Poland, Slovakia, Hungary and Romania, while neighbouring Moldova is a useful partner for logistics and the Ukraine reconstruction supply chain. The main export markets are the EU, Asia and the Middle East, with agri-food the largest export category.

The overall risk picture

Ukraine is a market at war, and investors should be clear about what that means: martial law and mobilisation remain in force, strikes on energy and other infrastructure continue, and the National Bank expects GDP to fall 0.4% in 2026 before recovering. Against this, the country's financing position is underpinned by an $8.1 billion IMF programme, the €90 billion EU Ukraine Support Loan for 2026–27 and reserves of $49.1 billion, and Ukraine has opened EU accession negotiations. Diplomacy is active, with a local ceasefire in Oleshky agreed in October and US-brokered technical talks planned for November 2026, but a comprehensive settlement has not been reached and Russia continues to demand territory Ukraine rejects ceding. Investors should combine political and war-risk insurance, DFI partnership and robust security planning; sanctions on Russia also apply to any counterparties with Russian links, so screening is essential. With these measures, Ukraine offers access to a large, EU-aligned reconstruction market at an early stage.

Business networks

The American Chamber of Commerce in Ukraine, the European Business Association, the British Ukrainian Chamber of Commerce and the Ukrainian Chamber of Commerce and Industry are the main business associations, with active dialogue with government on wartime regulation and reconstruction. The annual Ukraine Recovery Conference, held in Gdańsk in June 2026, is the main forum for governments, lenders and investors. The EBRD, IFC, MIGA, the World Bank, the EIB and the US DFC are the leading development finance partners, and the World Bank's new co-financing platform aims to mobilise up to $6 billion. UkraineInvest and embassy commercial sections arrange investor introductions and site visits.

Who this market suits

Ukraine suits investors with a long horizon and a tolerance for managed risk: energy investors in distributed generation, storage, wind and grid projects; agri-food and processing companies; logistics operators serving western border routes and the Black Sea ports; defence and dual-use technology firms; construction-materials and engineering suppliers for reconstruction; and IT companies using the Diia City regime. Projects structured with DFI partners and war-risk cover are best placed, and investors should screen counterparties for links to sanctioned Russian entities.

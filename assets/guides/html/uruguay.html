The economy today

Uruguay in October 2026 offers one of Latin America's most stable and predictable investment environments. The country retains investment-grade ratings, and its sovereign spreads are the lowest in the region and near historical lows, according to the IMF's September 2026 Article IV mission. GDP grew 1.8% in 2025 and the IMF projects 1.3% in 2026 before a pick-up to 2.4% in 2027, with construction (+4.2%), commerce (+2.8%) and pulp-led manufacturing (+1.9%) growing in the second quarter of 2026. Goods exports reached $13.5 billion in 2025, their highest level in a decade, led by record beef sales of $2.7 billion, and the foreign direct investment stock stood at $36.8 billion, around 45% of GDP. Reserves cover more than 11 months of imports, banks hold capital at almost double the regulatory minimum, and the EU–Mercosur Interim Trade Agreement, provisionally applied since 1 May 2026, opens tariff-preferential access to a market of more than 750 million people.

Uruguay's main structural challenge is lifting trend growth, which has averaged 1.2% since 2016, while managing the exposure of its agriculture to weather. A drought since the third quarter of 2025 cut agricultural output by 22.2% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, mainly through a smaller soybean harvest, leaving GDP 0.5% lower than a year earlier. The wider economy has absorbed the shock well: construction, services and pulp production continued to grow, and the government is advancing an irrigation agenda that the IMF says could expand farm output and climate resilience. Policy settings remain credible: inflation was 4.87% in October 2026, within the 3–6% tolerance band, and the Central Bank raised its policy rate by 25 basis points to 6% on 8 October to anchor expectations. A fiscal correction mechanism decreed in June 2026, the planned global minimum tax and a draft Competitiveness Law to cut red tape and ease trade together point to a reform path that creates opportunities in irrigation, agritech, water infrastructure and higher-value services.

How do I get in?

Uruguay XXI (national investment and export promotion agency) provides market intelligence, sector guidance and an annual guide to promotional regimes for investors

Investment Promotion and Protection Law (Law 16.906), which treats domestic and foreign investors equally, facilitates profit transfers and underpins tax incentives for approved investment projects

Free zone regime offering tax and customs benefits for qualifying services, logistics and industrial operations, including technology-focused parks such as Parque de las Ciencias

Free-floating peso managed by the Banco Central del Uruguay, with no capital controls and a highly liquid, well-capitalised banking system that operates in both pesos and dollars

Public–private partnership and concession frameworks for infrastructure, used for major projects such as the Ferrocarril Central railway

Energy-sector entry through partnerships and supply agreements with the state utilities UTE (electricity) and ANCAP (fuels), as in the HIF Global e-fuels project

Preferential access to the European Union under the EU–Mercosur Interim Trade Agreement, provisionally applied since 1 May 2026

Trade and market access

Uruguay is a founding member of Mercosur, alongside Argentina, Brazil and Paraguay, and hosts the bloc's headquarters in Montevideo, and has been a WTO member since its creation in 1995. The EU–Mercosur Interim Trade Agreement, signed on 17 January 2026, ratified by Uruguay on 26 February and provisionally applied since 1 May 2026, provides access to a market of more than 750 million people, with tariffs eliminated on around 75% of trade from the outset, rising to 92% of the tariff universe within ten years. China is the leading export market, taking $3.5 billion or 26% of goods exports in 2025, followed by Brazil, the EU and the US. Uruguay is also pursuing accession to the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP). Exports move principally through the port of Montevideo, linked to the interior by the Ferrocarril Central, giving Uruguay a strong position as a stable gateway between South America, Europe and Asia.

The overall risk picture

Uruguay's risk profile is among the most favourable in Latin America, built on strong institutions, investment-grade ratings, the region's lowest sovereign spreads, reserves covering more than 11 months of imports and a well-capitalised banking system. The main near-term considerations are the effect of drought on agricultural output, a fiscal deficit projected at 4.1% of GDP in 2026 and inflation slightly above the 4.5% target. Each is being actively addressed: the economy outside agriculture continues to grow, a fiscal correction mechanism and tax-administration measures are being implemented, and the Central Bank's October 2026 rate rise demonstrates a clear commitment to price stability. The IMF identifies strong agricultural revenues, EU–Mercosur opportunities and reform implementation as upside risks. Investors in agriculture should build weather scenarios into planning, and those with peso exposure should apply standard currency management, while investors across the wider economy can rely on Uruguay's long record of policy continuity and contract enforcement.

Business networks

Uruguay XXI runs investor promotion programmes and international roadshows, including presentations in London positioning Uruguay as a gateway to Latin America, and supports bilateral business chambers such as the recently launched Uruguay–Brazil Chamber. The Chamber of Industries of Uruguay and the National Chamber of Commerce and Services are the principal domestic business associations, and the Uruguay–United States Chamber of Commerce is active among international firms. Major foreign investors, including UPM in pulp and the operators in the free zones, are a useful source of practical intelligence on logistics, labour and regulation. The Inter-American Development Bank, the World Bank and CAF – Development Bank of Latin America and the Caribbean maintain active programmes in infrastructure, energy transition and private-sector development.

Who this market suits

Uruguay suits investors who value stability, rule of law and long-term predictability: global business services, software and fintech firms using the free-zone regime and Montevideo's skilled workforce; data-centre and technology investors drawn by a largely renewable power system; forestry, pulp and agri-food investors building on record beef exports and EU–Mercosur access; energy-transition investors in green hydrogen and e-fuels; and infrastructure and logistics investors in ports, rail and water. Agricultural investors should factor weather variability into project economics and will find growing opportunities in irrigation and climate-resilient production.

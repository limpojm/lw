The economy today

Australia in October 2026 offers one of the most stable and transparent investment environments in the Asia-Pacific. GDP grew 2.4% in the 2025-26 financial year and 0.4% in the June quarter of 2026, with private business investment 10.4% higher than a year earlier, led by data centres and transport equipment. Foreign investment in Australia reached A$5.1 trillion at the end of 2025, up A$151.6 billion in a year, including direct investment of A$1.3 trillion, with the United States, United Kingdom and Japan the largest sources. Australia is one of only nine countries rated AAA by all three major agencies, with gross debt forecast to peak at 35.8% of GDP. Policy momentum is supportive: the Australia–EU Free Trade Agreement was concluded in March 2026, the 2026-27 Budget announced faster FIRB processing for low-risk investors, and the Critical Minerals Strategic Reserve begins operating in the second half of 2026.

Australia's main structural consideration is the concentration of its export base in resources and in a single large market. China took 29.4% of Australia's A$665 billion of exports in 2025, and iron ore, coal and gas together made up more than a third of the total. Higher global energy prices linked to the Middle East conflict have also lifted inflation to 4.3% in September 2026 (trimmed mean 3.6%), prompting the Reserve Bank to raise the cash rate to 4.85% in November, its fifth increase of the year. The response has been purposeful: deeper trade links with the EU, Japan, the United States, Korea and India; a national critical minerals strategy moving the country up the value chain into processing; and strong fiscal buffers. For investors, diversification is itself the opportunity, with government co-financing, offtake support and allied capital now directed at critical minerals, clean energy, advanced manufacturing and infrastructure.

How do I get in?

Austrade (Australian Trade and Investment Commission), the national agency for investment attraction, providing sector intelligence and introductions, alongside state agencies such as Investment NSW and Invest Victoria

Foreign Investment Review Board (FIRB) screening under the Foreign Acquisitions and Takeovers Act 1975, with 2026-27 Budget reforms targeting decisions on low-risk applications within 30 days from 1 January 2027

Future Made in Australia incentives, including production tax incentives for critical minerals processing and renewable hydrogen, and the A$15 billion National Reconstruction Fund for manufacturing co-investment

Critical Minerals Facility (expanded to A$5 billion) and the A$1.2 billion Critical Minerals Strategic Reserve, operational from the second half of 2026, offering offtake, price-floor and co-investment support through Export Finance Australia

Free-floating Australian dollar with no capital controls; profits, dividends and capital can be repatriated freely, subject to standard tax and anti-money-laundering reporting

US-Australia Critical Minerals Framework (October 2025), under which each government committed at least US$1 billion to unlock a US$8.5 billion project pipeline

State-administered mining tenements and Commonwealth offshore petroleum titles as the main entry routes into resources, typically via joint ventures with established operators

Trade and market access

Australia has been a WTO member since its founding in 1995 and has an extensive network of free trade agreements, including the CPTPP, the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) and bilateral agreements with China, Japan, Korea, the United States, the United Kingdom and India. The Australia–EU Free Trade Agreement, concluded in March 2026, will raise FTA coverage from 78% to 88% of Australia's trade once in force. China, Japan, the United States, Korea and India were the top five export markets in 2025, with two-way trade of A$1.32 trillion. Resource exports flow through world-scale bulk and LNG ports in Western Australia and Queensland, while the October 2025 US-Australia Critical Minerals Framework and the AUKUS partnership deepen integration with allied supply chains.

The overall risk picture

Australia's risk profile is that of a mature, AAA-rated economy with deep capital markets, an independent central bank, a free-floating currency and a strong rule-of-law tradition. Its principal exposures are cyclical and external rather than institutional: commodity prices, Chinese demand, and imported energy costs, which have pushed inflation to 4.3% and interest rates to 4.85% in 2026. The policy response is credible and transparent, the budget deficit is narrowing, and gross debt is forecast to peak at a moderate 35.8% of GDP. Foreign investors should plan for rigorous FIRB screening in sensitive sectors and a temporary ban on buying established homes, but the 2026-27 reforms are designed to speed decisions for low-risk investors. Investors can take comfort from Australia's long record of policy continuity and from the scale of government and allied co-investment now available in strategic sectors.

Business networks

The American Chamber of Commerce in Australia, the Australian British Chamber of Commerce and the European Australian Business Council are well-established international networks, alongside the Business Council of Australia and the Australian Chamber of Commerce and Industry for domestic engagement. Sector bodies such as the Minerals Council of Australia and Australian Energy Producers provide practical access to the resources industry, and events such as Diggers & Dealers in Kalgoorlie are important dealmaking forums. Austrade and the state investment agencies run regular investor programmes and international roadshows. Government financiers, including Export Finance Australia, the Clean Energy Finance Corporation and the National Reconstruction Fund Corporation, are active co-investors in strategic projects.

Who this market suits

Australia suits long-term investors seeking stable, high-quality assets in a transparent jurisdiction: institutional and pension investors in infrastructure, renewable energy and real assets; mining and processing investors in critical minerals, supported by offtake, price-floor and allied financing; technology investors in data centres and digital infrastructure, where investment is growing strongly; and manufacturers in defence, medical science and clean technology seeking co-investment from the National Reconstruction Fund. Investors should plan for FIRB screening in sensitive sectors and for a higher interest-rate environment in the near term.

The economy today

Djibouti in October 2026 is a fast-growing services economy built on one of the world's most strategic maritime positions. GDP grew an estimated 6.5% in 2025, led by port operations, logistics, construction, transport, telecoms and trade, and the World Bank and African Development Bank project growth of 5.9% and 6.5% respectively in 2026. Inflation fell to around zero in 2025 and is forecast at 2.4% in 2026, anchored by the dollar peg, while fiscal consolidation brought the budget close to balance in 2025 and public debt below 65% of GDP. FDI inflows recovered to $149 million in 2025 from $68 million in 2024, and the DIFTZ added 22% more enterprises in the year to March 2026. The National Development Plan 2025–2030 and Vision 2035 prioritise energy and digital, and a September 2026 World Bank diagnostic found that targeted reforms could unlock at least $600 million of private investment and around 12,000 jobs over five years in off-grid solar, data centres and tourism.

Djibouti's main structural characteristic is its concentration on port and logistics services and on the Ethiopian corridor, which carries more than 90% of Ethiopia's trade. Port traffic dipped 10.5% in the first half of 2025 on lower transshipment, and 2026 disruptions in the Red Sea and the Strait of Hormuz have raised freight and energy costs. Djibouti has responded with practical resilience: its six ports have absorbed vessels diverted from the Gulf without congestion, Ethiopian cargo has been given priority, and the Doraleh Container Terminal has added ultra-large-vessel cranes. Regional ports such as Berbera and Lamu are expanding, but the IMF expects Ethiopia's growth and joint infrastructure projects to sustain demand for Djibouti's services. The government is also broadening the base: eight submarine cables position Djibouti as a regional digital hub with data-centre capacity nearing full utilisation, and renewable energy, tourism and the free zones offer diversification opportunities in which early investors can secure a strong position.

Where are the opportunities?

Djibouti City

Capital and services hub at the southern entrance of the Red Sea, home to government, banking and a large international presence, including French, US and Chinese military facilities

Financial services, trade, real estate, hospitality

Doraleh port complex

Djibouti's largest port facilities: the Doraleh Multipurpose Port and the Doraleh Container Terminal, which handles about 1.2 million TEU a year and can now serve vessels of up to 23,000 TEU

Port logistics, transshipment, bulk handling, warehousing

Djibouti International Free Trade Zone (DIFTZ)

Africa's self-described largest free trade zone, hosting 513 enterprises by March 2026 (up 22% year-on-year) with land utilisation of 70%

Assembly, manufacturing, distribution, trade

Djibouti–Addis Ababa railway and corridor

753 km rail link to Ethiopia that cuts freight transit to under 20 hours; the corridor carries over 90% of Ethiopia's trade

Rail and road logistics, dry ports, cross-border trade

Tadjourah (north)

Port opened in 2017 for ships of up to 65,000 dwt, designed to handle up to 4 million tonnes of potash a year and offered to Ethiopia as an additional outlet

Mining exports, bulk logistics, corridor development

Damerjog

Southern industrial area hosting the DDID commercial free zone, opened in 2013, and a port industrial zone being developed with a new oil jetty

Energy storage, industry, free-zone activities

Lake Assal, Ghoubet and Lake Abbe

Distinctive natural assets, including Africa's lowest point on land and whale shark sightings, alongside the Goubet salt port

Tourism, salt, renewable energy

How do I get in?

National Investment Promotion Agency (NIPA, est. 2001) and its Guichet Unique one-stop shop for registration and approvals, with registration stated to take as little as 72 hours; policy is overseen by the Secretariat of State for Investment and Private Sector Development

Investment Law of 16 October 1994, granting equal treatment of local and foreign investors, no local-partner requirement, free repatriation of profits and protection against expropriation without equitable compensation, with tiered tax incentives for projects above DJF 5 million and DJF 50 million

Free zones under Law No. 53 of 17 May 2014, offering exemption from most direct and indirect taxes for up to 50 years and duty-free import, including the DIFTZ, the Djibouti Free Zone (40 hectares, 318 companies) and the DDID zone at Damerjog

Djiboutian franc fixed at DJF 177.71 per US dollar under a currency board, with no foreign exchange restrictions and free transfer of profits

International dispute resolution through the ICSID Convention (in force since July 2020) and the New York Convention, plus bilateral investment treaties with France, Kuwait, Switzerland and Türkiye

Private power generation through power purchase agreements with the state utility, with around 10 MW of off-grid solar deployed by private investors since 2022

Sector entry is largely open, with foreign-ownership limits in maritime transport support, security services and insurance, and telecoms, water and electricity distribution held by state-owned enterprises

Trade and market access

Djibouti has been a WTO member since 31 May 1995 and belongs to COMESA, the African Union, the League of Arab States and IGAD. Its location on the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, linking the Red Sea with the Gulf of Aden and the Indian Ocean, places it on the main Asia–Europe shipping lane, and its ports are Ethiopia's principal sea outlet. Exports are dominated by re-exports, with Ethiopia taking 76.5% in 2023, while China (31.7%), India (12.1%) and the UAE (9.6%) are the leading import sources. The Addis Ababa railway, jointly managed with Ethiopia since 2024, carries about 14% of corridor cargo, and eight submarine cables connect Djibouti to Asia, Europe and Africa, giving it a strong position as both a physical and a digital gateway to the Horn of Africa.

The overall risk picture

Djibouti's risk profile reflects a stable, dollar-anchored hub economy with a consistent growth record, averaging 5.3% a year from 2016 to 2024 and an estimated 6.5% in 2025. President Ismail Omar Guelleh, in office since 1999, was re-elected for a sixth term in April 2026, which signals policy continuity around the National Development Plan 2025–2030. The main considerations are concentration on port services and Ethiopian trade, external shipping disruption in the Red Sea and Gulf, competition from regional ports and a debt stock that is largely owed to bilateral lenders. Each is being addressed: fiscal consolidation and a China Exim moratorium are bringing debt down, the IMF expects Ethiopian demand to offset trade diversion, and Djibouti's ports absorbed diverted Gulf traffic in 2026 without congestion. Investors should take international legal advice on contract structure, using ICSID, bilateral treaties and political-risk insurance, and should build freight and energy-cost scenarios into planning, while recognising that the dollar peg and free repatriation remove much of the currency risk found elsewhere in the region.

Who should I talk to first?

Office of the President (Ismail Omar Guelleh)

Sets national strategy, including the National Development Plan 2025–2030 and Vision 2035; re-elected for a sixth term in April 2026

Primary reference point for Djibouti's strategic and diplomatic direction

National Investment Promotion Agency (NIPA)

Promotes private investment and runs the Guichet Unique one-stop shop for registration and incentives

Standard first point of contact for market entry and Investment Law incentives

Djibouti Ports and Free Zones Authority (DPFZA)

State body managing the ports, airport and free zones, chaired by Aboubaker Omar Hadi

Essential counterpart for any port, logistics or free-zone investment

Central Bank of Djibouti (Banque Centrale de Djibouti)

Operates the currency board that fixes the franc to the US dollar and supervises the banking sector

Relevant for banking, currency and repatriation matters

Ministry of Economy and Finance (Minister Ilyas Moussa Dawaleh)

Leads fiscal policy, debt management and relations with the IMF, World Bank and AfDB

Key interlocutor on public–private partnerships, incentives and development finance

Ethio-Djibouti railway (joint management)

Operates the 753 km Addis Ababa–Djibouti line, jointly managed by the two governments since 2024

Counterpart for rail logistics, dry-port and corridor investments

Djibouti Telecom

State-owned telecoms operator at the centre of Djibouti's submarine-cable and connectivity infrastructure

Key partner for data-centre and digital-infrastructure opportunities

Business networks

The Djibouti Chamber of Commerce, established under Law No. 179 of 2002, is the main domestic business association, and NIPA provides structured outreach to foreign investors. The World Bank has an active programme of 13 projects with about $483 million in IDA financing, including a $25.75 million Program-for-Results approved in March 2026 for diversification and private-sector growth, and its 2026 Country Private Sector Diagnostic maps opportunities in solar, data centres and tourism. The African Development Bank's 2026 Country Report focuses on mobilising large-scale development finance, and MIGA has backed landmark projects including the Doraleh Container Terminal and the Red Sea Power wind farm. The US DFC can finance private investment, UNDP's SDG Investor Map highlights investable opportunities, and the many international companies operating in the DIFTZ are a practical source of market intelligence.

Who this market suits

Djibouti suits investors seeking a stable, dollar-linked platform for trade with Ethiopia and the wider Horn of Africa: logistics, warehousing and shipping-services investors positioned around Doraleh and the Ethiopian corridor; manufacturers and distributors using DIFTZ incentives to serve East African markets; renewable-energy investors, given high power costs and a World Bank estimate of up to $390 million of off-grid solar potential; digital-infrastructure investors drawn by eight submarine cables and data-centre capacity nearing full utilisation; and tourism and hospitality investors developing Djibouti's distinctive natural assets. DFIs and investors comfortable with structured contracts and political-risk cover are best placed to lead in larger infrastructure projects.

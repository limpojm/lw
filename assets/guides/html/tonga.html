The economy today

Tonga in November 2026 offers investors a stable constitutional monarchy, a new reform-minded government and strong external buffers. Published figures show real GDP growth of 4.4% in FY2025 as post-eruption reconstruction continued, and the IMF expects growth to moderate in FY2026. Foreign reserves cover about 10½ months of imports, remittances of around $265 million a year support household spending, and tourism is recovering: arrivals in March 2026 were 78.7% higher than a year earlier. Lord Fakafanua, elected Prime Minister on 15 December 2025 at the age of 40, has prioritised a leaner public service, easier dealings for the private sector and a broader tax base. The 2026/27 budget of TOP 949.4 million includes TOP 214.2 million of in-kind support from development partners, alongside renewable-energy, agriculture and tourism-recovery measures.

Tonga's main structural challenge is building resilience in a small, remote economy exposed to natural disasters, emigration and external price shocks. The 2022 Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha'apai eruption and tsunami caused widespread damage, the IMF rates Tonga at high risk of debt distress, and headline inflation rose to 9.3% in July 2026, above the 5% reference rate. Mitigation is substantial: the government has stopped taking on new loans and plans to clear its China Exim Bank debt, about $67 million, by 2030; grant financing from partners funds most major infrastructure; and the central bank is ready to tighten policy if needed. The Pacific Resilience Facility, with an office in Tonga and $173 million of pledges, adds a regional source of climate finance. For investors, reconstruction, renewable energy towards the 70% target, tourism and fisheries offer clear opportunities.

How do I get in?

Ministry of Trade and Economic Development, whose Chief Executive acts as Registrar of Foreign Investment, the first point of contact for foreign investors

Foreign Investment Act 2020: overseas investors may do business in Tonga after obtaining a registration certificate, with decisions due within five working days, and only short reserved and restricted lists set by regulation

Investor protections in the Act: no expropriation without due process and full, timely compensation; transfer of capital and income abroad, subject to tax and exchange-control laws; and the ICSID Convention given the force of law

Tax regime: 25% corporate income tax, 15% consumption tax and 15% withholding tax on dividends, interest and royalties

Land: foreigners cannot buy land, which is held under the Crown and noble estates system, so projects use registered leases

Currency: the pa'anga, managed by the National Reserve Bank of Tonga, with the policy rate at 2% and foreign reserves above required thresholds

Donor-financed projects: World Bank, ADB, Australian, New Zealand, Japanese and EU programmes in energy, transport and resilience create procurement and contracting opportunities

Trade and market access

Tonga has been a member of the WTO since 2007 and is a party to the PACER Plus regional trade and development agreement with Australia, New Zealand and other Pacific states, and it belongs to the Pacific Islands Forum. Its closest economic ties are with New Zealand, Australia and the United States, which provide most air visitors, most remittances and key markets for squash, root crops, kava, vanilla and tuna. Labour-mobility schemes with Australia and New Zealand are a major source of income and skills. Fua'amotu International Airport and the port at Nuku'alofa connect Tonga with New Zealand, Australia and Fiji, and cruise calls are rising, with 3,030 cruise passengers in March 2026. China remains an important development and trade partner, and Tonga hosts an office of the Pacific Resilience Facility, positioning it within the region's climate-finance architecture.

The overall risk picture

Tonga's risk profile is that of a politically stable Pacific monarchy with strong reserves, deep partnerships with Australia, New Zealand, Japan and multilateral banks, and a new government committed to faster delivery and private-sector facilitation. The main considerations are physical and fiscal: exposure to cyclones, earthquakes and volcanic activity, a high risk of debt distress as assessed by the IMF, and imported inflation, which reached 9.3% in July 2026. Each is actively managed: the government has stopped new borrowing and is repaying its China Exim Bank loan, grants fund most major infrastructure, reserves cover about 10½ months of imports, and the central bank has signalled readiness to tighten policy. Investors should note that foreigners cannot buy land, that capital transfers are subject to exchange-control rules, and that partial US visa restrictions on Tongan nationals apply. With resilient design, full insurance and early engagement with government, Tonga offers a dependable base for targeted projects in tourism, energy, fisheries and construction.

Business networks

The Tonga Chamber of Commerce and Industry is the main private-sector body and engages regularly with government on business conditions. The Asian Development Bank and the World Bank are the largest multilateral partners, financing energy, transport and resilience projects such as the Fanga'uta Lagoon Bridge, while Australia, New Zealand, Japan, the European Union and China provide substantial bilateral support. The Pacific Islands Forum, Pacific Trade Invest offices in Auckland and Sydney, and the Pacific Resilience Facility offer regional networks and co-financing links. The diaspora in New Zealand, Australia and the United States is a valuable source of investment, visitors and introductions.

Who this market suits

Tonga suits investors with a long-term, partnership-based approach and an appetite for small-scale, high-impact projects: renewable-energy and battery developers supporting the 70% renewable target; engineering and construction firms bidding for grant-funded resilience and transport projects; tourism and hospitality investors targeting Vava'u, marine tourism and the growing diaspora and cruise markets; and fisheries and agro-processing businesses serving New Zealand, Australian and US markets. Investors should plan around leasehold land, exchange-control approvals and disaster-resilient design.

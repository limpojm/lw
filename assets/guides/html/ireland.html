The economy today

Ireland in November 2026 remains one of Europe's most successful destinations for foreign direct investment, combining euro-area membership, an English-speaking common-law system and a long record of hosting global technology and life-sciences companies. GDP grew 8.0% in 2025, and modified domestic demand, the best measure of the domestic economy, rose 4.7%; it grew 3.1% in the first half of 2026. Multinational output drove GDP up 3.9% year-on-year in the third quarter of 2026. IDA Ireland approved a record 323 investments in 2025, expected to create more than 15,300 jobs, with client firms employing 312,400 people and committing a record €2.5 billion to research and innovation. Public finances are strong: the 2025 general government surplus was €11.6 billion, 1.8% of GDP, and gross debt fell to 32.9% of GDP.

Ireland's main structural challenge is building the housing, energy and water infrastructure needed to keep pace with growth, while reducing reliance on a few large corporate taxpayers. The Fiscal Council estimates that three companies paid almost half of corporation tax in 2025 and that about €20 billion of receipts in 2026 may be transitory. The government is using these revenues to invest, with capital spending set to exceed €20 billion in 2027, and the new grid policy for data centres requires on-site generation or storage and new Irish renewable supply. Inflation rose to 4.0% in October 2026, driven by energy. US tariffs are a further consideration, but EU pharmaceutical exports to the US are capped at 15% under the EU-US arrangement, and many leading pharma firms with Irish plants have agreed terms that bring their rate to zero.

How do I get in?

IDA Ireland, the state inward-investment agency, provides site, grant, R&D and talent support; it approved a record 323 investments in 2025, 57% of them outside Dublin

Corporation tax of 12.5% on trading income, with a 15% minimum rate under the EU Pillar Two rules for groups with revenue of €750 million or more

IDA grants for capital, R&D, training and employment, plus R&D tax relief, for qualifying projects across all regions

Euro-area membership: the euro is the national currency, with free movement of capital under EU rules

EU single-market membership, with Ireland the only English-speaking common-law jurisdiction in the euro area

Central Bank of Ireland authorisation for banks, funds, insurers and payment firms, giving passporting rights across the EU

Shannon Free Zone and regional IDA business parks offering serviced sites for manufacturing and services

Trade and market access

As an EU member, Ireland gives investors tariff-free access to the EU single market of some 450 million people and to all the EU's free trade agreements, with WTO membership and the EU's common trade policy governing trade with third countries. As the only English-speaking common-law country in the euro area, it is a natural European base for US, UK and Asian companies, and it shares a Common Travel Area with the United Kingdom. Under the Windsor Framework, goods move freely across the border with Northern Ireland. The United States is Ireland's largest export market, led by pharmaceuticals, chemicals and medical devices, and EU exports to the US are covered by the EU-US arrangement that caps most tariffs at 15%. Dublin, Cork, Shannon and Rosslare connect Ireland with Britain, continental Europe and North America.

The overall risk picture

Ireland's risk profile is that of a stable, well-governed EU and euro-area member with a budget surplus, falling debt and a long record of policy continuity on foreign investment. Gross debt of 32.9% of GDP and a 2025 surplus of €11.6 billion give the state ample room to invest in infrastructure. The main risks are external and structural: US trade policy, the concentration of corporation tax in a small number of firms, and capacity limits in housing, energy and water. Each is being addressed, through the 15% cap on EU pharmaceutical tariffs, the saving of windfall receipts and record capital investment. Inflation of 4.0% in October 2026 and higher ECB rates, with the deposit rate at 2.50%, add to costs. There are no sanctions or legal restrictions specific to Ireland beyond those applying across the EU, and the government formed in January 2025 under Taoiseach Micheál Martin provides continuity.

Business networks

The American Chamber of Commerce Ireland, the British Irish Chamber of Commerce, Ibec (the main employers' body), Chambers Ireland and the Dublin Chamber are the principal business networks, with regular dialogue with government on tax, infrastructure and skills. IDA Ireland and Enterprise Ireland maintain international offices and run investor and trade programmes, and sector groups such as Biopharmachem Ireland and the Irish Medtech Association connect investors with the life-sciences ecosystem. The European Investment Bank is an active lender for infrastructure and housing, and the Ireland Strategic Investment Fund co-invests alongside private capital. Embassies of major partners maintain commercial sections that provide introductions.

Who this market suits

Ireland suits investors seeking an English-speaking, common-law base inside the EU and the euro area: pharmaceutical, biotech and medical-technology companies building on the Cork, Galway and Dublin clusters; technology, software and semiconductor firms drawing on Ireland's talent pool; financial-services, fund and aircraft-leasing businesses using Central Bank of Ireland authorisation to serve the EU; data-centre and energy investors able to meet the new grid rules; and infrastructure and housing investors partnering with a state committing more than €20 billion a year to capital projects.

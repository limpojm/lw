The economy today

The Dominican Republic in November 2026 is one of Latin America's most consistent growth stories, with real GDP averaging nearly 5% a year over the past 15 years. After a slower 2.1% in 2025, activity rebounded strongly, growing 4.6% in January–September 2026 led by construction (+7.9% in August), and the Central Bank expects around 4.5% for the full year. Foreign direct investment reached a record $5.03 billion in 2025, up 11.3%, and a record $3.28 billion in the first half of 2026 (+7.7%), led by energy (27.8%) and tourism (20.1%), with more than $5.3 billion projected for the year. Exports rose 16.6% in H1 2026, tourism welcomed more than 9.2 million visitors in January–September, and Moody's upgrade to Ba2 in August 2025 recognised the country's improving credit profile. International reserves of about $15.2 billion (roughly five months of imports) provide a solid buffer.

The country's main structural challenge is the electricity distribution sector. The three state distribution companies lost 39.1% of the energy they purchased in H1 2026, and government transfers to them reached RD$68.7 billion in the period, weighing on public finances. Generation, however, is being strengthened rapidly: the 414 MW Manzanillo combined-cycle plant, financed through a $440 million syndicated facility and linked to the north by a new 345 kV line, came on stream in March 2026, energy is now the largest destination for FDI, and the share of circuits with 24-hour supply rose to 44.4% by July 2026. For investors, the practical response is well established — self-generation, private power-purchase agreements and free zone supply arrangements — while the push to modernise distribution and add renewable and gas-fired capacity offers a substantial pipeline of energy and grid opportunities.

How do I get in?

ProDominicana (Export and Investment Centre) acts as the one-stop shop for investment information, single-window FDI registration and investor aftercare

Foreign Investment Law 16-95 and Article 221 of the Constitution grant national treatment, with foreign investment permitted in almost all sectors and no general screening mechanism

Free Zone regime under Law 8-90, licensed by the National Free Zones Council (CNZFE), offering full exemption from taxes and duties on production and export activities; border-zone incentives extended to 30 years under Law 12-21

Tourism incentives under Law 158-01 (as amended), granting 15-year tax exemptions for qualifying new projects approved by CONFOTOUR

Renewable energy incentives under Law 57-07, including duty-free equipment imports and income-tax exemptions on qualifying power sales

Public-private partnership framework offering concessions of up to 40 years for infrastructure and energy projects

The Dominican peso is freely convertible under a managed float; profits and capital can be repatriated once the investment is registered, with remittances reported to the Central Bank within 60 days

Trade and market access

The Dominican Republic is a party to the Dominican Republic–Central America–United States Free Trade Agreement (CAFTA-DR), giving preferential access to the US and Central American markets, and is a member of CARIFORUM, through which it holds Economic Partnership Agreements with the European Union and the United Kingdom. It has been a WTO member since 1995 and is a party to the New York Convention on arbitral awards. Exports reached a record $15.9 billion in 2025 (+14.4%), with free zone exports of $8.5 billion, led by medical devices, tobacco products and electrical equipment, and gold exports rising sharply. The United States is the principal market for goods, tourists and remittances. Container ports at Haina and Caucedo, a growing LNG and port platform at Manzanillo and extensive air connectivity position the country as a logistics hub for the Caribbean and a natural nearshoring base for North American supply chains.

The overall risk picture

The Dominican Republic's risk profile is that of a stable, fast-growing economy on an improving credit trajectory with a strong record of attracting foreign capital. Record FDI in 2025 and H1 2026, a narrowing current account deficit (1.2% of GDP in 2025), reserves of about $15.2 billion and Moody's upgrade to Ba2 reflect sustained macroeconomic credibility, and the Central Bank's preventive rate rise in September 2026 shows a clear commitment to price stability. The principal structural issues — electricity distribution losses, a narrow tax base and slow administrative and judicial processes — are well understood and are being addressed through new generation capacity, digital investor services and institutional strengthening. External factors, including oil prices, US trade policy and the situation in Haiti, are manageable with standard planning: securing reliable power, reviewing tariff classifications and building robust contracts. Investors who structure carefully can take comfort from the depth of the country's incentive regimes and the consistency of its policy direction across successive governments.

Business networks

The American Chamber of Commerce of the Dominican Republic (AMCHAMDR) is the leading international business association, alongside the National Council of Private Enterprise (CONEP), the Dominican Association of Free Zones (ADOZONA) and the Dominican Exporters' Association (ADOEXPO), which are active in dialogue with government on trade and tariff issues. ProDominicana organises investor outreach and sector promotion, and the Central Bank maintains regular dialogue with the foreign-investor business association. Development finance is well established: the US International Development Finance Corporation opened a regional office in Santo Domingo in 2024 and has provided $245 million to Banco Popular and Banreservas for small-business lending, while the Inter-American Development Bank and World Bank Group support energy, infrastructure and private-sector programmes.

Who this market suits

The Dominican Republic suits a wide range of investors seeking exposure to a stable, high-growth Caribbean economy with privileged access to the US market: manufacturers and nearshoring investors, particularly in medical devices and electronics, using the free zone regime; hotel groups, developers and tourism-real-estate investors building on record visitor numbers and new destinations such as Cabo Rojo–Pedernales; energy investors in renewables, gas-fired generation and grid modernisation; and logistics investors serving Caribbean and Central American supply chains. Mining investors will also find established international partners, building on the strong growth of gold exports.

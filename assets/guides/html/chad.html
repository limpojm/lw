The economy today

Chad enters late 2026 with solid growth momentum and improving public finances. The World Bank reports GDP growth of 5.6% in 2025, well above the CEMAC average of 2.8%, with agriculture up 4.9%, services up 5% and oil output up 6.6%, while average inflation fell to -2.6% on stronger harvests. The budget deficit narrowed to 1.7% of GDP and public debt eased to 30.2% of GDP. In June 2026 the World Bank raised its 2026 growth forecast by 1.5 points to 5.2%, the largest upgrade in Africa, and first-quarter 2026 exports rose 29% on gold and antimony. Reforms are anchored by an IMF Extended Credit Facility of about $655 million approved in July 2025, and the Tchad Connexion 2030 plan has mobilised $20.5 billion in pledges and agreements, including 40 private-sector agreements worth $4.1 billion. UNCTAD puts the FDI stock at around $9.3 billion, although recorded annual inflows have been heavily revised and were modest in 2024–2025.

Chad's main structural challenge is its reliance on oil, which provides about 41% of government revenue and 76% of exports, leaving public finances sensitive to oil prices; the 2026 budget assumes oil revenue falls 22% to about 35% of total revenue. The government is responding with practical diversification: non-oil revenue rose 23.7% in 2025 on mandatory e-payment of taxes and customs reform, an oil revenue audit is being published under the IMF programme, and mining has been placed under a clearer state framework for critical minerals, with gold production up 25.6% and antimony up 49% in early 2026. Agriculture, which accounts for about 40% of GDP, and one of Africa's largest livestock herds offer a broad base for agro-processing. For investors, this transition creates entry points in mining, agribusiness, power and logistics, while a national oil strategy targeting 250,000 barrels a day by 2030 keeps the hydrocarbons sector open to experienced partners.

How do I get in?

Agence pour la Promotion des Investissements et des Exportations (ANIE), the national investment and export agency, with a one-stop shop (Guichet Unique) for company registration and online file tracking

National Investment Charter granting foreign investors equal treatment, protection against expropriation except in the public interest, and five-year tax exemptions for qualifying new industrial, mining, agricultural, forestry and real-estate activities

OHADA uniform business law and CEMAC membership, providing a harmonised commercial-law framework used across much of francophone Africa

CFA franc (XAF) issued by the regional central bank BEAC and pegged to the euro, with cross-border transfers governed by CEMAC foreign-exchange regulations through licensed banks

Tchad Connexion 2030 national development plan: 268 projects and reforms seeking $30 billion, with project and co-investment opportunities across energy, agriculture, mining, infrastructure and digital services

Mining entry through partnership with SONEMIC and Ministry-approved licences for critical minerals, under a 12.5% royalty paid via the E-tax digital platform

Oil-sector participation alongside the state oil company SHT, with a preferential tax regime for contractors and subcontractors on major oil projects

Trade and market access

Chad is a member of the Central African Economic and Monetary Community (CEMAC) and the Economic Community of Central African States (ECCAS), giving access to a regional market with a shared currency, the CFA franc, which is pegged to the euro. It has been a WTO member since 1996, and a GATT contracting party since 1963, and applies OHADA business law in common with much of francophone Africa. Crude oil, around three-quarters of exports, flows through the Chad–Cameroon pipeline to the Atlantic terminal at Kribi, while most other trade moves along the road corridor to the port of Douala via the Ngueli crossing. Gold, antimony, livestock, gum arabic and other agricultural products are a growing share of exports, and Tchad Connexion 2030 prioritises road, airport and trade-facilitation investment to reduce transit costs and strengthen Chad's role as a crossroads between Central, West and North-East Africa.

The overall risk picture

Chad's risk profile reflects an economy with strong recent growth, falling inflation, moderate public debt and an active IMF-supported reform programme, alongside the challenges of oil dependence, a landlocked position and a demanding regional security environment. Investors should note plainly that the 2023 takeover of former ExxonMobil oil assets and the 2026 centralisation of critical-mineral licensing show the state's intention to retain strong control over natural resources; agreements should therefore be built on clear ministerial approvals, international arbitration provisions and political-risk insurance. The IMF rates the risk of external debt distress as high, although debt fell to about 30% of GDP in 2025 and commercial borrowing has been reduced. The Sudan conflict and refugee inflows require careful site selection and security planning in the east. Against this, the $20.5 billion mobilised for Tchad Connexion 2030, strong multilateral backing and fast-growing gold, antimony and agricultural exports point to a widening opportunity set for well-advised, patient investors.

Business networks

Multilateral partners are the most active convening channels in Chad. The World Bank, IMF and African Development Bank support fiscal, agricultural and infrastructure programmes, while the Islamic Development Bank Group, through ICIEC, ICD and ITFC, offers blended finance, trade finance and risk cover and held an IsDB Day with the government in N'Djamena in January 2026. The UAE–Chad Trade and Investment Forum in Abu Dhabi, which launched Tchad Connexion 2030 in November 2025, the Chad–France economic forum scheduled in Paris for September 2026 and the SEMICA Tchad mines and hydrocarbons fair provide regular access to decision-makers. The EU-Africa Chamber of Commerce and the US Embassy, which has engaged SONEMIC on mining cooperation, are further useful channels for Western investors.

Who this market suits

Chad best suits investors with experience in frontier markets and a long-term horizon: mining companies and critical-minerals specialists prepared to partner with SONEMIC under the new licensing framework; agribusiness, livestock and food-processing investors building on a large agricultural base; energy and power developers responding to the plan's 90% urban electricity-access target; and logistics and construction firms positioned for donor- and state-financed infrastructure. Development finance institutions, Gulf investors and impact funds are well placed to lead, while experienced oil-service and upstream partners can engage alongside SHT with robust contractual protection.

The economy today

Niger is one of Africa's fastest-growing economies in 2026, driven by its emergence as a crude oil exporter. Real GDP grew an estimated 6.9% in 2025 and the IMF and World Bank both project 7.0% for 2026, with prices falling (inflation of -4.7% in 2025 and -1.9% expected in 2026). On 29 July 2026 the IMF completed the ninth review of Niger's Extended Credit Facility, noting that all end-December 2025 performance criteria were met, and in October 2026 IMF staff agreed a new programme of about $203 million, subject to Board approval. Public debt is about 45.6% of GDP, and the IMF projects foreign direct investment rising from CFAF 108 billion in 2025 to CFAF 177 billion in 2026. The May 2026 agreement with CNPC and its partners is a turning point: $1 billion of new upstream investment, a 45% state stake in the WAPCO pipeline and a fall in transport costs from $27 to $15 per barrel, with output targeted to rise to 145,000 barrels a day by 2029.

Security and the state's assertive approach to natural resources are the defining structural challenges. Armed groups remain active in Tillabéri, Diffa and Dosso, more than 1 million people are displaced, and Niamey itself saw attacks on its airport in January and June 2026 and a coup attempt that was put down on 29 August 2026. In parallel, the government has nationalised SOMAIR, reassigned the former Orano uranium permit and cancelled several gold concessions, while Orano's arbitration claims continue. The trend is towards negotiated outcomes rather than stand-off: the China oil settlement resolved a year of tensions with a balanced package, pipeline security has prevented further attacks, and Benin and Niger agreed a roadmap towards reopening their border in June 2026. For investors, the opportunity lies in partnership models aligned with national priorities in oil services, local content, power and agriculture, structured with strong contractual protections and security planning.

How do I get in?

Investment Code (Law No. 2014-09) offering promotional and conventional privileged regimes, equal treatment of foreign and national investors, guarantees against uncompensated expropriation and ICSID arbitration after amicable settlement

Single window for the Investment Code (Guichet Unique) under the industry ministry, with approvals due within 30 working days, alongside the Maison de l'Entreprise for company registration

Petroleum sector entry through partnerships with the state and CNPC-led operators, under contracts the government has begun to publish as part of its IMF transparency commitments

Mining Code requiring a minimum 10% free state stake, with the option for the state to acquire up to 33%; recent permits show the state favouring greater direct participation

WAEMU membership and the CFA franc, pegged to the euro under the BCEAO, with the Investment Code guaranteeing transfer of profits and liquidation proceeds subject to exchange regulations

OHADA uniform business law, with a national centre for business mediation and arbitration

Free zone regime provided for in the Investment Code for export-oriented enterprises, set by decree

Trade and market access

Niger is a member of the West African Economic and Monetary Union (WAEMU), sharing the euro-pegged CFA franc and a regional securities market with seven other states, and of the OHADA business-law area. It left ECOWAS in January 2025 and is a founding member of the Alliance of Sahel States with Burkina Faso and Mali, which has created a common passport, a confederal parliament and a Confederal Investment and Development Bank (BCID-AES); ECOWAS continues to apply its trade liberalisation scheme to AES members until further notice. Niger is a WTO member but lost AGOA preferences from January 2024. Crude oil, uranium and gold are the main exports, with crude moving by pipeline to the Beninese port of Sèmè-Podji and other trade passing through Nigeria and coastal corridors while the Benin road border remains closed.

The overall risk picture

Niger combines strong growth, falling prices and an IMF programme that remains on track with a security and political environment that investors must assess plainly. The country is led by General Abdourahamane Tiani's military government, which has ruled since July 2023 and put down a coup attempt in Niamey in August 2026. Niger is not subject to broad UN, US or EU economic sanctions, but there are practical compliance constraints: the EU maintains a targeted sanctions framework on Niger, renewed to 24 October 2027; the US has suspended entry of Niger nationals since January 2026, restricts most bilateral assistance and removed Niger from AGOA; and Niger has suspended visas for US citizens in response. The state's direct role in uranium, oil and gold means ownership terms and licence obligations should be agreed carefully and documented. With oil exports growing, a lower pipeline tariff, a stable CFA franc anchor and improving arrears management, investors who use sanctions and local legal counsel, security advisers and political-risk insurance can position for sectors where national priorities and private capital meet.

Regional central bank managing the CFA franc; held its policy rate at 3.00% in September 2026

Relevant for currency, transfers and banking-sector matters

Ministry of Economy and Finance

Key counterpart for fiscal incentives, payments and public-private partnerships

Essential counterparts for oil services, local content and logistics opportunities

SOPAMIN and Tsumco SA (state mining)

Key counterparts for uranium-sector partnerships, offtake and services

Confederal Investment and Development Bank (BCID-AES)

AES development bank launched in December 2025 to fund regional infrastructure

Relevant for regional infrastructure co-financing across Niger, Mali and Burkina Faso

Business networks

The Chamber of Commerce and Industry of Niger is the principal channel to domestic business, and the Maison de l'Entreprise supports company creation. The IMF, through its Extended Credit Facility, and the World Bank, which is supporting the banking sector and small businesses, remain active and are a useful source of macroeconomic and policy insight. CNPC and its partners, including WAPCO, are the main entry points for oil-sector subcontracting under the local-content commitments agreed in 2026. The WAEMU regional securities market and the BCID-AES are emerging channels for financing, and Niger's diplomatic missions and the AES institutions provide routes into government dialogue.

Who this market suits

Niger best suits experienced frontier-market investors with a high tolerance for security and political risk and a partnership-based approach: oilfield services, logistics and local-content suppliers serving an oil sector targeting 145,000 barrels a day by 2029; energy-minerals and mining-services firms willing to work alongside state companies; power, solar and irrigation developers linked to projects such as the Kandadji dam; and agribusiness, telecoms and financial-services investors serving a young and fast-growing population. Investors should be comfortable with state participation, rigorous sanctions and counterparty screening, and with planning for evolving regional and security conditions.

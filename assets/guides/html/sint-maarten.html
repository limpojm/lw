Investor Entry Routes

  • Ministry of Tourism, Economic Affairs, Transport and Telecommunication (TEATT) handles business licensing and investment policy, and in August 2026 commissioned the framework for a proposed Sint Maarten Investment Promotion Agency with one-stop investor services
  • Investment Policy and Diversification Strategy (2022) prioritising niche tourism, the marine industry, agriculture technology, an airport-city logistics hub and medical tourism
  • Tax investment deduction of 8% (12% for real estate other than land) on qualifying investments above Cg 5,000, with ten-year loss carry-forward; a Penshonado regime taxes qualifying foreign income of new residents at 10%
  • Caribbean guilder (Cg), introduced on 31 March 2025 and fixed at Cg 1.79 = $1.00, managed by the Centrale Bank van Curaçao en Sint Maarten (CBCS), which also licenses and supervises financial institutions; the US dollar is widely used
  • Sint Maarten Reconstruction, Recovery and Resilience Trust Fund, financed by the Netherlands and managed by the World Bank until December 2028, procuring contractors and consultants for infrastructure, health and resilience projects
  • Partnership and concession routes with state-linked companies such as Port St. Maarten and the Princess Juliana International Airport operating company for port, aviation and logistics projects
  • Company registration through the Chamber of Commerce, with ultimate beneficial owner (UBO) reporting now required for legal entities

Market-Entry Observation

Sint Maarten enters late 2026 with steady growth and improving public finances. The CBCS estimates real GDP growth of 3.4% in 2025 and projects 2.4% for 2026, driven by tourism, construction, transport and trade. Cruise arrivals rose 16% to 1,597,940 in 2025, close to their pre-Irma peak, while the airport recorded 855,994 arriving passengers and around 1.8 million passengers in total. The IMF projects government debt to fall from 44.2% of GDP in 2024 to 40.4% in 2025 and 38.4% in 2026, with inflation of 3.6% in 2024 expected to ease towards 2%. At monetary-union level, the current account deficit narrowed from 16.4% to 9.2% of GDP in 2025, and reserves covered 5.5 months of imports at end-May 2026, comfortably supporting the dollar peg, with the CBCS pledging rate at 4.25%.

Sint Maarten's main structural challenge is its exposure to Atlantic hurricanes in a tourism-centred economy: Hurricane Irma in 2017 caused damage estimated at 129% of GDP. The country has since rebuilt with resilience in mind, supported by €550 million set aside by the Netherlands, most of it channelled through the World Bank-managed Trust Fund running to December 2028. The rebuilt airport terminal was recommissioned in November 2024, and the new Category 5-resilient general hospital reached topping out in March 2026, while the existing hospital kept operating through Hurricane Erin in August 2025. The IMF's priority is to shift investment from Trust Fund-led projects to private investment, faster permitting and higher-value tourism, creating openings in resilient construction, renewable energy, marine services and upscale hospitality for investors who build resilience into project design.

Investor Risk Note

Sint Maarten's risk profile is that of a stable, dollar-pegged island economy within the Kingdom of the Netherlands, with growing tourism, declining public debt and the institutional backing of Kingdom financial supervision and a shared central bank. The main considerations are exposure to hurricanes, reliance on tourism, permitting delays and continued work on the AML/CFT framework following the 2025 mutual evaluation. Each is being addressed: post-Irma reconstruction has delivered a rebuilt airport and a hurricane-resilient hospital, the government is developing an investment promotion agency and a diversification strategy, and beneficial-ownership reporting is now in force. Investors should plan for hurricane-resilient design and insurance, allow time for permits, and apply strong compliance standards. With cruise traffic close to pre-Irma levels, new luxury resorts in the pipeline and significant reconstruction investment still to be deployed before 2028, the outlook remains constructive.

Market Access

Sint Maarten is an autonomous constituent country of the Kingdom of the Netherlands. It is not part of the EU but is associated with it as an Overseas Country and Territory (OCT), which gives it preferential access to the EU market and eligibility for EU cooperation funding, and its nationals hold Dutch and therefore EU citizenship. It participates in the WTO through the Kingdom of the Netherlands and shares a monetary union and central bank with Curaçao. The open border with French Saint-Martin creates a single island market, and Princess Juliana International Airport is the main air gateway for the surrounding Leeward Islands. Services are the main export, led by tourism, with North America supplying about two-thirds of stay-over visitors and Europe about a quarter. Port St. Maarten's cruise and cargo facilities anchor the island's role as a regional hub for visitors, distribution and yachting.

Current International Business Channels

The Chamber of Commerce and Industry is the business registry and main liaison body for the private sector, and TEATT is consulting businesses and investors in 2026 on the design of the planned investment promotion agency. The World Bank-managed Trust Fund publishes tenders and results and is a practical route into reconstruction and resilience projects until 2028. Kingdom channels, including the Dutch government and the Cft, shape larger public projects, while Sint Maarten's OCT status gives access to EU cooperation programmes, including funding for culture and the creative economy. The IMF's Article IV reviews, conducted jointly with Curaçao, and the CBCS economic bulletins provide independent references on policy direction.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Sint Maarten suits investors seeking a dollar-pegged jurisdiction within the Kingdom of the Netherlands with a strong tourism franchise: upscale hotel, resort and residential developers serving growing North American and European demand; cruise, aviation and logistics businesses building on a busy port and rebuilt airport; construction, engineering and consultancy firms able to deliver hurricane-resilient infrastructure and Trust Fund contracts; and renewable-energy, storage and efficiency investors supporting the power transition. Marine and yachting businesses, healthcare and medical-tourism operators, and agri-tech ventures aligned with the diversification strategy can also find attractive niches, provided they build in strong compliance and resilience planning.

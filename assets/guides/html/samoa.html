The economy today

Samoa offers investors one of the strongest macroeconomic positions in the Pacific: public debt of just 18.1% of GDP in 2025/26, budget surpluses in each of the last two years and foreign reserves of about SAT$1.7 billion, equal to 15.7 months of imports in June 2026. Growth reached 4.2% in 2024/25, helped by hosting the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting in 2024, and inflation was only 1.0% in May 2026. Tourism and remittances are the twin pillars of the external accounts: visitor earnings rose 5.6% to SAT$377.2 million in July to December 2025, while remittances, led by Australia, New Zealand and the United States, reached SAT$478.1 million. Political stability improved after the August 2025 election gave the FAST party a clear majority under Prime Minister La'auli Leuatea Schmidt.

Samoa's main structural challenge is lifting growth in a small, remote economy with a narrow production base. The IMF estimates growth slowed to 0.4% in 2025/26 as consumption, private investment and agriculture weakened and higher oil prices took hold, and it projects a recovery to 2.0% in 2026/27, with inflation rising to around 4.5%. Labour shortages linked to migration to New Zealand and Australia and an ageing power network add to costs. The response is well funded: the 2026–27 budget of about SAT$1.5 billion raises development spending to SAT$215.9 million and sets aside SAT$100 million to secure fuel and power supply, while ADB grants for the Apia port and Vaisigano dam and new fisheries governance create openings in energy, infrastructure, agro-processing and tourism.

How do I get in?

A Foreign Investment Certificate from the Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Labour under the Foreign Investment Act 2000, due within five working days and required before a business licence

100% foreign ownership is allowed in most sectors; a short reserved list (including retail, buses, taxis and car rental) is kept for citizens, and fishing and some coconut and nonu processing are restricted

Priority sectors named by government include tourism, agro-processing, fisheries, ICT and business-process outsourcing, light manufacturing, renewable energy and creative industries

A 27% corporate income tax, with Samoa removed from the EU list of non-cooperative tax jurisdictions in February 2026

The tala is managed by the Central Bank of Samoa under a basket peg, which the IMF describes as an appropriate anchor; confirm repatriation procedures with the bank and commercial banks

Leases of customary land, which makes up about 80% of land, typically for 30 years with renewal; freehold purchases by foreigners require the approval of the Head of State

Trade and market access

Samoa joined the WTO in 2012 and is a founding party to the Pacific Island Countries Trade Agreement (PICTA) and to PACER Plus, the regional trade and development agreement with Australia and New Zealand, which entered into force in December 2020. It acceded to the EU–Pacific interim Economic Partnership Agreement in 2018, giving duty-free, quota-free access to the EU, and its accession to the UK–Pacific agreement is pending. Older preference schemes such as SPARTECA and the US Generalised System of Preferences also apply. New Zealand, Australia and the United States are the main sources of visitors and remittances, and Faleolo International Airport and Apia port connect Samoa to these markets and to neighbouring American Samoa.

The overall risk picture

Samoa's risk profile is anchored by very low public debt, reserves of 15.7 months of imports, a basket-pegged currency endorsed by the IMF and a government with a clear parliamentary majority since the August 2025 election. The main near-term issues are a slowdown to 0.4% growth in 2025/26 and rising fuel-driven inflation, both being addressed through a larger 2026–27 budget, fuel and power support and continued grant financing from the ADB and partners. Labour shortages, customary land rules and infrastructure bottlenecks are structural constraints that investors can manage with leases, training and careful logistics planning. Correspondent-banking pressures affect the region, although the IMF finds Samoa's remittance corridors present limited threats, and Samoa left the EU's tax non-cooperative list in February 2026. There are no international sanctions restricting investment in Samoa.

Business networks

The Samoa Chamber of Commerce and Industry is the main private-sector body and a channel for dialogue with government, alongside sector groups in tourism and manufacturing. The Asian Development Bank, with seven grants worth $232 million in its current Samoa portfolio, and the World Bank fund infrastructure, energy and resilience projects, while ADB private-sector finance has supported solar generation on Upolu. New Zealand, Australia, Japan, China and the EU are major development partners, and their missions in Apia provide useful introductions. Strong diaspora networks in New Zealand, Australia and the United States are a further channel for tourism, trade and investment links.

Who this market suits

Samoa suits investors seeking a stable, low-debt Pacific base: resort and eco-tourism investors targeting visitors from New Zealand, Australia and the United States; agro-processors in coconut, nonu, cocoa and taro, and fishing and aquaculture investors working with the new fisheries ministry; renewable energy and water investors aligned with ADB-backed projects; and light manufacturers and business-process outsourcing firms drawing on an English-speaking workforce and time-zone links to Australasia and the United States.

Investor Entry Routes

  • Foreign Investment Law (Law 118 of 2014), administered by the Ministry of Foreign Trade and Foreign Investment (MINCEX), with joint ventures and contracts with state entities as the established routes
  • June 2026 reform package of 176 measures, implemented from September 2026: private Cuban companies may partner directly with foreign investors, hold overseas bank accounts and import and export without state intermediaries
  • Mariel Special Development Zone (Decree-Law 313 of 2013), offering tax exemptions and dedicated port, rail and utility infrastructure
  • Long-term usufruct rights for real estate, industrial and agricultural projects, alongside new scope for foreigners to invest in Cuban real estate
  • Sectors opened to private participation in July 2026, including fuel stations, pharmacies, ports and banks, and removal of the requirement to hire Cuban staff through state employment agencies
  • Currency and repatriation: the peso trades at multiple rates (around 700 to the dollar informally in September 2026) and hard-currency availability is limited, so offshore revenue and payment structures are central to project design
  • Compliance-led entry: all structures need screening under the US CACR, the Cuba Restricted List and the 2026 Cuba Sanctions Regulations, and EU operators must also observe the EU Blocking Regulation

Market-Entry Observation

Cuba in October 2026 is undertaking its most far-reaching economic opening in decades. President Miguel Díaz-Canel announced 176 pro-market reforms on 12 June 2026, Parliament approved them swiftly, and implementing decrees began to take effect in early September: private companies, including firms with more than 100 employees, can now partner directly with foreign investors, hold accounts abroad and trade internationally without state intermediaries, while banks, pharmacies, fuel stations and ports have been opened to private participation and long-term usufruct rights support real-estate, industrial and agricultural projects. These changes build on a private sector of more than 15,000 small and medium-sized enterprises that already employ over a third of the workforce. The macroeconomic starting point is challenging: ECLAC estimates GDP contracted 3.8% in 2025 and projects a further 6.5% decline in 2026, and official year-on-year inflation stood at 25.2% in August 2026, giving early movers the opportunity to enter at a low valuation base.

Cuba's main structural challenge is the combination of an energy and foreign-exchange shortage with a tightening US sanctions environment. Fuel shortages since the January 2026 US measures against oil suppliers have caused repeated nationwide blackouts, the most recent on 18 September, and international visitor arrivals fell 64.4% to 450,353 in January–August 2026 as Canadian airlines suspended flights. In parallel, Executive Order 14404 of 1 May 2026 introduced the first US secondary sanctions on Cuba, the Cuba Sanctions Regulations took effect on 30 September 2026, and the US Supreme Court's 2026 rulings have strengthened Title III property claims. The government's response is to widen the role of private and foreign capital, which creates real openings in agri-food, consumer distribution and light manufacturing for non-US investors operating lawfully, while positioning prepared investors for any future change in US policy.

Investor Risk Note

Cuba combines a genuine and accelerating domestic opening with one of the most restrictive external legal environments of any market. The 2026 reforms allow foreign investors to work directly with a dynamic private sector, use long-term usufruct and access previously closed sectors, and the scale of the current downturn means assets and partnerships can be secured at a low base. Investors must, however, treat the legal position as the starting point: the US embargo under the CACR continues to bar most US involvement; Executive Order 14404 and the Cuba Sanctions Regulations now expose non-US companies and banks to secondary sanctions in key sectors; the Cuba Restricted List and State Sponsor of Terrorism designation drive bank de-risking; and Title III litigation risk has risen after the 2026 Supreme Court rulings. EU and UK operators must also reconcile US rules with blocking legislation that prohibits compliance with listed US measures. The prudent approach is to take specialist sanctions counsel in every relevant jurisdiction, confine activity to lawful, non-designated sectors and screened private-sector partners, and use this period to prepare well-documented plans for any future easing of US policy. Nothing in this guide suggests ways to avoid sanctions.

Market Access

Cuba is a founding member of the WTO and belongs to the Latin American Integration Association (ALADI) and the ALBA-TCP alliance, has a trade and economic cooperation agreement with CARICOM, and has a Political Dialogue and Cooperation Agreement with the European Union. Its principal exports include nickel and cobalt, tobacco and cigars, rum, sugar, pharmaceuticals and professional services, although overseas medical missions and nickel interests have been targeted by US designations in 2026. The Mariel container terminal, with initial capacity of 800,000 TEUs expandable to 3 million and dredged for Neo-Panamax vessels in 2023, gives Cuba a deep-water Caribbean logistics platform, although its US designation in July 2026 requires careful screening by shippers and lenders. Proximity to the US market remains Cuba's largest latent advantage, unavailable under current US law but central to any future normalisation scenario.

Current International Business Channels

The Cámara de Comercio de la República de Cuba and MINCEX are the principal official channels, and the annual Havana International Fair (FIHAV) has traditionally been the main venue for trade and investment introductions. The commercial sections of Spanish, Canadian, Mexican, Chinese and other EU embassies, together with bilateral business associations, remain the best sources of practical intelligence on doing business under the current reforms and sanctions regime. Cuba is not a member of the IMF or World Bank, so multilateral development finance is limited, and investors should rely instead on bilateral export-credit support where available. Specialist law firms in the US, EU, UK and Canada have issued detailed guidance on the 2026 US measures and are an essential part of any engagement network.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Cuba currently suits experienced, patient non-US investors with strong compliance functions and no material US nexus: agri-food and food-processing investors able to use the new long-term land usufruct; consumer-goods, distribution and light-manufacturing investors partnering with the expanding private sector; and diaspora and Latin American, European and Canadian investors with established Cuban relationships. Investors in energy, mining, finance or tourism assets should recognise the heightened US secondary-sanctions and Title III exposure in those areas, while US investors should limit activity to what OFAC licences permit and focus on preparing for any future change in policy.

STEP 3: Secure payment and banking channels earlyConfirm with your banks which Cuba-related flows they will process, given the end of US U-turn authorisations and wider de-risking.
STEP 4: Prioritise lawful private-sector partnershipsUse the September 2026 reforms to partner directly with screened private enterprises in non-designated sectors such as agri-food, consumer goods and light manufacturing, with offshore revenue structures and on-site power.
STEP 5: Prepare for policy changeMonitor US and Cuban policy developments closely and keep documented scenario plans so you can act quickly and lawfully if US restrictions are eased.

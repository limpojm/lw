The economy today

Cyprus in November 2026 is one of the euro area's best-performing economies, combining strong growth, the EU's largest budget surplus and rapidly falling debt. GDP grew 3.8% in 2025 and 3.3% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, led by trade, information and communication, financial services and construction. The government recorded a surplus of 3.6% of GDP in 2025, and public debt fell to 48.5% of GDP by mid-2026, the largest quarterly decline in the EU. S&P raised Cyprus to an A rating with a positive outlook in September 2026, its first A grade since the financial crisis. Gross FDI inflows reached €8.5 billion in 2024, placing Cyprus among the world's top ten for FDI per capita, and the government reports a further rise of about 10% in 2025, with €2.6 billion of 2024 inflows directed to ICT. A major tax reform effective January 2026 has modernised the system while keeping it competitive.

Cyprus's main structural challenge is energy: it is the only EU member state without an electricity connection to another country, and consumer price inflation rose to 4.37% in October 2026, driven by transport and housing and utility costs. The response is a multi-track programme that creates sizeable opportunities. The Cronos gas field, developed by Eni and TotalEnergies via Egypt's Zohr infrastructure, targets first gas by end-2027 or the first half of 2028, and Chevron's Aphrodite project is in front-end engineering with a decision expected in 2027. A tender is under way to complete the Vasiliko LNG import terminal, and the €1.9 billion Great Sea Interconnector to Greece, supported by €658 million of EU funding, remains a strategic priority pending a cost review due by the end of 2026. Together with strong public finances, these projects open opportunities in energy infrastructure, renewables and storage.

Trade and market access

As an EU and euro-area member, Cyprus gives investors tariff-free access to the EU single market and to all the EU's free trade agreements, with WTO membership and the common trade policy governing trade with third countries. Cyprus is not yet in the Schengen area, which it now expects to join in 2027. Its location at the meeting point of Europe, the Middle East and North Africa, its ports at Limassol and Larnaca and its two international airports make it a regional base for shipping, services and headquarters. In energy, gas from the Cronos and Aphrodite fields is planned to flow through Egypt to regional and European markets, and the Great Sea Interconnector would link Cyprus to the European grid via Greece.

The overall risk picture

Cyprus's risk profile is that of a stable, fast-growing euro-area member with exceptional public finances: a 3.6% of GDP surplus in 2025, debt falling to 48.5% of GDP by mid-2026 and an A rating from S&P. The May 2026 parliamentary election returned the Democratic Rally as the largest party, with gains for ELAM and two new parties, but within a presidential system that keeps the executive stable. The main issues are energy costs and inflation, and the timing of gas and interconnection projects. Investors should note two legal realities: the government does not exercise effective control over the north of the island, where EU law is suspended, and some Cyprus-registered companies are subject to US, UK or EU Russia-related sanctions. Cyprus has tightened enforcement, including a dedicated sanctions unit and criminal penalties for breaches, so thorough counterparty screening is essential and positions the island well as a compliant international business centre.

Business networks

The Cyprus Chamber of Commerce and Industry, the Employers and Industrialists Federation, the American Chamber of Commerce in Cyprus and bilateral chambers such as the Cyprus-British Chamber of Commerce are the main business networks. Invest Cyprus organises investor events and promotes the country at international forums, and the government's partnerships with NVIDIA, Columbia University and Plug and Play support the technology ecosystem. The European Investment Bank and the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development have co-financed energy infrastructure, and EU funds support energy, digital and innovation projects. The commercial sections of EU partners' embassies and the UK High Commission are useful sources of introductions.

Who this market suits

Cyprus suits investors seeking an internationally oriented base inside the EU and the euro area: shipping and ship-management groups in Limassol; fund managers, fintech and professional-services firms; technology companies relocating teams or regional headquarters; energy and infrastructure investors targeting gas, LNG, renewables, storage and interconnection projects; and tourism and real-estate investors. All investors should apply rigorous sanctions and anti-money-laundering screening to counterparties.

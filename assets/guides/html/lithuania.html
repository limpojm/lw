The economy today

Lithuania in November 2026 combines euro-area membership and low public debt with one of the most dynamic fintech and defence-industrial sectors in Europe. The European Commission estimates growth of 2.9% in 2025 and forecasts 3.0% for 2026; output rose 4.1% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, and the flash estimate for the third quarter showed 1.8% annual growth despite a quarterly dip in construction and transport. The stock of foreign direct investment reached €43.8 billion at the end of 2025, 52.1% of GDP, led by investors from Germany, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, Estonia and Sweden. Invest Lithuania confirmed 31 projects in 2025 expected to create 2,139 jobs, with recent investors including Robinhood, Checkout.com, Rheinmetall and Pentasweet, whose €65 million facility adds to a growing life-sciences cluster. A new Social Democrat-led government under Prime Minister Mindaugas Sinkevičius took office in July 2026.

Lithuania's main structural challenge is financing a rapid rise in defence spending while keeping inflation and public finances on a sound footing. The 2026 budget allocates €4.79 billion, 5.38% of GDP, to defence, among the highest shares in NATO, funded partly by tax changes that raised corporate income tax to 17%. Inflation rose to 6.7% in September 2026, driven by transport and energy costs, and the ECB raised its deposit rate to 2.50%, where it was held on 29 October 2026. The starting position is strong: the Commission puts the 2025 deficit at 1.8% of GDP and debt at 39.5%, rising to 44.6% in 2026, and EU rules allow added defence spending within agreed limits. Defence investment is itself an opportunity, with Rheinmetall building a plant at Baisogala and talks continuing on a second facility worth more than €400 million.

How do I get in?

Invest Lithuania, the national investment agency under the Ministry of Economy and Innovation, provides free facilitation, site selection and incentive guidance; it confirmed 31 FDI projects in 2025

Seven free economic zones (Kaunas, Klaipėda, Akmenė, Kėdainiai, Marijampolė, Panevėžys and Šiauliai) offering no corporate income tax for 10 years, then a 50% reduction for 6 years, for qualifying investments

Large investment project status, with up to 20 years of corporate tax exemption for qualifying manufacturing and data projects and 'green corridor' fast-track permitting under an agreement with the government

Corporate income tax of 17% from January 2026 (7% for small companies and for qualifying patent and software income), with a triple deduction for R&D costs

Euro-area membership since 2015: the euro is the national currency, with free movement of capital under EU rules

EU single-market, Schengen and NATO membership, giving free movement of goods, services, capital and people across the EU

Bank of Lithuania licensing for payment, e-money and crypto-asset firms, used by fintechs as an EU-wide gateway

Trade and market access

As an EU member, Lithuania gives investors tariff-free access to the EU single market and to all the EU's free trade agreements, with WTO membership and the EU's common trade policy governing trade with third countries. Euro membership since 2015 removes exchange-rate costs with euro-area partners, and Schengen membership allows passport-free travel. Klaipėda, Lithuania's ice-free Baltic Sea port, links Lithuania with Scandinavia and global shipping, and its LNG terminal supplies the wider Baltic region. Rail Baltica, the standard-gauge railway connecting the Baltic states to Poland and Western Europe, is under construction. Lithuania's largest trading partners are its EU neighbours, while trade with Russia and Belarus is heavily restricted by EU sanctions.

The overall risk picture

Lithuania's risk profile is that of a stable EU, euro-area and NATO member with low debt, strong institutions and a clear pro-investment policy. Debt of 39.5% of GDP in 2025 and a deficit of 1.8% leave room to finance record defence spending of 5.38% of GDP in 2026, though debt is forecast to rise. Inflation of 6.7% in September 2026 and higher ECB rates are the main near-term cost pressures, and the 2026 tax reform raised corporate income tax to 17%. Lithuania borders Belarus and Russia's Kaliningrad region, both subject to comprehensive EU sanctions; investors should screen any dealings with those markets with sanctions counsel. Allied troop deployments and rising defence investment have strengthened security and created new industrial opportunities. The June 2026 government change kept the Social Democrats in charge, with continuity on core economic and foreign policy.

Business networks

The American Chamber of Commerce in Lithuania, the British Chamber of Commerce in Lithuania, the German-Baltic Chamber of Commerce (AHK) and the Association of Lithuanian Chambers of Commerce, Industry and Crafts are the main business networks, alongside the Lithuanian Confederation of Industrialists. Invest Lithuania runs investor events and sector programmes in fintech, life sciences, defence and advanced manufacturing, and connects foreign firms with local partners and suppliers. The European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, the European Investment Bank and the Nordic Investment Bank are active in energy, infrastructure and private-sector finance. Embassies of major EU partners, the United Kingdom and the United States maintain commercial sections that provide introductions.

Who this market suits

Lithuania suits investors seeking a stable euro-area base on NATO's eastern flank: fintech and payments companies using Bank of Lithuania licences as an EU gateway; software, AI and business-services firms drawing on talent in Vilnius and Kaunas; defence and dual-use manufacturers supplying the national programme and Rheinmetall's local supply chain; advanced manufacturers and life-sciences companies using free economic zones; and logistics and energy investors around Klaipėda port and Rail Baltica. Companies with any exposure to Russia or Belarus should take sanctions advice first.

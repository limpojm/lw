Investor Entry Routes

  • General Authority for Investment and Free Zones (GAFI), reporting to the Prime Minister, offers one-stop company set-up and licensing through its Investors Services Center and branches nationwide
  • Investment Law No. 72 of 2017, guaranteeing protection against nationalisation and expropriation, allowing 100% foreign ownership of most projects and the right to remit income and repatriate capital
  • Golden License: a single Cabinet-approved permit covering set-up, operation, land allocation and building licences for strategic, national and public-private partnership projects
  • Suez Canal Economic Zone (SCZone) offering 100% foreign ownership, customs and sales-tax exemptions on imports, fast-track visas and a one-stop shop; plus nine public free zones and 13 investment zones
  • Central Bank of Egypt oversight of a flexible, market-determined exchange rate since March 2024, which has restored foreign-currency availability for profit repatriation
  • Sovereign Fund of Egypt and the State Ownership Policy, opening co-investment and divestment opportunities in state-owned assets
  • Cabinet-approved Economic Entities Platform, a single digital window covering 468 economic activities, with a new national FDI strategy targeting 12 priority sectors

Market-Entry Observation

Egypt in October 2026 is delivering its strongest growth in several years on the back of a sustained reform programme. GDP expanded 5.1% in FY2025/26, up from 4.4% the year before, led by non-petroleum manufacturing (+9%), telecommunications and a 23.3% rebound in Suez Canal activity, and the government targets 5.2–5.4% for the current fiscal year. Egypt was Africa's top FDI destination for the fourth consecutive year in 2025, attracting around $15 billion; excluding the one-off Ras El Hekma deal, inflows rose about 25%. Net international reserves reached a record $57.35 billion in September 2026, urban inflation eased to 13.9%, and the central bank's policy rate stands at 19% after cumulative easing. The IMF-supported programme, extended to end-2026, has anchored a flexible exchange rate and a primary surplus of 4.8% of GDP, while the State Ownership Policy is opening state assets to private investors.

Egypt's main structural challenge is its external financing need, which leaves the economy exposed to regional shocks. The Iran war from February 2026 triggered portfolio outflows of around $10 billion and briefly interrupted gas imports, yet the economy absorbed the shock: the pound adjusted under a market-driven regime, Israeli gas deliveries resumed in April, and reserves have risen for 49 consecutive months. External debt stood at $164.8 billion at end-March 2026 but fell to 38.9% of GDP from 44.2%, while debt-service payments eased. Strong diversified foreign-currency earnings provide the buffer, with remittances up 28.1% to $29.7 billion in January–July 2026 and Suez Canal revenue projected to approach $10 billion by FY2027/28. For investors, the opportunity lies in export-oriented manufacturing, logistics and energy projects that earn hard currency and benefit directly from Egypt's competitive cost base.

Investor Risk Note

Egypt's risk profile reflects a large, reforming economy that has demonstrated its ability to absorb successive external shocks. The 2024 shift to a flexible exchange rate, the IMF-supported programme and the Ras El Hekma inflow resolved the foreign-currency shortages of 2022–2024, and record reserves of $57.35 billion, a banking-sector net foreign asset position of $31.2 billion and 49 consecutive months of reserve growth provide a meaningful cushion. The 2026 Iran war tested these buffers through capital outflows, a temporary gas-supply interruption and pressure on canal traffic, yet growth reached 5.1% in FY2025/26 and inflation continued to ease. Public debt and gross financing needs remain the key variable to monitor, alongside the pace of state-asset divestment as the current IMF arrangement concludes in December 2026. Investors earning hard currency, or structuring with development-finance partners, are best placed to capture Egypt's growth while managing currency and regional exposure.

Market Access

Egypt has been a WTO member since 1995 and offers preferential access to markets across three continents. Its Association Agreement with the EU, in force since 2004, provides tariff-free trade in industrial goods with Egypt's largest trading partner, which accounted for 24.6% of its total trade (€32.3 billion) in 2025. Egypt is a member of COMESA, the Greater Arab Free Trade Area and the Agadir Agreement, ratified the African Continental Free Trade Area in 2019, and has free trade agreements with Mercosur and Turkey, while Qualifying Industrial Zones give duty-free access to the US market. The Suez Canal and SCZone ports position Egypt as a manufacturing and transshipment platform between Europe, the Gulf, Asia and Africa, with exports rising to $31.9 billion in January–July 2026.

Current International Business Channels

The American Chamber of Commerce in Egypt, the Federation of Egyptian Industries and the Federation of Egyptian Chambers of Commerce are the main business associations, alongside bilateral chambers including the Egyptian British Chamber of Commerce. GAFI and the Ministry of Investment and Foreign Trade run regular investor events, and sector gatherings such as the Egypt Energy Show (EGYPES) attract international participants. The EBRD, World Bank Group and African Development Bank maintain active programmes in Egypt supporting private-sector development, energy transition and infrastructure, and the IMF's Resilience and Sustainability Facility has advanced renewable-energy and climate-finance reforms. Gulf sovereign and commercial investors, highly active since Ras El Hekma, are useful co-investment partners.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Egypt suits investors seeking scale, a competitive cost base and a regional export platform: manufacturers and logistics operators using SCZone and free-zone incentives to serve Europe, Africa and the Gulf; renewable-energy developers responding to strong power demand and an energy-transition agenda; tourism and real-estate investors building on record visitor revenues and North Coast development; and ICT, fintech and pharmaceutical investors targeting a large domestic market. Private-equity and strategic investors can access state-asset divestments through the Sovereign Fund of Egypt. Projects with hard-currency revenue or DFI co-financing are best positioned to manage currency exposure.

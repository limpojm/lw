The economy today

Switzerland offers investors political stability, a strong currency, low taxes and world-class research and financial infrastructure. Growth has surprised on the upside in 2026: in September the State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO) raised its 2026 GDP forecast to 1.7% from 0.9%, with 1.6% expected in 2027, and unemployment forecast at 3.1%. Inflation remains among the lowest in Europe at 1.5% in October 2026, and the Swiss National Bank held its policy rate at 0% in September. The stock of foreign direct investment in Switzerland was CHF 926 billion at the end of 2024. Eight cantons cut their corporate tax rates in 2026, and in June 2026 voters rejected an initiative to cap the population at 10 million, preserving the current immigration and labour-market framework.

Switzerland's main structural challenge is securing its trading relationships with its two largest partners, the EU and the United States. The EU treaty package, signed in Brussels on 2 March 2026, updates access to the single market and adds new agreements on health, food safety and electricity; parliament is still debating it, and one or more referendums are expected in 2027 or 2028. With the United States, a November 2025 framework cut tariffs on Swiss goods from 39% to 15%; after the US Supreme Court's February 2026 ruling on emergency tariffs, a 10% general duty and up to 15% on pharmaceuticals applied, and talks on a binding agreement continue. Switzerland's resilience is considerable: federal finances recorded a CHF 0.3 billion surplus in 2025 under the debt brake, the franc is a global safe haven, and diversified FTAs open alternative markets.

How do I get in?

Switzerland Global Enterprise, the national promotion agency, works with cantonal economic development agencies, which handle sites, permits and incentives directly with investors

Competitive cantonal taxation: maximum effective corporate tax rates range from about 11.7% in Lucerne and Zug to around 20% in Zurich and Bern, with patent boxes available; the OECD 15% minimum tax applies to large multinational groups

No general foreign-investment screening regime for most sectors, with sector-specific rules in areas such as banking, real estate and broadcasting; investors should check the status of proposed screening legislation

A freely convertible Swiss franc and no capital controls, with free repatriation of profits and capital

Swiss Exchange (SIX) listings and access to one of the world's deepest pools of private and institutional capital

Access to EU markets under the bilateral agreements, and to over 30 free trade partners through EFTA and Swiss agreements, including the EFTA–India agreement

Trade and market access

Switzerland is not a member of the EU or the EEA, but a network of bilateral agreements gives Swiss-based companies wide access to the EU single market, which remains its main trading partner; the EU treaty package signed in March 2026 is designed to update and secure that access. As a member of EFTA and the WTO, Switzerland has a large network of free trade agreements, including the EFTA–India Trade and Economic Partnership Agreement, and an EFTA–Mercosur agreement awaits parliamentary approval. The United States is a key market, especially for pharmaceuticals, and Swiss companies have pledged $200 billion of US investment by the end of 2028 under the November 2025 framework. Switzerland is also a member of the Schengen area, and the Gotthard and Lötschberg rail tunnels connect northern and southern Europe.

The overall risk picture

Switzerland is one of the world's most stable and creditworthy investment destinations, with low public debt, a federal surplus in 2025, inflation of 1.5% and a direct democracy that gives policy a high degree of predictability. The main issues for investors are external: US trade policy, where Switzerland has a non-binding framework deal and talks continue on a binding agreement, and the EU treaty package, which still faces parliamentary approval and referendums. Investors should also note that Switzerland generally applies EU sanctions against Russia, and that the OECD minimum tax affects large groups. Switzerland is a FATF member and is not on the FATF list of jurisdictions under increased monitoring. These factors are manageable with good advice, and the franc, deep capital markets and strong institutions give investors exceptional resilience.

Business networks

Economiesuisse, the main federation of Swiss business, and sector bodies such as scienceindustries for chemicals and pharmaceuticals and Swiss Medtech are influential voices in policy debates on trade and tax. Bilateral chambers including the Swiss-American Chamber of Commerce, the British-Swiss Chamber of Commerce and the Swiss Chambers of Commerce network provide introductions and market intelligence. Cantonal economic development agencies, regional promotion bodies such as Greater Zurich Area and Greater Geneva Bern area, and Switzerland Global Enterprise organise investor programmes. Universities such as ETH Zurich and EPFL, and Innosuisse, the innovation agency, are important partners for research collaboration.

Who this market suits

Switzerland suits investors seeking a stable, low-tax base for high-value activity: regional headquarters and holding companies in Zug, Lucerne and Zurich; life-sciences, medtech and chemical companies in Basel and the Lake Geneva region; financial-services, wealth-management, fintech and commodity-trading firms in Zurich and Geneva; and research-intensive technology and AI companies working with ETH Zurich and EPFL. Exporters to the United States and the EU should plan for evolving trade arrangements on both fronts.

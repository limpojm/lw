The economy today

Canada in November 2026 offers investors political stability, resource wealth and a determined push to build national infrastructure. Foreign direct investment reached C$96.8 billion in 2025, the highest annual total since 2007, led by US investors. The economy grew at an annualised 3.3% in the second quarter of 2026, and business investment has rebounded on oil and gas activity and machinery spending. Prime Minister Mark Carney's Liberals secured a parliamentary majority in April 2026, the first move from minority to majority between elections in Canada's history, giving the government a mandate to 2029. The Major Projects Office is fast-tracking projects including LNG Canada Phase 2 and the Darlington new nuclear project, and in October 2026 the West Coast Oil Pipeline, designed to carry about one million barrels a day to the Pacific, became the first project designated under the Building Canada Act.

Canada's main structural challenge is its trade relationship with the United States. In July 2026 the US declined to extend the Canada–US–Mexico Agreement (CUSMA), which remains in force until 2036 with annual reviews. Since 22 August 2026 the US has applied 50% tariffs under Section 338 to a range of Canadian goods, including dairy, alcoholic beverages and motor vehicles, alongside Section 232 tariffs on steel, aluminium, autos and lumber; Canada responded with counter-tariffs on about C$27.6 billion of US goods. CUSMA-qualifying goods remain exempt from the US Section 301 tariffs, and energy, critical minerals and potash are excluded from the Section 338 measures. Canada is diversifying actively, including a January 2026 agreement with China on electric vehicles and canola and a target of 50 million tonnes a year of LNG exports to Asia by 2030, creating opportunities in energy, minerals and new trade corridors.

How do I get in?

Invest in Canada, the federal investment promotion agency, now chaired by Dominic Barton with Gurinder Grewal as chief executive, has a stronger mandate to guide the largest foreign investors from interest to construction

The Major Projects Office in Calgary, created in August 2025, coordinates approvals for nation-building projects, which can be listed as being in the national interest under the Building Canada Act

Investment Canada Act: net-benefit review applies above C$2.179 billion enterprise value for trade-agreement investors and C$1.452 billion for other WTO investors in 2026, while national-security review can apply to any investment

Budget 2026 introduced a C$36.1 billion Productivity Mega Deduction, expanding accelerated write-offs for new capital investment, and a Canada Growth Incentive for early-stage investment

Floating Canadian dollar with no capital controls and free repatriation of profits

Provincial investment agencies and incentive programmes, which complement federal support for projects in each province

Trade and market access

Canada is a WTO member and a party to CUSMA with the United States and Mexico, which remains in force until 2036. It also has free trade agreements with the European Union (CETA) and the Asia-Pacific through the CPTPP, giving access to markets across Europe and Asia, and in January 2026 agreed reduced tariffs with China on electric vehicles and canola. The United States remains by far its largest export market, especially for energy, autos and metals. Pacific ports at Vancouver, Prince Rupert and Kitimat connect Canada to Asia, and new projects such as the West Coast Oil Pipeline, LNG Canada Phase 2 and the Contrecoeur terminal will expand export capacity to markets beyond the United States.

The overall risk picture

Canada offers investors a stable democracy, a strong legal system and a government with a clear parliamentary majority until 2029, together with record foreign investment in 2025. The principal risk is trade with the United States: 50% Section 338 tariffs, sector tariffs on steel, aluminium, autos and lumber, and annual CUSMA reviews create uncertainty for export-oriented manufacturers. Mitigation lies in confirming CUSMA origin, using remission orders, focusing on excluded sectors such as energy and critical minerals, and diversifying markets; Canada is pursuing new links with Europe and Asia. Inflation of 3.1% and the October rate rise to 2.5% raise borrowing costs, but long-term expectations are anchored. The Investment Canada Act applies heightened national-security scrutiny to state-owned investors, critical minerals and sensitive technologies, so early legal advice is essential, while investors from allied countries are routinely welcomed.

Business networks

The Canadian Chamber of Commerce, the Business Council of Canada and the Canadian Manufacturers and Exporters represent business nationally, while bilateral bodies such as the Canada-UK Chamber of Commerce and the American Chamber of Commerce in Canada support foreign investors. Invest in Canada works with provincial, territorial, Indigenous and municipal partners, and the Canada Infrastructure Bank and Export Development Canada provide co-financing and trade finance for qualifying projects. Indigenous-owned development corporations are increasingly important equity partners in resource and infrastructure projects, and foreign embassies' commercial sections in Ottawa offer introductions.

Who this market suits

Canada suits investors seeking a stable, rules-based base with resource depth: energy and LNG investors targeting new export capacity to Asia; mining and critical-minerals investors in Saskatchewan, British Columbia and the North; infrastructure, nuclear and power investors participating in nation-building projects; and technology, AI and financial-services firms drawing on Toronto and Montreal talent. Manufacturers serving the US market should plan carefully around current US tariffs and CUSMA origin rules. State-owned investors and those in sensitive sectors should expect national-security review.

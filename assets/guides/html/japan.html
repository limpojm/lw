Investor Entry Routes

  • JETRO (Japan External Trade Organization) and its Invest Japan Business Support Center provide one-stop facilitation, incorporation guidance and partner matching (J-Bridge) for foreign investors
  • Foreign Exchange and Foreign Trade Act (FEFTA) framework: most investments proceed freely, with prior notification and a standard 30-day waiting period applying only in designated sensitive sectors
  • Special Zones for Financial and Asset Management Businesses in Hokkaido, Tokyo, Osaka and Fukuoka, with Financial Services Agency support for new entrants including English-language procedures
  • The Takaichi administration's 17 Strategic Sectors and Public-Private Investment Roadmap, offering government co-investment and support in areas such as semiconductors, AI, shipbuilding, aerospace and medical devices
  • Freely floating, fully convertible yen managed by the Bank of Japan, with no general restrictions on capital movement or profit repatriation
  • Business Manager visa for foreign entrepreneurs, with a minimum capital requirement raised to 30 million yen from October 2025
  • Acquisitions and partnerships, supported by corporate-governance reforms that have encouraged restructuring, carve-outs and take-private transactions

Market-Entry Observation

Japan in October 2026 is a large, stable economy completing a historic return to normal monetary conditions. Real GDP grew 1.2% in 2025, with nominal GDP up 4.6%, and second-quarter 2026 growth was revised up to 0.4% quarter on quarter (1.4% annualised). On 18 September 2026 the Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to 1.25%, the highest since 1995, as wages and underlying prices settle around its 2% goal, with core-core inflation at 1.9% in August. Inward FDI stock reached a record 53.3 trillion yen at end-2024 and greenfield investment hit a record $31.6 billion, while the government has raised its 2030 FDI target to 120 trillion yen. Official reserves of $1.18 trillion, a 17-sector public-private investment roadmap and a world-class semiconductor revival led by TSMC's Kumamoto plants and Rapidus in Hokkaido underpin a strong investment proposition.

Japan's main structural challenge is energy-import dependence: around 96% of its oil comes from the Middle East and about 90% of shipments pass through the Strait of Hormuz, where traffic has been heavily disrupted since the 2026 Iran conflict. Japan has responded decisively, releasing 80 million barrels (45 days of supply) from strategic reserves in March 2026, supporting fuel prices for households and businesses, and launching an energy resilience package in August 2026 covering Hormuz-bypass pipelines with JOGMEC financing, new naphtha reserves and faster expansion of nuclear and renewables. Under the Strategic Energy Plan, renewables are set to provide 40–50% of power by 2040 and nuclear 20–22%, creating substantial long-term opportunities in clean power, grid, storage and energy-efficiency investment alongside the near-term resilience build-out.

Investor Risk Note

Japan's risk profile is that of a mature, institutionally strong economy with deep capital markets, very large external buffers and a transparent legal system. Reflation, rising wages and the Bank of Japan's orderly move to a 1.25% policy rate mark a durable exit from decades of deflation, and the IMF judges the central bank to be withdrawing accommodation appropriately. The principal near-term consideration is energy-import exposure following the 2026 Hormuz disruption, which Japan is managing through reserve releases, fuel support and a structured diversification programme. High public debt and rising JGB yields warrant monitoring, though debt is predominantly domestically held and reserves stand at $1.18 trillion. Strengthened investment screening is a compliance step for sensitive sectors rather than a barrier to mainstream investment. Investors should include energy-cost and currency scenarios in planning, while the strategic-sector roadmap, semiconductor revival and corporate-governance reforms offer substantial upside.

Market Access

Japan has one of the world's most extensive trade-agreement networks, with economic partnership or free trade agreements covering about 79% of its trade at end-2024. It is a founding member of the CPTPP, which the UK joined in December 2024, and of the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP), in force since January 2022 and covering about 30% of world GDP, trade and population. The EU–Japan Economic Partnership Agreement (in force since February 2019) and the UK–Japan Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (since January 2021) give preferential access to European markets, and Japan is a long-standing WTO member. The United States and China are its largest export markets; the July 2025 US agreement set a 15% tariff alongside a $550 billion Japanese investment programme in the US through 2029, whose first $36 billion tranche was announced in February 2026. Energy corridors are being diversified through support for pipelines that bypass the Strait of Hormuz.

Current International Business Channels

The American Chamber of Commerce in Japan, the British Chamber of Commerce in Japan and the European Business Council in Japan are the leading foreign business associations, alongside Keidanren (Japan Business Federation) and the Japan Chamber of Commerce and Industry for domestic business liaison. JETRO runs investor seminars, regional ecosystem programmes and its J-Bridge partner-matching platform, while the Financial Services Agency and the four special-zone cities offer dedicated support desks for financial-sector entrants. JBIC and JOGMEC are important counterparts for energy and resource partnerships, and Japan's large trading houses and corporate groups are valuable sources of market intelligence and joint-venture partners.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Japan suits long-term investors seeking stable, rules-based exposure to a large, high-income market: technology and semiconductor-supply-chain investors aligned with the strategic-sector roadmap and the Kumamoto and Hokkaido clusters; data-centre, logistics and clean-energy investors responding to rising power demand and energy-security priorities; asset managers and fintech firms using the financial special zones; private equity and strategic acquirers benefiting from corporate-governance reforms and carve-outs; and automation, healthcare and life-science investors addressing the needs of an ageing society. Energy-intensive investors should factor fuel-cost scenarios into project economics.

STEP 4: Build energy-cost and currency scenarios into planningModel fuel-price and yen movements in light of the Hormuz disruption and monetary normalisation, applying standard hedging where exposure is significant.

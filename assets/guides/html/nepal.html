The economy today

Nepal in October 2026 combines exceptionally strong external buffers with a reform-minded new government. Following the March 2026 elections, the Rastriya Swatantra Party won 182 of 275 seats, giving Prime Minister Balen Shah's government the clearest parliamentary majority in years and a mandate centred on anti-corruption, transparency and job creation. GDP grew by an estimated 3.85% in FY2025/26 while the external position strengthened markedly: remittances rose 37.1% to Rs2,363 billion, the current account recorded a $6.33 billion surplus, and gross reserves reached a record $26.48 billion by mid-September 2026, enough for 20.9 months of merchandise imports. Inflation averaged 3.08% in FY2025/26 before rising to 6.34% year on year in mid-September 2026, the policy rate stands at 4.5%, and equity FDI inflows rose 137% to Rs28.49 billion. Fitch reaffirmed its BB- rating with a stable outlook, and the 2026/27 budget targets 7% growth, faster foreign-investment approvals, simpler profit repatriation and an early exit from the FATF grey list.

Nepal's main structural challenge is exposure to Himalayan natural hazards, highlighted by the 26 August 2026 flood in Rasuwa, Nuwakot and Dhading, which claimed more than 1,400 lives. The World Bank estimates direct physical damage at $1.66 billion and has revised FY2026/27 growth to 3.7%, recovering to 5.2% in FY2027/28; about 430 MW of generation and transmission capacity, around 10.6% of installed hydro and solar capacity, was affected. Nepal enters reconstruction from a position of strength: record reserves, public debt of about 45% of GDP and a record Rs18.76 billion electricity trade surplus in FY2025/26 provide room to respond. Reconstruction and recovery needs of around $4.8 billion, two-thirds of them in infrastructure, create significant opportunities in climate-resilient hydropower, transmission, roads, early-warning systems and engineering services, supported by the World Bank, ADB and other partners.

How do I get in?

Investment Board Nepal (IBN), chaired by the Prime Minister, approves and facilitates large projects (above NPR 6 billion) through Project Development Agreements and a one-window service

Department of Industry and its one-stop service centre handle approvals under the Foreign Investment and Technology Transfer Act 2019 (amended 2025), with a general minimum foreign investment of NPR 20 million

Automatic approval route, extended in February 2026 to cover a wider range of manufacturing, tourism, agriculture, IT and services activities, accounting for 711 of 914 FDI approvals in the first 11 months of FY2025/26

Special Economic Zones, including Simara and Bhairahawa, managed by the SEZ Authority and offering serviced industrial land at concessional lease rates

Nepal Rastra Bank oversight of foreign exchange; the rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee, and dividends and capital may be repatriated subject to NRB approval and documentation, with a currency-hedging facility for foreign loans and investment announced in the 2026/27 budget

Hydropower development through generation licences, power purchase agreements with the Nepal Electricity Authority and access to Indian and Bangladeshi markets under the 2024 long-term India–Nepal power trade agreement

Trade and market access

Nepal has been a WTO member since 2004 and is a member of the South Asian Free Trade Area (SAFTA) and BIMSTEC, linking it to the wider South Asian and Bay of Bengal markets. India is by far its largest trading partner, and bilateral trade, transit and power-trade arrangements give Nepali producers preferential access to the Indian market of more than 1.4 billion people; electricity is also exported to Bangladesh via India under a 2024 tripartite agreement. A 2024 long-term agreement envisages India importing up to 10,000 MW of Nepali power over ten years, and Nepal signed a Belt and Road cooperation framework with China in December 2024, supporting northern connectivity. As a least developed country, Nepal enjoys preferential access to the EU and the UK, including under the UK's Developing Countries Trading Scheme, with transition arrangements to follow LDC graduation.

The overall risk picture

Nepal's risk profile is that of an economy with unusually strong external buffers, a government with a fresh and decisive mandate, and growing natural advantages in clean energy and tourism. Record reserves of $26.48 billion, a $6.33 billion current account surplus, moderate public debt of about 45% of GDP and a stable BB- rating give the country real capacity to absorb shocks. The August 2026 Rasuwa flood is a significant setback, and investors should treat climate and seismic hazards as core design and insurance considerations; at the same time, reconstruction will channel substantial public and donor finance into resilient infrastructure. Nepal's continued FATF grey listing is a compliance consideration that adds to banking due diligence, and investors should ensure robust KYC and anti-money-laundering documentation. The orderly transition from the September 2025 protests to an elected majority government, with a reform-focused budget, points to improving policy predictability over the medium term.

Who should I talk to first?

Office of the Prime Minister (Balen Shah)

Leads a majority government elected in March 2026 on an anti-corruption, transparency and job-creation agenda; the Prime Minister also chairs the IBN

Primary reference point for Nepal's reform direction and investment priorities

Investment Board Nepal (IBN)

Approves and facilitates large-scale and strategic projects, including major hydropower and infrastructure, with 53 projects approved and progressing

Essential counterpart for large-ticket and infrastructure investment

Department of Industry / Ministry of Industry, Commerce and Supplies

Handles FDI approvals, automatic-route registrations and technology-transfer agreements below the IBN threshold

Standard first point of contact for most foreign-investment entries

Nepal Rastra Bank

Central bank; sets monetary policy, manages the rupee peg and approves foreign-exchange and repatriation transactions

Relevant for capital inflows, repatriation, hedging and banking matters

Ministry of Finance (Swarnim Wagle)

Leads the 2026/27 budget, FATF reform and foreign-investment procedural reforms

Key interlocutor on fiscal incentives, reconstruction finance and reform timelines

Nepal Electricity Authority / Ministry of Energy, Water Resources and Irrigation

State utility and policy lead for power purchase, transmission and cross-border electricity trade

Central counterpart for any hydropower, solar or transmission investment

Special Economic Zone Authority

Manages Nepal's SEZs, including Simara and Bhairahawa, and allocates industrial land to investors

Relevant for export-oriented manufacturing and processing projects

Business networks

The Federation of Nepalese Chambers of Commerce and Industry (FNCCI) and the Confederation of Nepalese Industries (CNI) are the leading private-sector bodies, and the Nepal Britain Chamber of Commerce and Industry (NBCCI) in Kathmandu and the Britain Nepal Chamber of Commerce (BNCC) in London are the main bilateral channels for UK investors. The Nepal–UK Economic Partnership Forum, held in London in July 2026, saw the West London Chamber of Commerce sign memoranda with CNI and NBCCI, with priority areas including hydropower, renewable energy, tourism, IT and digital services. The Investment Board Nepal organises periodic investment summits and project pipelines, while the World Bank, ADB and other development partners maintain large country programmes now focused on post-flood reconstruction, energy and private-sector development.

Who this market suits

Nepal suits investors seeking long-term exposure to clean energy and a reform-minded frontier market with strong external buffers: hydropower, solar and transmission investors positioned to serve growing export demand from India and Bangladesh; infrastructure, engineering and climate-resilience specialists able to participate in post-flood reconstruction alongside development finance institutions; tourism and hospitality investors building on more than 1.15 million foreign arrivals in 2025; and IT, outsourcing and digital-services investors, the most active category of FDI approvals. Investors should be comfortable with project-level hazard planning, enhanced compliance procedures during FATF grey listing and a measured pace of approvals.

    Engage the Investment Board Nepal or Department of IndustryOpen early dialogue with IBN for large projects, or the Department of Industry for smaller entries, to confirm eligibility for the automatic route and current approval terms.

    Align with the new government's reform and reconstruction prioritiesMap opportunities against the 2026/27 budget and post-flood recovery plans, where resilient infrastructure and energy are central.
  3. 3
    Build hazard resilience and insurance into project designCommission site-level climate and seismic assessments, drawing on lessons from the August 2026 flood, and secure appropriate cover.
  4. 4
    Prepare robust compliance and banking documentationEstablish strong KYC, anti-money-laundering and NRB repatriation documentation from the outset to ease transactions during FATF grey listing.
  5. 5
    Partner with development finance and established local groupsCo-invest alongside the World Bank, ADB and experienced Nepali partners, and use FNCCI, CNI and NBCCI networks for introductions.

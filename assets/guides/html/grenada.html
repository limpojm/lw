Investor Entry Routes

  • Grenada Investment Development Corporation (GIDC), the national investment promotion agency since 1985, screens proposals, guides investors through licences and consents, and administers a performance-based incentive regime
  • Investment Act No. 42 of 2014, the framework for investment registration and incentives, prioritising tourism, agribusiness, health and wellness, ICT, renewable energy and the creative industries
  • Citizenship by Investment programme, run by the Investment Migration Agency since 2024, with contributions from $235,000 to the National Transformation Fund or $270,000 in approved real-estate projects, now being re-legislated in 2026
  • Eastern Caribbean dollar, pegged to the US dollar at EC$2.70 for 50 years under the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank, with reserve backing of 97.6% against a 60% statutory floor
  • Public-private partnership and special-purpose-vehicle structures, as with Polaris Development Company Limited for the Project Polaris hospital, financed through concessional loans, equity and philanthropy
  • Branded resort development and approved CBI hotel projects, eight of which were approved as of late 2025, offering a route into the tourism pipeline
  • Renewable-energy procurement, including the geothermal exploration programme and grid-scale solar opportunities, supporting the shift away from imported fuel

Market-Entry Observation

Grenada in October 2026 is one of the Eastern Caribbean's steadiest performers, combining sustained growth with falling debt and very low inflation. Real GDP grew 3.3% in 2024 and an estimated 4.4% in 2025 (IMF; the government estimates 6.2%), led by investment and construction, with the IMF projecting 3.2% for 2026 and the government 4.1%. Inflation averaged just 0.7% in 2025 and unemployment fell to 7.5% by October 2025. Foreign direct investment reached $207 million in 2025, up about 4% and equivalent to around 14% of GDP, while stayover arrivals rose 12% in the first quarter of 2026. Public debt has fallen from 72.7% of GDP in 2024 to 68.3% in 2025, anchored by the Fiscal Resilience Act's 60% target, and a pipeline of branded resorts and the $250 million Project Polaris hospital is broadening the investment base.

Grenada's principal structural challenge is exposure to hurricanes, and its response has become a regional model of financial preparedness. Hurricane Beryl struck Carriacou and Petite Martinique in July 2024, causing losses estimated at 17% of GDP ($220 million). Pre-arranged instruments delivered fast liquidity: a CCRIF parametric insurance payout of about $55.5 million, a World Bank Catastrophe Deferred Drawdown Option of about $20 million, and the first-ever use of the hurricane clause in Grenada's restructured bonds, deferring two payments. Recovery is now well advanced through the Build Back Better housing programme and an EC$64.8 million 2026 allocation for the sister islands, and the emphasis on climate-resilient infrastructure, renewable energy and resilient construction is creating a steady flow of investment opportunities.

Investor Risk Note

Grenada's risk profile is that of a well-managed small island economy with credible fiscal institutions, a stable currency anchor and a proven disaster-response toolkit. The Eastern Caribbean dollar's 50-year peg to the US dollar, reserve backing of 97.6% and inflation below 1% provide macroeconomic stability, while the Fiscal Resilience Act and a falling debt ratio support medium-term confidence. Hurricane exposure is the main structural risk, but the speed of liquidity from parametric insurance and contingent finance after Hurricane Beryl demonstrated the strength of Grenada's preparedness. The most material policy consideration is the EU's push to end Caribbean Citizenship by Investment programmes, which could affect CBI-linked revenue and real-estate projects from 2028; investors should take legal advice on CBI-structured investments and prioritise projects with strong standalone demand. The general election on 5 November 2026 is a routine democratic event, and investors may wish to align key approvals with the incoming government's early priorities.

Market Access

Grenada is a member of CARICOM and its Single Market and Economy, and of the Organisation of Eastern Caribbean States (OECS) Economic Union, giving access to a regional market with free movement of goods and, within the OECS, of people. As a member of the eight-country Eastern Caribbean Currency Union, it shares a common central bank, currency and regional securities market. Grenada is a WTO member and a party, through CARIFORUM, to Economic Partnership Agreements with both the European Union and the United Kingdom, providing preferential access to European markets. Tourism, its principal export, draws mainly on the United States and the United Kingdom, supported by direct airlift into Maurice Bishop International Airport from North America and Europe. This combination of regional integration, European trade preferences and a US dollar-linked currency gives investors a stable, outward-facing platform.

Current International Business Channels

The Grenada Chamber of Industry and Commerce is the principal private-sector body and a practical first contact for local partnerships, alongside GIDC's investor facilitation and SME programmes. Regionally, the OECS Commission and CARICOM institutions provide channels into wider Eastern Caribbean opportunities. The World Bank, the Caribbean Development Bank and the IMF are closely engaged on resilience, fiscal reform and infrastructure, while the UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office supports the geothermal programme and CCRIF underpins disaster-risk finance. International hotel groups and developers active at La Sagesse and Grand Anse, and the Mount Sinai partnership behind Project Polaris, are useful sources of practical intelligence on doing business in Grenada.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Grenada suits investors seeking long-term exposure to a stable, US dollar-linked island economy with a strong tourism brand: luxury and boutique hospitality groups and branded-residence developers drawn by rising arrivals and new airlift; healthcare, medical-education and wellness investors aligned with Project Polaris; renewable-energy developers targeting geothermal, solar and storage; resilient-construction and infrastructure firms supporting public investment and PPPs; and agribusiness investors adding value to Grenada's spice, cocoa and fisheries base. Investors using Citizenship by Investment structures should plan for the evolving EU and regional regulatory framework.

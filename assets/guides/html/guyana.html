Investor Entry Routes

  • Guyana Office for Investment (GO-Invest), the national investment promotion agency, which assists with business registration, tax-concession applications, investment agreements and land applications
  • Investment Act 2004, which bars discrimination between foreign and domestic investors and provides for tax holidays, exemptions, accelerated depreciation and repatriation of capital, profits and dividends
  • Local Content Act 2021, overseen by the Local Content Secretariat, which reserves 40 categories of oil and gas goods and services for qualifying Guyanese firms and makes joint ventures a natural entry route
  • Planned industrial and free zones, including the Wales development area beside the gas-to-energy project and a proposed free trade zone at Lethem on the Brazilian border
  • Bank of Guyana currency regime: the Guyana dollar is legally convertible at an official rate held near G$208.5 per $1, with invoice verification for conversions since September 2025, so US-dollar access should be planned with banks in advance
  • Oil and gas participation through the Ministry of Natural Resources and the supply chains of the Stabroek operators (ExxonMobil, Chevron and CNOOC)
  • International dispute protection through ICSID membership, the New York Convention (in force since 2014) and the Caribbean Court of Justice as final court of appeal

Market-Entry Observation

Guyana is the fastest-growing economy in the Americas and one of the fastest-growing in the world. Real GDP grew 19.3% in 2025, with the non-oil economy expanding by about 14%, and the government estimates growth of 33.3% in the first half of 2026, raising its full-year forecast to 20.8%, with non-oil growth of 10.2%. Oil output passed 900,000 barrels a day in late 2025, and the 250,000-barrel-a-day Uaru project, due to start in Q4 2026, is set to take production above one million barrels a day. Foreign direct investment reached about $9 billion in 2025, according to UNCTAD data. The IMF described the outlook as highly favourable in July 2026, with 2025 inflation at 3.3%, and still rates the risk of debt distress as low, with public debt at 28.6% of GDP at end-2025. The Natural Resource Fund held $4.29 billion at end-June 2026, and the PPP/C government's re-election with 36 of 65 seats in September 2025 gives policy continuity.

Guyana's main structural challenge is turning oil wealth into broad, lasting prosperity. Crude oil accounted for over 85% of export receipts in 2025, and rapid growth is putting pressure on power, housing, skills and US-dollar liquidity. The policy response is substantial. Oil revenues are saved and managed through the Natural Resource Fund. The 300 MW gas-to-energy project is designed to cut the cost of power for industry. Large public investment in roads and bridges is lifting non-oil sectors, with construction, manufacturing and agriculture leading growth. The IMF expects non-oil growth of about 7% a year over the next five years, and a larger share of oil income will flow to the Fund as cost recovery winds down. For investors, this is the core opportunity: supplying the infrastructure, services, food production and manufacturing that a fast-growing, newly wealthy economy needs.

Investor Risk Note

Guyana offers growth on a scale few markets can match, backed by world-class offshore resources, a sovereign wealth fund and a debt ratio the IMF considers low risk. Its main considerations are manageable with planning. First, Venezuela's claim to the Essequibo region is now before the International Court of Justice, which held hearings in May 2026 and is expected to rule within months. Guyana's commitment to a legal resolution and its strong international partnerships support stability. Second, US-dollar access has tightened as the economy has expanded, and the invoice-verification rules introduced in September 2025 mean investors should plan currency conversion and repatriation timing with their banks from the outset. Third, local content rules and the capacity of the courts favour well-structured joint ventures and arbitration clauses. With the gas-to-energy project, new bridges and roads, and continued strong non-oil growth, these constraints are easing, and investors who build them into early planning are well placed to benefit from Guyana's expansion.

Market Access

Guyana is a member of CARICOM and its Single Market and Economy, which gives preferential access across the Caribbean Community, and it has been a member of the WTO since 1995. Through CARIFORUM, it is a party to the Economic Partnership Agreement with the European Union. Crude oil dominates exports, with Europe, the United States and Panama among the leading destinations in 2025, while rice, sugar, gold and bauxite remain important non-oil exports. Guyana is also CARICOM's land bridge to South America: Lethem connects it by road to northern Brazil, and a bridge across the Corentyne River to Suriname is planned. As export volumes rise, investors are well placed to supply both the regional Caribbean market and the wider Atlantic basin.

Current International Business Channels

The Private Sector Commission and the Georgetown Chamber of Commerce and Industry are the principal domestic business bodies, and the American Chamber of Commerce in Guyana represents a growing international business community. GO-Invest runs investor outreach and sector promotion, and the annual Guyana Energy Conference and Supply Chain Expo in Georgetown is the main meeting point for energy and supply-chain businesses. The Stabroek operators run supplier-development programmes aligned with the Local Content Act, which are useful for finding partners. Multilateral and development lenders, including the Inter-American Development Bank, the World Bank, the Caribbean Development Bank and US EXIM, are active in infrastructure, energy and private-sector development, and they can co-finance projects.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Guyana suits investors who can move quickly into a fast-growing economy and who value long-term positioning. These include: oil services, engineering and logistics firms building local-content partnerships around the Stabroek projects; construction, materials and real-estate investors serving housing, hotels and public works; power, gas and industrial investors drawn to the gas-to-energy project and the planned Wales industrial area; and agribusiness and food-processing investors building on Guyana's land, water and CARICOM market access. Financial-services and healthcare providers also find room to grow in a market that is expanding fast. Investors with patient capital and experience of frontier markets, who can manage currency planning and local partnerships, are best placed to benefit.

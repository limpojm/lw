The economy today

French Polynesia in November 2026 offers investors a stable, euro-pegged economy under French legal protection, with record tourism and an improving credit profile. GDP grew 1.2% in 2025 to XPF 729 billion, slightly above the national rate, with about 1,800 jobs created and unemployment down to 6.7%. Tourism reached a record 281,277 visitors, up 7%, with hotel occupancy at 67.7%, and pearl exports rebounded strongly. Inflation was 1.3% in 2025 and close to zero in early 2026. In June 2026 S&P raised the territory's rating to A+ with a stable outlook, citing stronger governance, moderate debt and large cash reserves. The government's Cap 2033 plan targets 450,000 visitors by 2033, renewables at 55–60% of the energy mix by 2030 and growth in agriculture and technology, while the 2026 budget of XPF 238.7 billion includes XPF 35.7 billion of investment.

French Polynesia's main structural challenge is broadening a small, import-dependent economy beyond tourism and public spending. Goods exports of XPF 13.8 billion cover only about 6% of imports, fish exports fell 31% in 2025, household consumption is flat, and the April 2026 split in the ruling Tavini party has left President Moetai Brotherson without a stable Assembly majority. Mitigation is strong: the Speaker and the main factions have said they will not seek to remove the government before the 2028 elections, the government used the engagement-of-responsibility procedure to adopt a budget amendment in July 2026, and sound public finances provide room to support activity. The planned 2027–2035 tourism strategy, generous local and national tax incentives and the energy transition offer investors clear opportunities in hotels, renewables, aquaculture and digital services.

How do I get in?

Governing relationship: a French overseas collectivity with an autonomy statute (an "overseas country"), whose own government and Assembly set tax, economic and labour law, while the French State retains defence, justice, public order and currency

Economic Development Agency (ADE): investment, business-creation and export support, and the entry point for local tax-incentive applications

Local tax-incentive scheme (défiscalisation locale): tax credits of 30–40% for approved productive investments, combinable with French national overseas schemes for a combined benefit that can exceed 52.4% of eligible investment

Priority Development Zones for investments of XPF 10 billion or more, offering long-term tax exemptions and, on request, import-duty relief

Tax regime: no personal income, wealth or inheritance tax; 25% corporate income tax (20% for renewable energy, R&D and digital firms); VAT at 16%, 13% and 5%

Currency: the CFP franc, issued by the IEOM and pegged at 1,000 XPF = €8.38, with convertibility guaranteed by the French Republic

EU status: an overseas country and territory associated with the EU, outside the single market but with duty-free, quota-free access and EU cooperation funding

Trade and market access

As an overseas country and territory associated with the European Union, French Polynesia sits outside the EU single market and customs union, sets its own customs duties and VAT, and has duty-free, quota-free access to the EU market together with EU cooperation funding; its French nationals are EU citizens. Exports are led by tourism services worth XPF 88 billion in 2025, Tahitian pearls and fish, with total exports of goods and services of XPF 126.2 billion, up 8.1%. Faa'a international airport connects Tahiti with mainland France, North America, Asia and the wider Pacific, and cruise passengers reached 53,900 in 2025. Within the region, French Polynesia is a full member of the Pacific Islands Forum, giving it a voice alongside Australia, New Zealand and Pacific neighbours, while France remains its principal economic and financial partner.

The overall risk picture

French Polynesia's risk profile is that of an autonomous French territory with sound public finances, an A+ rating from S&P (raised in June 2026) and the legal and monetary security of the French Republic, including a CFP franc pegged to the euro with French-guaranteed convertibility. Growth of 1.2% in 2025, record tourism and falling unemployment underline the economy's resilience. The main considerations are political and structural: since the April 2026 split in the ruling party the government has lacked a stable Assembly majority, although the main groups have said they will not seek to remove it before the 2028 elections; the long-term status debate continues, with the President favouring a gradual approach; and the economy remains dependent on tourism and imports. These are well mitigated by strong reserves, a clear tourism and energy strategy and generous incentive frameworks. Investors should take local tax and legal advice, secure incentive approvals early and diversify markets to capture the territory's substantial upside.

Who should I talk to first?

Presidency of French Polynesia (President Moetai Brotherson)

Heads the government, which sets economic, tax and investment policy and leads the Cap 2033 development plan

Primary reference point for strategic direction and major projects

Economic Development Agency (ADE)

Supports investment, business creation and exports, and receives local tax-incentive applications

Standard first point of contact for market entry and incentives

IEOM (Institut d'émission d'outre-mer)

Issues the CFP franc and monitors banking, credit and economic conditions in the French Pacific territories

Authoritative source of economic and banking intelligence

Ministry of Economy and Finance (Minister Warren Dexter)

Leads the budget, tax policy and the Cap 2033 economic plan

Key counterpart on tax, incentives and public finance

Assembly of French Polynesia (Speaker Antony Géros)

57-member legislature that votes local laws, taxes and the budget

Relevant for tax, incentive and sector legislation

High Commission of the Republic

Represents the French State, which retains defence, justice, public order and currency

Interlocutor on State financing, legality and national policy

Tahiti Tourisme and the tourism observatory

Promote the destination and steer the 2027–2035 tourism strategy

Partners for hotel and tourism-infrastructure investment

Business networks

The Chamber of Commerce, Industry, Services and Trades (CCISM) and employer bodies such as the MEDEF Polynésie and CPME Polynésie represent business and engage regularly with the government. The Economic Development Agency coordinates investor support, while Business France and Team France Invest connect international investors with French networks. AFD, Bpifrance and the French State are the main public financing partners, and the European Union supports the territory as an overseas country and territory. Pacific Islands Forum membership and air links to North America, Asia and the Pacific provide wider regional channels.

Who this market suits

French Polynesia suits investors seeking a secure, euro-pegged base with strong tax incentives: hotel and resort developers targeting the Society Islands and outer archipelagos as visitor numbers grow towards 450,000; renewable-energy and storage developers benefiting from a 20% corporate tax rate and the 55–60% renewable target; pearl, aquaculture, seafood and agri-food businesses serving export and local markets; and digital and R&D companies attracted by reduced tax rates and the absence of personal income tax. Large projects of XPF 10 billion or more can use Priority Development Zone status.

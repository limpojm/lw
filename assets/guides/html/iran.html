Investor Entry Routes

  • Sanctions licensing route: US persons are barred from virtually all dealings under the Iranian Transactions and Sanctions Regulations, and since 10 September 2026 OFAC applies a presumption of denial to Iran-related specific licences except where required by law or in cases such as risk to life or environmental safety
  • Humanitarian channels: limited authorisations for food, agricultural commodities, medicine, medical devices and NGO work (including Iran General Licenses 8A and E), to be confirmed with counsel because OFAC suspended several other general licences in August and September 2026
  • UN, EU and UK regimes: UN sanctions were reimposed on 28 September 2025; EU Regulation 2025/1975 restores bans on Iranian oil, gas and petrochemicals, related finance and insurance, and unauthorised fund transfers; the UK has reimposed asset freezes and nuclear-related controls
  • Foreign Investment Promotion and Protection Act (FIPPA), administered by OIETAI, offering on paper national treatment, protection against expropriation and transfer of capital and profits, although sanctions currently prevent Western use of these guarantees
  • Free Trade Zones (Kish, Qeshm, Chabahar, Aras, Anzali, Arvand, Maku) with 20-year tax holidays and customs exemptions, all fully within the scope of US, EU, UK and UN sanctions
  • Currency regime: the rial has fallen to around 2.69 million per dollar on the open market (3 October 2026), and the Central Bank of Iran is itself designated under US sanctions, so repatriation through formal channels is not available to Western investors
  • Normalisation-readiness route: building knowledge, compliance frameworks and lawful relationships so that investors can act promptly if and when sanctions are lawfully eased

Market-Entry Observation

Iran has long-term fundamentals that few markets can match: a population of over 80 million, a well-educated workforce, a substantial domestic market and the world's second-largest natural gas and fourth-largest proven oil reserves. In October 2026, however, the economy is in a deep wartime contraction. The IMF's October 2026 World Economic Outlook projects real GDP to shrink 7.6% in 2026. The Statistical Center of Iran reported point-to-point inflation of 89.8% and annual inflation of 73.6% in September 2026, and the rial reached a record low of around 2.69 million per dollar on 3 October, from about 1.75 million when the war began on 28 February. Oil, the main source of export revenue, has been hit hardest: Iran exported around 2 million barrels a day of crude, condensate and products in 2025, but terminal loadings had fallen to about 640,000 barrels a day by May 2026 under the US blockade, with China its only major buyer.

Iran's defining challenge for international investors is the combination of comprehensive sanctions and active conflict. UN sanctions were reimposed through the snapback mechanism on 28 September 2025, followed immediately by restored EU and UK measures. The US–Israeli strikes that began on 28 February 2026 killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, who was succeeded on 8 March by Mojtaba Khamenei. An April ceasefire and the June Islamabad Memorandum both broke down, the US has reinstated its naval blockade of Iranian ports, and October 2026 has seen the highest number of attacks on shipping since the war began, although Hormuz oil flows recovered to about 68% of pre-war levels in the week to 13 October. Washington has also tightened its sanctions, adding aviation, shipping, gold, technology, digital-asset, automotive and rail sectors to its secondary-sanctions determinations and suspending several general licences. For compliant investors, the opportunity is limited to licensed humanitarian trade where each home jurisdiction permits it, and to long-term readiness for a market of considerable scale should a lawful settlement be reached.

Investor Risk Note

Iran is, in October 2026, a market that Western investors cannot currently enter. Comprehensive US sanctions, the UN sanctions reimposed in September 2025 and restored EU and UK measures prohibit new investment and most trade, and the 2026 war, the US blockade of Iranian ports and attacks on shipping in the Strait of Hormuz add direct physical and logistical risk. US measures have tightened further since August 2026, extending secondary-sanctions exposure to non-US firms in many sectors and narrowing licensing. Every engagement should therefore start with specialist sanctions advice in each relevant jurisdiction. Within those limits, lawful humanitarian trade in food, agricultural commodities, medicine and medical devices may be possible where authorised. Looking further ahead, Iran's 80-million-plus population, educated workforce, hydrocarbon reserves and Eurasian trade links mean that any lawful settlement would create one of the region's largest reconstruction and investment opportunities, and investors who build knowledge and compliance readiness now will be best placed to respond.

Market Access

Iran is not a member of the WTO; its accession working party has existed since May 2005 without completing. It became a full member of the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation in July 2023 and of BRICS on 1 January 2024, and its full free trade agreement with the Eurasian Economic Union, in force since 15 May 2025, covers about 90% of goods traded with Russia, Kazakhstan, Belarus, Armenia and Kyrgyzstan. China has been the only major buyer of Iranian oil, which in 2025 accounted for around two million barrels a day of exports; those flows have fallen sharply under the US blockade. Iran's geography gives it potential corridor roles, including the Chabahar port link to Afghanistan and Central Asia and northern routes to the Caspian and EAEU, but these routes are constrained by sanctions, by the expiry of the US waiver for India's Chabahar operations in April 2026 and by insecurity in and around the Strait of Hormuz.

Current International Business Channels

Western business channels with Iran are, for now, largely confined to information, compliance and humanitarian work. The British Iranian Chamber of Commerce remains a reference point for UK businesses on zone regimes and the investment framework, and home-country sanctions authorities, trade ministries and sanctions counsel are the essential first channel for any question about permitted activity. Mediation by Pakistan and Qatar is the main diplomatic track to watch, and any settlement emerging from it would be the trigger for broader business-council, chamber and development-finance engagement. Investors should treat Iranian state promotion events and offers of facilitation with caution until counsel has confirmed that participation is lawful in their jurisdiction.

Investor Profile Best Suited

In current conditions Iran suits only a narrow group of participants: established suppliers of food, agricultural commodities, medicine and medical devices with robust sanctions-compliance programmes and authorisation from their home jurisdictions; humanitarian NGOs operating under applicable licences; and long-horizon investors, advisers and research teams building normalisation-readiness without making commitments. Investors from jurisdictions outside the US, EU and UK should note that UN sanctions bind all member states and that US secondary sanctions now reach non-US firms across most major sectors. Should a lawful settlement come, Iran's scale, human capital, energy reserves and reconstruction needs would make it relevant to infrastructure, energy, healthcare, consumer and logistics investors.

From Intelligence to Engagement

STEP 1: Obtain specialist sanctions advice in every relevant jurisdictionBefore any Iran-related activity, confirm with counsel the current US, UN, EU and UK position, including OFAC's September 2026 licensing changes and EO 13902 sector determinations.
STEP 2: Confine any activity to clearly authorised humanitarian tradeFood, agricultural commodities, medicine and medical devices may be possible only under specific authorisations; document licence coverage for every transaction and payment.
STEP 3: Screen counterparties, banks and shipping for secondary-sanctions exposureCheck all parties against current designation lists, use only explicitly authorised payment channels, and follow OFAC and UKMTO alerts on the Strait of Hormuz.
STEP 4: Track diplomacy and the security situationMonitor mediation through Pakistan and Qatar, the US blockade and Hormuz shipping conditions as the leading indicators of any change in the legal position.
STEP 5: Build normalisation readiness without commitmentsDevelop market knowledge and scenario plans, but avoid negotiations, contingent contracts or pre-commitments unless counsel confirms they are permitted.

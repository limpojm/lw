The economy today

Poland in November 2026 is the EU's largest and most consistent growth story in Central Europe, combining a large domestic market with full access to the single market. GDP grew 3.6% in 2025 and 3.9% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, among the fastest rates in the EU. Inflation eased to 3.5% in October 2026, within the central bank's 2.5% ±1 point target band, and the National Bank of Poland has held its reference rate at 3.75%, the lowest since 2022, for seven consecutive meetings. Foreign direct investment inflows reached PLN 65.5 billion in 2025, 2.6% higher than in 2024, led by reinvested earnings that show established investors are expanding. Very large programmes are now under way: Poland signed the EU's first SAFE defence loan for €43.7 billion in May 2026, the 1.1 GW Baltic Power offshore wind farm delivered first power in July 2026, and the European Commission has approved state aid for the first nuclear plant, a project of about €42 billion.

Poland's main structural challenge is public finances. The general government deficit reached 7.3% of GDP in 2025, the second highest in the EU, and the draft 2027 budget keeps it at 7.1%, reflecting defence spending of 4.5% of GDP alongside higher health and social spending. Public debt passed 60% of GDP in June 2026, and Moody's lowered its rating to A3 with a stable outlook in September 2026, citing fiscal strength and political constraints before the November 2027 election. The starting point remains sound: debt of 59.7% of GDP in 2025 was well below the EU average of 81.7%, growth is strong, and the EU excessive deficit procedure sets a path to bring the deficit below 3% by 2028. For investors, the spending is itself an opportunity, channelling large sums into defence, energy security and transport through transparent tenders.

How do I get in?

Polish Investment and Trade Agency (PAIH) provides free investor support; it finalised 64 projects worth more than €4 billion with 6,600 new jobs in 2025

Polish Investment Zone: support decisions grant corporate income tax exemptions for new investments anywhere in Poland, currently for 10–15 years; a draft amendment would extend this to 15–20 years from 2027

Corporate income tax of 19%, with a 9% rate for small taxpayers with revenue up to the equivalent of €2 million

EU single-market and Schengen membership, with free movement of capital under EU rules; the złoty floats freely and Poland is not in the euro area

EU co-financing through the €54.7 billion National Recovery Plan (KPO) and cohesion programmes for energy, transport and digital projects

Defence-sector entry through the €43.7 billion EU SAFE loan programme, with the government planning to spend 89% of the funds in Poland

Large-project tenders from state developers such as Polskie Elektrownie Jądrowe (nuclear) and the CPK central airport and rail company

Trade and market access

As an EU member, Poland gives investors tariff-free access to the EU single market of some 450 million people and to all EU free trade agreements, with trade with third countries governed by the EU's common trade policy and WTO rules. Poland is a central link in European manufacturing supply chains, particularly with Germany. Schengen membership allows passport-free movement across most of the EU. Poland sits on the main east–west corridor between Germany and Ukraine and on the north–south route from the Baltic to the Adriatic, with Baltic ports at Gdańsk, Gdynia and Szczecin–Świnoujście. It is also the main logistics hub for supplies to Ukraine and is expected to play a leading role in reconstruction.

The overall risk picture

Poland's risk profile is that of a large, diversified EU economy with strong growth, a flexible exchange rate and a sound banking system. It is not on the FATF grey list and is rated investment grade by all three major agencies, with Moody's at A3 stable following its September 2026 downgrade. The main issues are the fiscal deficit, now under an EU excessive deficit procedure with a 2028 target, and political cohabitation between Prime Minister Donald Tusk's government and President Karol Nawrocki, which has slowed some laws ahead of the November 2027 parliamentary election. Both are manageable: debt is below the EU average and the government has kept major programmes such as SAFE on track. Poland borders Ukraine, Belarus and Russia; EU sanctions on Russia and Belarus apply in full, so cross-border trade with those two countries requires sanctions screening. For most investors Poland offers the legal certainty of EU law and a fast-growing domestic market.

Business networks

The American Chamber of Commerce in Poland, the British Polish Chamber of Commerce, the Polish-German Chamber of Industry and Commerce and the Polish Chamber of Commerce are the main business associations, with sector committees and regular dialogue with government. PAIH runs investor services and promotes Poland abroad, and its annual investor awards highlight leading projects. Bank Gospodarstwa Krajowego, the state development bank, and the Polish Development Fund (PFR) co-finance projects and channel National Recovery Plan funds. The European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, the European Investment Bank and the World Bank are active in energy, infrastructure and private-sector finance.

Who this market suits

Poland suits investors seeking scale and growth inside the EU: manufacturers serving German and European supply chains from Lower Silesia, Silesia and Wielkopolska; technology and business-services firms drawing on large talent pools in Kraków, Warsaw and Wrocław; energy investors targeting nuclear, offshore wind, grids and storage; defence and security companies positioned for SAFE-funded procurement; and infrastructure and construction groups bidding for airport, rail and port projects. Investors preparing for Ukraine's reconstruction can use Poland as an EU-based logistics and manufacturing platform.

The economy today

Czechia in November 2026 offers investors one of Central Europe's most industrialised economies, tightly integrated into European supply chains, with low inflation and moderate public debt. GDP grew 2.6% in 2025 and 1.6% year-on-year in the third quarter of 2026, supported by household consumption, investment and external demand. Inflation was 2.4% in October 2026, close to the Czech National Bank's target, and public debt stood at 44.2% of GDP in 2025, with a deficit of 2.2%. CzechInvest supported 25 investment projects worth $1.21 billion in 2025, three-quarters of it in automotive, led by Toyota's $776 million expansion, following onsemi's planned semiconductor investment of up to $2 billion in 2024. The government led by Prime Minister Andrej Babiš, in office since December 2025, has prioritised industry, transport and energy, and Czechia has received 88% of its €8.67 billion Recovery and Resilience Plan allocation.

Czechia's main structural challenge is sustaining competitiveness as its export-led industrial model faces higher energy costs, slower growth in Europe and the shift to electric vehicles, while keeping public finances on track. The 2027 budget plans a state deficit of CZK 386 billion, which passed its first reading in October 2026, and the Czech National Bank raised its two-week repo rate by 0.25 percentage points to 4.00% on 5 November 2026. The response combines energy security and industrial upgrading: the CZK 407 billion Dukovany nuclear programme with KHNP, investment incentives for higher-value manufacturing, and EU funds for energy, transport and digital projects. Together with a skilled workforce and central location, this creates opportunities in nuclear and energy supply chains, semiconductors, automation and EU-funded infrastructure.

How do I get in?

CzechInvest, the business and investment development agency under the Ministry of Industry and Trade, which handled 25 investment projects worth $1.21 billion in 2025

Investment incentives (tax relief and grants, including under the EU Temporary Crisis and Transformation Framework), which helped secure $913 million of the projects CzechInvest supported in 2025

A 21% corporate income tax rate, applied since 2024

EU single-market and Schengen membership, giving free movement of goods, services, capital and people across the EU

The Czech koruna, a floating currency managed under the Czech National Bank's inflation target, with free capital movement and profit repatriation under EU rules

EU co-financing through the €8.67 billion Recovery and Resilience Plan and EU cohesion programmes for energy, transport, digital and industrial projects

Supply-chain entry into the Dukovany nuclear new build, where KHNP is selecting a new construction contractor

Trade and market access

As a member of the EU, Czechia gives investors tariff-free access to the EU single market and to all the EU's free trade agreements, with WTO membership and the common trade policy governing trade with third countries. Schengen membership allows passport-free movement of people and goods across most of the continent. Czechia lies at the heart of Central Europe, with dense road and rail links to Germany, its largest trading partner, as well as Austria, Poland and Slovakia, making it a natural base for supplying German and wider European manufacturing. The country keeps its own currency, the koruna, while trading largely in euros, and EU funds are supporting upgrades to transport corridors and energy networks.

The overall risk picture

Czechia's risk profile is that of a stable EU, NATO and Schengen member with moderate public debt, low inflation and a deep industrial base. Inflation of 2.4% in October 2026 is close to target, debt was 44.2% of GDP in 2025, and the current account recorded a surplus of 0.7% of GDP. The main near-term issues are the larger planned deficit in the 2027 budget and the central bank's November 2026 rate rise to 4.00%, which together point to tighter financing conditions. The government of Prime Minister Andrej Babiš, formed by ANO, SPD and the Motorists, survived a no-confidence vote in October 2026; it faces opposition criticism on conflict of interest and institutional independence, which investors should monitor without expecting abrupt changes to the open, EU-anchored business environment. Czechia is not subject to international sanctions, and standard EU sanctions and export-control rules apply to trade with Russia.

Business networks

The American Chamber of Commerce in the Czech Republic, the British Chamber of Commerce in the Czech Republic, the Czech-German Chamber of Industry and Commerce, the Economic Chamber of the Czech Republic and the Confederation of Industry are the main business networks, with active sector committees and regular dialogue with government. CzechInvest supports foreign investors and suppliers, while CzechTrade promotes Czech exporters abroad. The European Investment Bank and EU programmes managed by national ministries offer co-financing for qualifying projects. The commercial sections of the embassies of major EU partners, the United Kingdom, Japan, South Korea and the United States are useful sources of introductions.

Who this market suits

Czechia suits investors seeking a skilled, industrial base inside the EU and Schengen: automotive and component manufacturers serving German and European supply chains; electronics and semiconductor firms; engineering and nuclear suppliers positioning for the Dukovany programme; IT, software and business-services companies in Prague and Brno; and infrastructure and energy investors targeting EU-funded transport, grid and renewable projects. Investors with euro revenues and koruna costs should plan currency hedging.

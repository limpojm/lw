The economy today

Argentina in November 2026 is one of the most significant reform stories in the emerging world. Fiscal discipline has been firmly established, with a primary surplus of 1.4% of GDP and an overall surplus of 0.2% in 2025, the second consecutive year in surplus. Energy and mining are transforming the external accounts: oil output reached a record 904,000 barrels a day in May 2026, crude overtook corn and soy to become the top export, and the trade surplus approached a record $14 billion in the first half of 2026. The RIGI regime has approved $56 billion of large projects with around $150 billion more under review, Congress passed a landmark labour modernisation law in February 2026, and markets have responded: Fitch and S&P upgraded the sovereign to B- in May and June 2026, and country risk fell to an eight-year low of 421 points in July. GDP was 2% higher year-on-year in Q2 2026, although activity eased 0.6% on the quarter, with the IMF now forecasting 2.1% growth for 2026.

Argentina's main structural challenge is completing its macroeconomic normalisation after decades of high inflation and currency controls. Annual inflation stood at 33.4% in September 2026, but monthly core inflation has eased to 1.7% and the trend has broadly slowed since April. The central bank has bought $16.0 billion of foreign exchange so far in 2026, gross reserves stand at about $49.6 billion and net reserves have risen by more than $20 billion on a cash basis since December 2023, supported by a $20 billion IMF programme of which around $15.8 billion has been disbursed. Recorded foreign direct investment was a modest $3.1 billion in 2025, partly reflecting one-off debt cancellations, and some corporate FX restrictions remain. These are precisely the areas in which progress is now visible: energy companies resumed dividend remittances in 2026 for the first time since 2019, and the government reports that 2026–27 foreign-currency debt maturities are already funded. For investors, the opportunity is to establish positions ahead of further normalisation, with RIGI providing long-term protection for large projects.

How do I get in?

PromArgentina (Argentine Agency for the Promotion of Investments and International Trade) supports foreign investors with market intelligence and connections to opportunities across regions and sectors, working alongside Argentina's embassy network

Incentive Regime for Large Investments (RIGI), created under the 2024 Ley Bases, offering long-term tax, customs and foreign-exchange benefits for qualifying large projects; $56 billion of projects approved as of 1 October 2026, with a further ~$150 billion under evaluation

Flexible exchange-rate band since April 2025, with most currency controls lifted; companies may remit dividends through the official FX market for fiscal years starting on or after 1 January 2025, while legacy dividends and pre-December 2023 debts are channelled through BOPREAL central bank bonds

Provincial authorities as the gateway to hydrocarbon and mining concessions, often in partnership with YPF for oil and gas or with established operators in copper and lithium

United States–Argentina Agreement on Reciprocal Trade and Investment, signed 5 February 2026, lowering barriers in areas including motor vehicles and agricultural goods

EU–Mercosur Partnership Agreement, provisionally applied from 1 May 2026, opening preferential access between Argentina and the EU market

Trade and market access

Argentina is a founding member of Mercosur, giving preferential access to Brazil, Paraguay and Uruguay, and a WTO member. Its trade reach is widening: the EU–Mercosur Partnership Agreement has applied provisionally since 1 May 2026, and the Agreement on Reciprocal Trade and Investment with the United States was signed in February 2026. In 2025 exports reached $87.1 billion, led by Brazil (14.7%), China (11.3%), the EU (9.7%) and the United States (9.6%), with large bilateral surpluses with Chile, India and Peru. Agricultural exports flow through the Paraná river ports around Rosario, while new Atlantic export capacity for oil and LNG is being built on the Río Negro coast, adding energy to Argentina's established role as a global food supplier.

The overall risk picture

Argentina's risk profile is improving rapidly from a historically challenging base. Two consecutive years of fiscal surplus, steady reserve accumulation, rating upgrades to B- and an eight-year low in country risk show that the stabilisation programme is gaining credibility, while record energy production and trade surpluses are building a durable hard-currency base. Investors should still plan for inflation that, while falling, remains well above regional levels, for residual corporate FX rules on legacy positions, and for the policy sensitivity of the 2027 electoral cycle. These risks are manageable through inflation-indexed contracting, careful structuring with local counsel, multilateral co-financing and use of the RIGI framework, which offers long-term tax, customs and FX stability for qualifying projects. For investors willing to engage early, Argentina offers exposure to one of the world's leading shale basins, a major lithium and copper pipeline and a deep agribusiness sector at a point of improving fundamentals.

Business networks

The American Chamber of Commerce in Argentina and the British–Argentine Chamber of Commerce are the leading international business associations, and the EU Delegation hosted an EU Investment and Business Forum in Argentina in 2026. PromArgentina and Argentine embassies organise investment seminars, business visits and sector briefings, and major provinces run their own investment promotion offices. Mining and energy events, notably in San Juan and Neuquén, bring together operators, suppliers and officials. The World Bank, IFC, Inter-American Development Bank and CAF are active in infrastructure, energy and private-sector finance, with IFC and the World Bank in financing discussions on major lithium development.

Who this market suits

Argentina best suits investors with a medium- to long-term horizon who can benefit from an economy in active normalisation: energy investors in Vaca Muerta upstream, midstream and LNG; mining companies and suppliers targeting lithium and copper, where the government projects combined exports of $32.7 billion within ten years; agribusiness and food-processing investors building on a world-class export base; and infrastructure, logistics and power investors serving these sectors. Large-ticket projects benefit most from RIGI's stability guarantees, while technology and services investors can draw on a skilled workforce in Buenos Aires and Córdoba. Consumer-facing investors should calibrate entry to the pace of disinflation and domestic recovery.

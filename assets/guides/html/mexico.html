Investor Entry Routes

  • Secretaría de Economía (Ministry of Economy) leads investment attraction and trade policy, including a new single investment window (ventanilla única) announced in May 2026 targeting authorisations within 90 days, and under 30 days in specific cases
  • Plan México, the federal industrial strategy, combining infrastructure investment, regulatory simplification and incentives to raise domestic production and exports
  • Polos de Desarrollo Económico para el Bienestar offering a 100% immediate deduction for new fixed assets and additional 25% deductions for incremental training and R&D spending, for fiscal years 2025–2030 (decree of 22 May 2025)
  • Energy-sector participation through CFE mixed-investment calls offering contracts of up to 25 years (CFE buying 70% of output), alongside fully private renewable and battery-storage calls
  • Free-floating peso under Banco de México's credible inflation-targeting framework, with no general restrictions on profit repatriation
  • USMCA rules-of-origin compliance as the gateway to tariff-free access to the US and Canada, complemented by the EU–Mexico Modernised Global Agreement signed in May 2026

Market-Entry Observation

Mexico in October 2026 combines record foreign investment, a recovering economy and strong macroeconomic anchors. GDP grew 1.5% quarter-on-quarter in Q2 2026 (2.1% year-on-year), led by services and agriculture, following growth of 0.8% in 2025. Foreign direct investment reached a record $40.9 billion in 2025, up 10.8%, with new investment more than doubling to $7.4 billion, and a record $35.0 billion arrived in H1 2026, 38.6% of it in manufacturing. Inflation stood at 3.45% in September within Banco de México's target range, the policy rate is 6.50% after an extended easing cycle, international reserves reached a record $258.6 billion in August, and a $24 billion IMF Flexible Credit Line reflects what the IMF calls very strong policy frameworks. Under Plan México the government has launched a single investment window, tax incentives for new development poles and private energy calls that attracted offers of 34 GW against 7 GW sought.

Mexico's main structural consideration is its close integration with the United States, which takes more than 80% of its exports. At the first USMCA joint review on 1 July 2026 the US declined to confirm a 16-year extension, triggering annual reviews; the agreement remains fully in force, runs to 2036 and can be extended at any review, and USMCA-compliant goods, around 85% of exports to the US, retain preferential access. US Section 232 tariffs of 50% on steel and aluminium and 25% on non-compliant autos are the focus of bilateral negotiations, which Mexico describes as constructive. Mexico is diversifying actively, signing the Modernised Global Agreement with the EU in May 2026 and raising tariffs on imports from non-FTA countries to strengthen North American supply chains. On the fiscal side, the deficit is being reduced gradually from 5.8% of GDP in 2024 to a projected 3.9% in 2027, with public debt stable at around 54–55% of GDP and largely fixed-rate and in local currency, supporting Mexico's investment-grade ratings.

Investor Risk Note

Mexico's risk profile is that of a large, deeply integrated manufacturing economy with credible macroeconomic institutions and record investment inflows. The IMF has highlighted Mexico's flexible exchange rate, credible inflation targeting, fiscal responsibility law and well-regulated financial sector, and record reserves of $258.6 billion provide a substantial cushion. The main considerations are the USMCA annual review cycle and US sectoral tariffs, a gradual fiscal consolidation that includes continued support for Pemex, regional security variation and changes to judicial and regulatory structures. Each is being actively managed: USMCA-compliant trade retains preferential access, negotiations with the US are ongoing, the deficit is on a declining path and official homicide data show sustained improvement. Investors should embed USMCA compliance and tariff scenarios in planning, conduct location-specific due diligence and use robust contractual protections, while positioning to benefit from nearshoring, Plan México incentives and a potential USMCA extension.

Market Access

Mexico is a party to the United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA), giving tariff-free access for compliant goods to a combined North American market, and is a founding WTO member and a member of the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) and the Pacific Alliance. The Modernised Global Agreement with the EU, signed on 22 May 2026, broadens access in a relationship worth €86.8 billion in goods trade in 2025. Exports reached a record $664.8 billion in 2025, with manufacturing accounting for 91.6% and more than 80% going to the United States. Northern land border crossings, the Bajío rail and highway corridors and Pacific and Gulf ports, including the Interoceanic Corridor, connect Mexican production to North American, European and Asian markets.

Current International Business Channels

The American Chamber of Commerce of Mexico, the British Chamber of Commerce in Mexico and European chambers provide well-established networks for foreign investors, while the Consejo Coordinador Empresarial (CCE) and the industrial confederation CONCAMIN are the main domestic business voices and partners in Plan México. More than 11,000 EU businesses already operate in Mexico, and state-level economic development secretariats in Nuevo León, the Bajío and Jalisco actively court investors with site and incentive support. Development banks Nacional Financiera and Bancomext support supply-chain and export finance, and the Inter-American Development Bank, IFC and World Bank are active in infrastructure, energy and private-sector development.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Mexico is best suited to manufacturers seeking USMCA-compliant production close to the US market, particularly in automotive, electronics, medical devices, aerospace and electrical equipment; industrial real-estate and logistics investors serving nearshoring demand in the north and the Bajío; renewable-energy and storage developers able to participate in CFE and private calls; and financial-services, technology and consumer investors targeting Mexico's large domestic market. Investors willing to locate in the new development poles or southern Mexico can access additional tax incentives and less congested labour markets, while all investors should plan for USMCA review cycles and tariff scenarios.

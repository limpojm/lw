The economy today

Timor-Leste in October 2026 is entering a new phase of regional integration with a stable macroeconomic base. The economy grew 4.5% in 2025, its fastest pace since 2014, and the central bank projects 5% growth in 2026, while inflation has eased to 0.5% year-on-year in July 2026 in a fully dollarised economy. Timor-Leste became ASEAN's 11th member on 26 October 2025, following WTO accession in 2024, and will chair ASEAN in 2029. FDI inflows rose to $253 million in 2025 from $225 million in 2024, and connectivity is improving fast: the Timor-Leste South Submarine Cable began commercial operation in July 2026 with 27 Tbps of capacity, complementing the Tibar Bay Port. A Private Investment Law offering tax holidays of up to ten years and guaranteed profit repatriation provides a clear entry framework.

Timor-Leste's central structural task is the transition beyond its first generation of petroleum revenue. Bayu-Undan stopped producing in June 2025 and the state budget is financed largely by transfers from the Petroleum Fund, which the IMF recommends managing through gradual fiscal consolidation, including the introduction of VAT by 2027. The country approaches this transition from a position of considerable strength: the Petroleum Fund stood at $18.43 billion at end-June 2026, around seven times the proposed $2.57 billion 2027 budget, and has generated $12.65 billion in cumulative investment returns. The next growth platform is advancing too. Timor Gap agreed in September 2026 to acquire Osaka Gas's 10% stake in Greater Sunrise, lifting its holding to 66.56%, and negotiations with Australia on the field's mining code, production-sharing contract and fiscal regime have progressed. Together with plans to restart gas production near Bayu-Undan within three to four years, this creates openings in energy services alongside a broader diversification drive in tourism, agriculture and digital services.

Where are the opportunities?

Dili

Capital and commercial centre; seat of government, the main banking and services hub and landing point of the new international submarine cable

Services, retail, hospitality, construction, digital

Tibar Bay Port (west of Dili)

The country's first modern container terminal, a $490 million PPP under a 30-year concession and now the main maritime gateway

Port logistics, warehousing, import distribution

Greater Sunrise fields (Timor Sea)

Major gas and condensate resource 150 km offshore, with 5.13 trillion cubic feet of gas and 225.9 million barrels of condensate in contingent resources; Timor Gap is moving to a 66.56% stake

Gas, LNG, oilfield services, engineering

South coast (Natarbora)

Proposed landing point for gas from Bayu-Undan, Chuditch and nearby fields to support domestic processing industries, and Dili's preferred direction for a Greater Sunrise pipeline

Gas processing, construction, energy infrastructure

Bayu-Undan (Timor Sea)

Production ended in June 2025 after two decades; the government is preparing a strategy covering possible carbon capture and storage and a gas restart within three to four years

Carbon capture and storage, decommissioning services, gas

Oe-Cusse (ZEESM Special Economic Zone)

Exclave special economic zone offering extended tax holidays, simplified customs and pre-approved industrial land, within the highest-incentive investment zone

Agro-processing, light manufacturing, tourism

Ataúro Island

Island destination close to Dili that sits in the top incentive zone under the Private Investment Law, with the longest tax incentives and land concessions

Eco-tourism, hospitality, marine activities

How do I get in?

TradeInvest Timor-Leste (national investment promotion agency) certifies investors and coordinates Investment Certificates, working with SERVE, the one-stop business registry, where company registration takes around five days

Private Investment Law 15/2017 and its 2018 implementing decree, offering income-tax holidays of up to ten years, duty-free import of capital goods and a guarantee of free repatriation of profits and dividends

Location-based incentive zones (Dili; other municipalities; Ataúro and Oe-Cusse), with the Oe-Cusse Special Economic Zone (ZEESM) offering the most generous terms

US dollar as legal tender, removing exchange-rate risk, with Banco Central de Timor-Leste supervising the banking system; corporate income tax on non-petroleum activity is 10%

Petroleum-sector participation through licensing by the National Petroleum Authority and partnership with the national oil company Timor Gap, with the Greater Sunrise legal and fiscal framework being finalised with Australia

Public–private partnerships encouraged in infrastructure, power and education, building on the Tibar Bay Port precedent

Trade and market access

Timor-Leste became the 11th member of ASEAN on 26 October 2025, opening a path into a regional market of more than 650 million people as it phases into ASEAN economic agreements, and has been a WTO member since August 2024. It is also a member of the Community of Portuguese Language Countries (CPLP), linking it to Lusophone markets, and will chair ASEAN in 2029. Historically, petroleum from the Timor Sea was its dominant export, while non-oil exports, led by specialty coffee, reached $35.8 million in 2025. Tibar Bay Port is the main maritime gateway, and the country's first international submarine cable, connected via Australia, entered commercial service in July 2026, with links to Indonesia and other regional networks planned. Proximity to Darwin and Indonesia gives Timor-Leste a natural position between Australia and South-East Asia.

The overall risk picture

Timor-Leste's risk profile is that of a young, peaceful democracy with very low inflation, a dollarised economy and a substantial sovereign wealth fund. The end of Bayu-Undan production in 2025 marks a well-anticipated transition rather than a shock: the $18.43 billion Petroleum Fund, a clear IMF and World Bank reform agenda, and advancing work on Greater Sunrise give the state substantial time and resources to broaden its revenue base. ASEAN membership since October 2025 and WTO membership are anchoring trade and regulatory reform, while the Private Investment Law's tax holidays and repatriation guarantee offer a predictable entry route. Investors should allow for a small domestic market, land-lease rather than freehold structures, developing infrastructure and possible tax changes such as the planned VAT, all of which can be addressed through careful structuring, local partnerships and early engagement with TradeInvest.

Business networks

The Timor-Leste Chamber of Commerce and Industry (CCI-TL) is the main domestic business association and a partner for matchmaking with local firms. The Darwin to Dili Business & Investment Summit and Invest Timor-Leste 2026 business forum, held in Dili on 27–30 October 2026 with TradeInvest, CCI-TL, the Property Council of Australia NT and Australian Government support, offers structured business-to-business meetings with Timorese companies and officials. The Australian Embassy is an active commercial partner, reflecting Australia's position as one of the main sources of FDI alongside Indonesia and the United States. The World Bank Group, ADB and IMF maintain active programmes in infrastructure, public finance and private-sector development, and ASEAN membership is opening new regional business networks.

Who this market suits

Timor-Leste best suits investors with a long-term horizon who value early-mover positioning in ASEAN's newest member: tourism and hospitality investors drawn by Ataúro, the coast and the government's tourism priority; agribusiness investors in specialty coffee and food processing; energy-services, engineering and carbon-capture specialists positioning for Greater Sunrise and Bayu-Undan; and infrastructure, logistics and telecoms investors building on the Tibar Bay Port PPP and the new submarine cable. Development finance institutions and impact investors can play a catalytic role, while dollarisation and guaranteed profit repatriation give all investors a simple currency environment.

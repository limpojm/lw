The economy today

Namibia in November 2026 offers political stability, sound institutions and one of Africa's most significant emerging energy frontiers. Growth has picked up: GDP rose 4.8% year-on-year in Q2 2026, the strongest pace since early 2024, after 3.1% in Q1, led by services (+6.1%), agriculture (+17.8%) and manufacturing (+3.9%), and following 1.7% growth in 2025. Net FDI inflows reached N$6.6 billion in Q3 2025, up about 22% year-on-year and driven mainly by mining. Policy credibility has been reinforced: Namibia redeemed its $750 million Eurobond in October 2025, exited the FATF grey list in June 2026 ahead of schedule, and the Bank of Namibia raised the repo rate to 7% in October 2026, with reserves of N$58 billion (about $3.6 billion), or 3.6 months of import cover, at end-July 2026. Investment and Special Economic Zone legislation is under review, and TotalEnergies' Venus field, targeting about 150,000 barrels a day, is expected to reach a final investment decision in Q4 2026.

Namibia's central structural challenge is turning resource wealth into broad-based employment. Unemployment stood at 36.9% in the 2023 census labour report, and the IMF notes that growth has been concentrated in public and extractive sectors, while public debt is projected at around 66% of GDP in 2026 as the government absorbs lower customs-union (SACU) receipts. The policy response is practical and well defined: the FY2026/27 budget targets consolidation through wage-bill and medical-scheme reform, and the authorities are prioritising faster permitting, a workable local-content policy, digitalisation and skills alignment. The opportunity is substantial: offshore oil, with first Venus oil expected around 2030 and Mopane targeting a decision in 2027–28, green hydrogen, expanding uranium output and a fast-growing agricultural sector together create long-term demand for capital, skills, logistics and services partners.

How do I get in?

Namibia Investment Promotion and Development Board (NIPDB): national investment promotion agency, operating a One-Stop Centre to streamline procedures for investors

Namibia Investment Promotion and Facilitation Bill, under detailed review after public consultation, setting out performance-based incentives and the future framework for investor rights and sector access

Export Processing Zone regime (exemption from corporate tax, VAT, customs duty and stamp duty for qualifying exporters) and a Special Economic Zone Bill under legal review, with the SEZ tax regime also being reviewed

Corporate tax at 31%, with legacy registered-manufacturer incentives (18% rate and enhanced allowances) being phased out, and an 80% export allowance on qualifying manufactured exports

Namibian dollar pegged one-to-one to the South African rand within the Common Monetary Area, with exchange-control approvals handled by the Bank of Namibia through authorised dealers

Petroleum exploration licences awarded with the state oil company NAMCOR, which typically holds around 10% (as at Venus and Mopane), with upstream oversight coordinated by a petroleum unit in the Presidency

Exit from the FATF grey list in June 2026 after all 13 action items were addressed, easing correspondent banking and due-diligence processes for investors

Trade and market access

Namibia is a member of the Southern African Customs Union (SACU), the Common Monetary Area and the Southern African Development Community (SADC), giving duty-free access to South Africa and the wider regional market, and is a party to the African Continental Free Trade Area and a WTO member. It trades with the EU under the SADC–EU Economic Partnership Agreement, and the US extended AGOA preferences through December 2028 in September 2026. Exports reached N$90.4 billion in 2025, led by uranium, gold, fish and diamonds, with South Africa (N$26.6 billion), China (N$22 billion), the EU (N$17.6 billion) and Botswana (N$9.3 billion) the main markets. The port of Walvis Bay and its road and rail corridors link Namibia to Botswana, Zambia, the DRC and South Africa, positioning the country as an Atlantic gateway for southern Africa.

The overall risk picture

Namibia's risk profile is that of a stable, well-governed democracy with credible institutions, a currency anchored to the rand and a financial sector described by the IMF as liquid and well capitalised. Recent milestones, including the full repayment of the 2025 Eurobond and an ahead-of-schedule exit from the FATF grey list in June 2026, show policy delivery. The principal considerations are fiscal pressure from lower SACU receipts, high unemployment and a growth base still concentrated in mining, together with the timing of offshore oil investment decisions and the finalisation of new investment, SEZ and empowerment legislation. Each is being addressed through budget consolidation, regulatory reform and diversification into energy, agriculture and services. Investors should plan for evolving local-content and empowerment requirements and build realistic timelines for oil and hydrogen projects, while recognising that Namibia's frontier energy resources and strategic position on the Atlantic coast offer long-term upside.

Who should I talk to first?

Office of the President (Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah)

Sets national economic priorities and hosts the upstream petroleum unit overseeing oil and gas development

Primary reference point for Namibia's strategic direction and energy policy

Namibia Investment Promotion and Development Board (NIPDB)

National investment promotion agency running the One-Stop Centre and supporting economic diversification

Standard first point of contact for market entry, incentives and project facilitation

Bank of Namibia

Monetary authority; maintains the peg to the rand, sets the repo rate and administers exchange controls

Relevant for currency, banking and capital-repatriation matters

Ministry of Finance

Leads fiscal policy, tax reform and the investment-related tax and SEZ regimes, and led the FATF exit process

Key counterpart on tax incentives, budget priorities and financing frameworks

Ministry of Industries, Mines and Energy (Petroleum Commissioner)

Licenses mining and petroleum activity and approves upstream transactions such as the Mopane operatorship transfer

Essential for licensing and regulatory engagement in mining and energy

NAMCOR (National Petroleum Corporation of Namibia)

State oil company holding interests in the leading offshore licences, including 10% in Venus and Mopane

Central partner for any oil and gas or downstream energy engagement

Namport and the Walvis Bay Corridor Group

Port authority and public-private corridor body developing Walvis Bay as a regional logistics gateway

Key counterparts for logistics, port and transit-trade investment

Business networks

The Namibia Chamber of Commerce and Industry and the Namibian Employers' Federation are the principal domestic business associations and are active in consultations on investment, labour and empowerment policy. NIPDB organises investor engagement and delegation programmes, and the Walvis Bay Corridor Group convenes port, freight and logistics operators. The EU Delegation, which reports a strong and growing EU–Namibia trade relationship, and the major international oil companies active offshore are useful sources of practical market intelligence. The IMF, World Bank and African Development Bank are engaged on fiscal reform, private-sector development and energy, and the Development Bank of Namibia provides co-financing for local projects.

Who this market suits

Namibia suits investors with a medium- to long-term horizon who value stability and a rules-based environment: oil and gas service, logistics and engineering firms positioning ahead of the Venus and Mopane developments; mining and nuclear-fuel investors drawn to world-class uranium assets; renewable energy and green hydrogen developers seeking exceptional solar and wind resources; and agriculture, food-processing and fisheries investors building on strong recent sector growth and access to SACU, EU and US markets. Investors who can bring skills transfer and local partnerships will find the strongest alignment with government priorities.

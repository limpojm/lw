The economy today

Afghanistan's economy has continued to grow under difficult conditions. The World Bank estimates real GDP growth of 4.8% in FY2025, led by services (around 10.3%) and industry (around 10%), with mining output having grown by roughly 25–30% a year in 2022–2024, and projects growth of around 4% in FY2026. The afghani appreciated by about 10% over FY2025 and traded at around 65 to the dollar in August 2026, inflation stood at 6.7% year-on-year in August 2026, and domestic revenue reached 19.8% of GDP in 2025, with revenue broadly covering spending in 2026. Regional partners are pressing ahead with connectivity: around 80% of pipe-laying on the Herat section of the TAPI gas pipeline was complete by September 2026, and the Trans-Afghan Railway linking Uzbekistan to Pakistan's ports, now costed at about $7 billion, is due to complete its feasibility study by the end of 2026. Russia became the first state to formally recognise the Taliban government in July 2025, and at least 17 countries maintain embassies in Kabul.

The country's central structural challenge is its international legal position. The Taliban, the Haqqani Network and senior office-holders, including the Prime Minister, are subject to UN, US and UK sanctions; the authorities are not recognised by the UN or by Western governments; about $7 billion of central bank reserves have been frozen since 2021; and banking remains thin, with private credit at around 2.5% of GDP. These realities are compounded by the closure of the Pakistan border since October 2025 and the return of some 3.7 million Afghans from Iran and Pakistan, which lowered GDP per capita by about 5.6% despite growth. Afghanistan has shown resilience by rerouting trade through Central Asia and Iran and redirecting food exports toward India, and established licensing routes allow lawful commercial trade. For most international investors the opportunity is therefore twofold: carefully structured, sanctions-compliant participation in permitted trade and regional infrastructure today, and early readiness for any future normalisation.

How do I get in?

No sanctions-free general route exists: all engagement must be structured around the UN 1988 Taliban sanctions regime and US, UK and EU measures, beginning with legal and sanctions counsel

US OFAC General License 20 authorises commercial transactions involving Afghanistan and its governing institutions, including Da Afghanistan Bank and state-owned enterprises, and payment of taxes and fees, but not transfers to the Taliban, the Haqqani Network or blocked individuals; General Licenses 14–19 cover humanitarian, NGO and remittance activity

The de facto authorities have retained the previous Law on Private Investment and the 2003 and 2015 banking laws, alongside a 2018 Islamic banking regulation, though application and enforcement are uneven

Ministry-led concessions and contracts for mining, power and infrastructure, including the 15-year extension of the Mes Aynak copper contract and nearly $11 billion of electricity contracts signed with the Ministry of Energy and Water and the power utility DABS in 2025

Participation in regional corridor projects through partner states and consortia, notably the TAPI pipeline and the Trans-Afghan Railway, whose feasibility study is due by the end of 2026

Da Afghanistan Bank manages a currency that has been stable to stronger, at around 65 afghani to the dollar in August 2026; international payments are constrained, much activity runs through hawala networks, and repatriation should be planned with banks experienced in Afghanistan compliance

Trade and market access

Afghanistan is a World Trade Organization member and sits at the junction of Central Asia, South Asia and the Middle East. Iran (31.1%), China (14.3%), Uzbekistan (9.7%), the UAE (9.5%) and India (9.3%) were the leading export markets in FY2026 to August, with Iran, China, the UAE, Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan the leading sources of imports. With the Pakistan border closed to trade since October 2025, Iranian routes (56.6%) and Central Asian corridors (38.9%) carried more than 95% of recorded trade in August 2026, and food exports have been redirected toward India. Two regional projects underpin long-term connectivity: the TAPI pipeline, planned to carry 33 bcm a year of Turkmen gas toward South Asia, and the Trans-Afghan Railway from Termez to the Pakistani rail network, which would give Central Asia access to Karachi, Gwadar and Port Qasim. As those projects and border normalisation advance, Afghanistan's transit role could expand substantially.

The overall risk picture

Afghanistan is a legally restricted market, and investors should treat compliance as the starting point of any engagement. The Taliban remain designated under US terrorism sanctions, the Haqqani Network is a US Foreign Terrorist Organisation, and the UN 1988 sanctions list, which takes automatic effect in the UK, includes the Prime Minister and other senior officials; the EU applies the UN regime and maintains its own measures. Only Russia formally recognises the authorities, the UN has not seated them, and about $7 billion of central bank reserves remain frozen. Nevertheless, lawful routes exist: OFAC General License 20 permits commercial transactions with Afghanistan and its governing institutions, including tax and fee payments, provided no funds reach the Taliban, the Haqqani Network or listed individuals. The economy has shown resilience, growing by an estimated 4.8% in FY2025 with a stable currency, despite the Pakistan border closure and large-scale returns. Investors should obtain specialist sanctions and legal advice, screen all counterparties continuously, confine activity to clearly permitted sectors, and treat Afghanistan as a market in which careful positioning today builds readiness for any future normalisation.

Business networks

International commercial engagement with Afghanistan currently runs mainly through regional channels. Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan and Kazakhstan are the most active economic partners, through the Trans-Afghan Railway, TAPI and northern trade corridors, and investors from the UAE, Qatar and Kazakhstan have expressed interest in rail financing. The Afghanistan Chamber of Commerce and Investment is the main domestic business association. Western governments have no formal relations with the authorities; the UK, for example, handles contacts through its Mission to Afghanistan based in Doha. Donors have discontinued most development projects but continue humanitarian and basic-services funding through UN agencies and NGOs, and the World Bank's monthly Afghanistan Economic Monitor is the most reliable source of current economic data.

Who this market suits

Afghanistan suits a narrow group of investors with high risk tolerance and strong compliance capability: regional trade, logistics and infrastructure investors, particularly from Central Asia and the Gulf, already engaged in northern corridors, TAPI and the Trans-Afghan Railway; suppliers of food, agricultural inputs, medicines and essential goods operating within humanitarian and commercial licences; and agro-processing businesses building on demand from India, China and the region. Most Western institutional investors are best served by a watching brief, with legal analysis and relationship-building through regional partners, so that they are ready to act quickly if sanctions, recognition or border conditions change.

The economy today

Hungary in November 2026 is in a period of significant positive change. Péter Magyar's Tisza party won the April 2026 election with 141 of 199 seats, a two-thirds majority, and the new government quickly agreed with the European Commission to unlock €16.4 billion in frozen EU funds; Parliament amended more than 100 laws over the summer, mostly on the judiciary and anti-corruption, and all milestones were reported as met by the 31 August deadline. GDP grew 1.5% year-on-year in the third quarter of 2026, inflation was low at 1.6% in September, and the Magyar Nemzeti Bank has cut its base rate four times this year to 5.50%. Hungary keeps a 9% corporate tax, and HIPA-supported projects reached €7.07 billion in 2025, led by Chinese battery and electric-vehicle investment.

Hungary's main structural challenge is repairing public finances while restoring growth. The new government revised the 2026 deficit estimate to 7.5% of GDP from the original 3.7% target and expects public debt to rise to 77.5% of GDP, from 74.6% in 2025; Fitch affirmed its BBB rating with a negative outlook in June 2026. The response is credible: the government is cutting state operating costs by about HUF 700 billion, targets a deficit of 4.9% in 2027, and aims to meet euro-entry criteria by around 2030, with possible adoption in 2032. The returning EU funds, better relations with Brussels and a review of previous large investment agreements should improve transparency and give investors a more predictable, rules-based environment.

How do I get in?

Hungarian Investment Promotion Agency (HIPA) supports foreign investors; in 2025 it helped launch 108 projects worth €7.07 billion, creating 18,227 jobs

A 9% corporate income tax, one of the lowest headline rates in the EU, with incentive agreements available for large projects

EU single-market and Schengen membership, with free movement of capital; the forint floats and the government aims to meet euro-entry criteria by around 2030

€16.4 billion of previously frozen EU funds (€10 billion recovery funds and €6.4 billion cohesion funds) agreed for release in May 2026, for energy grids, railways and housing

Industrial parks and municipal sites in established manufacturing regions, with transport links to Austria, Germany and the Balkans

Energy diversification projects, including new oil and gas supply routes, as Hungary reduces reliance on Russian energy

Trade and market access

As an EU member, Hungary gives investors tariff-free access to the EU single market and to all EU free trade agreements, with trade with third countries governed by the EU's common trade policy and WTO rules. Schengen membership allows passport-free movement across most of the EU. Hungary's manufacturing sector is closely integrated with German and wider European supply chains, especially in automotive and electronics. It sits on road, rail and Danube corridors linking Western Europe with the Balkans, Romania and Ukraine. In energy, MOL has been testing Croatia's Adria pipeline as an alternative crude supply route and has said its refineries should be able to run fully on non-Russian crude by the end of 2026.

The overall risk picture

Hungary's risk profile is improving. The April 2026 election produced a stable two-thirds majority for a government committed to EU cooperation, judicial reform and anti-corruption, and the release of €16.4 billion in EU funds follows. Hungary is not on the FATF grey list and remains investment grade, though Fitch's outlook is negative because the deficit has widened to an estimated 7.5% of GDP in 2026. Investors should note Hungary's continuing links with Russian energy: Russian oil and gas imports continue, the government has set 2035 as its deadline to end reliance on Russian energy, and the Paks II nuclear project is led by Rosatom; EU and US sanctions must be checked for any dealings with Russian counterparts. The new government is also reviewing earlier incentive agreements with Chinese battery and car makers. With these points managed, Hungary offers a low-tax EU base with improving governance.

Business networks

The American Chamber of Commerce in Hungary, the British Chamber of Commerce in Hungary, the German-Hungarian Chamber of Industry and Commerce and the Hungarian Chamber of Commerce and Industry are the main business associations, with regular dialogue with government. HIPA provides investor services and supplier-development programmes linking foreign investors with Hungarian firms. The European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, the European Investment Bank and the World Bank are active in energy, infrastructure and private-sector finance, and the release of EU funds will create new co-financing opportunities. Embassies of major EU partners and the United States maintain commercial sections that are a useful source of introductions.

Who this market suits

Hungary suits investors seeking a low-tax manufacturing base in the heart of Europe: automotive, battery and electronics manufacturers serving German and European supply chains; business-services and IT companies in Budapest and Debrecen; construction and engineering firms targeting EU-funded rail, grid and housing projects; and energy companies supporting diversification away from Russian supply. Investors should take sanctions advice before any engagement with Russian-linked energy projects, and expect a more transparent approach to state incentives under the new government.

The economy today

Russia remains one of the world's largest economies, with GDP of around $2.56 trillion in 2025, and in October 2026 it is in a phase of policy normalisation after the overheating of 2023–24. Growth slowed to 1.0% in 2025 from 4.9% in 2024, and Rosstat estimates GDP grew 0.6% in the first half of 2026, with the second quarter improving to 1.3% after a calendar-affected first quarter; the Bank of Russia projects 0.5–1% for 2026 and 1.5–2.5% for 2027. The central bank has cut its key rate from a historical high of 21% to 14%, holding it there again on 23 October 2026 with annual inflation estimated at 6.8% on 19 October and a 4% target for 2027; its next decision is due on 18 December. External buffers are substantial: international reserves stood at $729.6 billion on 23 October 2026, the current account surplus widened to $21.4 billion in the second quarter as energy export prices rose, and government debt was a modest 18.3% of GDP in 2025. Foreign direct investment remains in net outflow, although the outflow narrowed to about $1.4 billion in the first quarter of 2026 from $4.0 billion in the previous quarter.

Russia's defining structural feature for international investors is the legal environment created by the war in Ukraine. US persons are prohibited from new investment in Russia under Executive Order 14071, the EU adopted its 20th, 21st and 22nd sanctions packages in April, July and October 2026, and the UK announced its largest package since 2022 in February 2026, with further designations of banks, oil companies and vessels in August and October. Russia's counter-measures require Government Commission approval for exits by investors from "unfriendly" states, on terms that since October 2024 combine a minimum 60% discount with a 35% "voluntary" budget contribution. Diplomacy remains active: US envoy Steve Witkoff met President Putin on 21 October 2026, and Moscow has sought sanctions relief as part of an energy ceasefire, but no sanctions have been lifted and the EU's 22nd package of 12 October added 1,569 listings. For compliant investors, the opportunity lies in two areas: lawfully permitted activity such as food, agriculture and medicine, and careful readiness for any future easing, given Russia's scale, resource base and strong reserve position.

How do I get in?

Sanctions licensing route: US (OFAC), UK (OFSI) and EU national competent authorities issue general and specific licences; US General License 6D authorises certain transactions in agricultural commodities, medicine and medical devices

Government Commission for Control over Foreign Investments (chaired by the Minister of Finance): approval required under Russian counter-measures for share deals and exits involving investors from "unfriendly" states, with presidential consent for assets above RUB 50 billion

Bank of Russia currency regime: the rouble floats, but proceeds and payments due to investors from "unfriendly" states are typically credited to special rouble accounts in Russia and cannot be freely transferred abroad

Case-by-case "return" framework announced by the Government in March 2025, under which a special commission will assess applications from foreign companies seeking to re-enter, prioritising those that preserved jobs and technology

Permitted-sector participation for existing operators in food, agriculture, medicine and other humanitarian supply chains, structured within the licence and exemption perimeter of each investor's home jurisdiction

Normalisation-readiness route: maintaining compliant legal entities, contracts and relationships so that investors are positioned to act promptly if and when sanctions are lawfully eased

Trade and market access

Russia is a member of the Eurasian Economic Union (EAEU) alongside Kazakhstan, Belarus, Armenia and Kyrgyzstan, and a member of the WTO and BRICS. Since 2022 its trade has reoriented decisively from Europe towards Asia, with China, India and Türkiye now its principal partners, and with Asia-facing routes through the Far East ports, the Northern Sea Route and the International North–South Transport Corridor gaining in importance. Exports rose 27.8% year-on-year to $40.9 billion in August 2026 on higher energy prices, with fuels and energy making up the majority of exports, followed by metals, chemicals, machinery and agricultural products. For Western investors, access to the Russian market is shaped primarily by home-country sanctions and export controls rather than by tariffs, and the EU and UK continue to widen restrictions on imports of Russian goods, including metals and LNG-related services, so trade activity requires careful product-by-product compliance review.

The overall risk picture

Russia offers scale, a large resource base and strong external buffers — reserves of $729.6 billion, a widening current account surplus and government debt of 18.3% of GDP — alongside a central bank that has cut its key rate from 21% to 14% and is targeting 4% inflation in 2027. These strengths currently sit within a highly restrictive legal environment that investors must treat as the starting point for any analysis. US, EU and UK sanctions prohibit most new Western investment and have continued to expand through 2026, US secondary-sanctions authorities extend risk to third-country counterparties, and Russia's own counter-measures make exits by investors from "unfriendly" states slow, approval-dependent and very costly, with proceeds generally held onshore in roubles. Active diplomacy, including US-led talks in October 2026, creates a credible possibility of future change, but no sanctions have been eased and timing cannot be predicted. Investors should therefore confine activity to what is lawfully permitted in each relevant jurisdiction, take specialist sanctions and Russian-law advice before any step, and use this period to prepare well-documented scenario plans for any future lawful normalisation.

Who should I talk to first?

Office of the President (Vladimir Putin)

Sets strategic and foreign policy, including the negotiation stance on Ukraine and sanctions relief; presidential consent is required for exits above RUB 50 billion

Key reference point for assessing the direction of any future normalisation

Government Commission for Control over Foreign Investments (Ministry of Finance)

Approves share transactions and exits involving investors from "unfriendly" states, setting discount and budget-contribution terms

Essential counterpart for existing investors managing or restructuring Russian assets, via Russian counsel

Bank of Russia

Monetary authority and regulator of the currency regime; key rate held at 14% on 23 October 2026, with next decision on 18 December; subject to US, EU and UK restrictions

Relevant for understanding rouble, capital-control and banking conditions

Government of the Russian Federation (Prime Minister Mikhail Mishustin)

Announced the case-by-case framework for foreign companies seeking to return to the Russian market

Relevant for investors assessing future re-entry options

Ministry of Economic Development

Leads economic forecasting and investment-climate policy

Useful source of official outlook and sector policy

US Treasury (OFAC), UK OFSI and EU national competent authorities

Administer the sanctions regimes and issue general and specific licences governing any Russia-related activity

First point of contact, through counsel, for licence applications and compliance guidance

Rosneft and Lukoil

Russia's two largest oil companies, both designated by the US in October 2025; OFAC licences cover only limited activities, such as certain Lukoil retail stations outside Russia

Monitor only; any engagement requires explicit licence coverage

Business networks

The Association of European Businesses and the American Chamber of Commerce in Russia continue to serve member companies that maintain operations in Russia, and are useful sources of practical intelligence on the evolving regulatory and counter-measures environment, alongside the Russian Union of Industrialists and Entrepreneurs for domestic business liaison. The St Petersburg International Economic Forum and the Eastern Economic Forum in Vladivostok are the principal government-led investment events, although attendance and any engagement with designated persons require prior sanctions screening. Western multilateral development banks, including the EBRD and World Bank, have suspended new activity in Russia since 2022. For most international investors the most valuable channels at present are specialist sanctions and Russian-law advisers, home-government licensing authorities and industry associations tracking the negotiation track.

Who this market suits

Russia currently suits a narrow but well-defined set of investors: companies already operating in Russia in permitted sectors such as food, agriculture, medicine and consumer staples, which can continue under the relevant licences and exemptions; long-horizon strategic investors and brand owners who exited or paused operations and wish to monitor the Government's case-by-case return framework while preparing for any lawful normalisation; and investors from jurisdictions that do not impose sanctions, who must still assess carefully their exposure to US secondary sanctions and EU and UK restrictions. All investors should treat sanctions compliance, counter-measures exposure and onshore cash management as core elements of any Russia strategy.

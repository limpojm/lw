The economy today

Bosnia and Herzegovina offers investors a low-tax, low-cost manufacturing and energy base on the EU's border, with a currency firmly pegged to the euro. GDP grew 2.4% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, up from 2.2% in the first quarter, led by information and communication and consumption. Foreign direct investment inflows were KM 1.41 billion (about €721 million) in 2025, led by investors from Germany, Serbia and Austria, and the EU accounts for over 60% of the cumulative FDI stock. The 10% corporate tax, one of the lowest in Europe, a currency board that removes exchange-rate risk against the euro and public debt estimated at around 35–40% of GDP give a stable foundation, and S&P rates the country B+ with a stable outlook.

Bosnia and Herzegovina's main structural challenge is a complex, decentralised constitutional system that slows decision-making and EU reforms. The first EU intergovernmental conference has not been held since negotiations were approved in March 2024, and the Commission has warned that €373.9 million of Growth Plan funds could be lost if reform steps are not completed by 31 December 2026. Inflation rose to 6.0% in September 2026, driven by transport and energy costs. The 4 October 2026 general election offers a fresh start: the Commission expects new authorities to restore reform momentum, and the April 2026 agreement with Croatia on the Southern Gas Interconnection, worth up to $1.5 billion, shows that strategic projects can advance. Investors who structure carefully can capture opportunities in energy, manufacturing, ICT and infrastructure.

How do I get in?

Foreign Investment Promotion Agency (FIPA), the state-level agency established in 1998, provides free and confidential advice on legislation, company set-up, site identification and project implementation

The state law on foreign direct investment guarantees national treatment, free transfer of proceeds abroad and protection against expropriation, and lets investors keep the more favourable regime if laws change

A 10% corporate income tax and 17% VAT, with personal income tax of 10% in the Federation and 8% in Republika Srpska

A currency board pegging the convertible mark (KM) to the euro at 1.95583, administered by the Central Bank of Bosnia and Herzegovina

Customs exemption for equipment imported as part of a foreign investor's share capital, and free zones listed in FIPA's location database

Preferential trade access to the EU, CEFTA, EFTA and Turkey, giving duty-free access for most goods to the EU, the main investor and export market

EU candidate status since December 2022, with a European Council decision in March 2024 to open accession negotiations and up to €976.6 million available from the EU Reform and Growth Facility against reforms

Trade and market access

Bosnia and Herzegovina is an EU candidate with preferential, largely duty-free access to the EU market, which accounts for most of its trade and over 60% of its FDI stock. It is a member of the Central European Free Trade Agreement (CEFTA) and has free trade agreements with EFTA and Turkey, with preferential access to several other markets. The country borders Croatia, an EU and Schengen member, along a long frontier, and the Corridor Vc motorway is progressively linking the Sava valley, Sarajevo and the Adriatic port of Ploče. The Southern Gas Interconnection agreed with Croatia in April 2026 will connect Bosnia's gas system to the Krk LNG terminal, diversifying supply away from Russia.

The overall risk picture

Bosnia and Herzegovina's risk profile combines strong monetary stability, a low-tax regime and moderate public debt with a complex political system. The currency board has kept the convertible mark fixed to the euro, and around 81% of banking capital is foreign-owned. The 4 October 2026 general election produced preliminary results giving the three-member Presidency to Denis Bećirović, Darijana Filipović and Željka Cvijanović, and government formation at state and entity level now determines the pace of EU reforms. The main issues for investors are slow decision-making, the risk of losing EU Growth Plan funds and inflation of 6.0% in September 2026; each is being addressed through the EU framework and new energy and infrastructure projects. The United States lifted its sanctions on Milorad Dodik and associates in October 2025, but investors should still screen Republika Srpska counterparts against current US, UK and EU sanctions lists and take legal advice where needed.

Business networks

The Foreign Investors Council in Bosnia and Herzegovina, the American Chamber of Commerce and the Foreign Trade Chamber of Bosnia and Herzegovina are the main channels for dialogue with government, alongside the entity chambers of commerce and bilateral chambers from Germany, Austria and other partners. FIPA promotes the country at international events and maintains a database of investment locations, including free zones and industrial parks. The European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, the European Investment Bank, the World Bank and the Western Balkans Investment Framework finance energy, transport and private-sector projects, and the EU Growth Plan supports reform-linked investment. Embassies of EU partners, the UK and the United States provide commercial support and introductions.

Who this market suits

Bosnia and Herzegovina suits cost-conscious investors seeking a base close to EU markets with a euro-pegged currency: manufacturers in automotive components, metals, wood processing and agri-food serving German, Austrian and Croatian supply chains; renewable-energy and gas-infrastructure investors; ICT and software firms drawing on a growing talent pool; and infrastructure contractors and financiers in motorway and energy projects. Investors should be comfortable navigating entity-level rules and should screen counterparts against sanctions lists.

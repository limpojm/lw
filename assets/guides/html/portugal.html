The economy today

Portugal in November 2026 stands out in the euro area for combining above-average growth with budget surpluses and rapidly falling public debt. GDP grew 1.9% in 2025 and 2.4% year-on-year in the third quarter of 2026, with investment rebounding, and the government forecasts 2.3% growth for 2026. Public debt fell below 90% of GDP in 2025 for the first time in 16 years and is projected at 84.5% in 2027, when the draft budget targets a fifth consecutive surplus of 0.1% of GDP. Foreign investment is gaining scale: AICEP contracted a record €3.58 billion in 2025, Sines has attracted a pipeline of more than €25 billion in batteries, data centres and green hydrogen, and in October 2026 the government chose Lufthansa to buy 44.9% of TAP for €1.2 billion. Corporate tax is falling steadily towards 17% by 2028, and the election of António José Seguro as President in February 2026 with about 67% of the vote reinforced institutional stability.

Portugal's main structural challenge is building the infrastructure, housing and workforce needed to absorb this wave of investment. In May 2026 the Economy Minister said Sines no longer had capacity to host further large companies without upgrades to water, grid, waste and port facilities, and Storm Kristin caused about €6 billion of damage in central Portugal in January 2026. Inflation rose to 3.8% in October 2026, driven by energy prices. The policy response creates opportunities: the 2027 budget pairs fiscal prudence with lower taxes, incentives are focused on strategic export-oriented projects, a new customs office opens in Sines in January 2027, and reconstruction, grid, water and housing investment is a priority. Investors in utilities, construction, engineering and affordable housing are well placed to benefit.

How do I get in?

AICEP Portugal Global, the trade and investment agency, which contracted a record €3.58 billion of investment in 2025 with €803 million of incentives and commitments to 6,600 jobs

Strategic investment incentive contracts for large export-oriented projects, such as the €350 million of incentives agreed for CALB's Sines battery plant

Corporate income tax cut to 19% in 2026, falling to 18% in 2027 and 17% from 2028, with a 15% rate on the first €50,000 of profit for SMEs and small mid-caps

The IFICI regime, offering a 20% flat rate on income from qualifying professional activities for eligible new residents

Euro-area, EU single-market and Schengen membership, with free capital movement and the legal certainty of EU law

Privatisation and partnership opportunities, including the sale of a 44.9% stake in TAP Air Portugal to Lufthansa for €1.2 billion agreed in October 2026

Trade and market access

As an EU member, Portugal gives investors tariff-free access to the EU single market and all EU free trade agreements, with WTO membership and the EU common trade policy governing trade with third countries. Euro and Schengen membership remove currency risk and border checks within the area, and Spain is Portugal's closest market and supply-chain partner. Portugal's Atlantic position and the deep-water port of Sines link Europe with the Americas and Africa, while shared language and long-standing ties give companies a platform into Brazil and Portuguese-speaking African markets. Portugal and Spain have jointly proposed an €8 billion European AI gigafactory split between Sines and Abrantes, underlining the country's ambition as a digital and energy gateway.

The overall risk picture

Portugal has a low-risk profile as a long-standing EU, euro-area and Schengen member with improving public finances and stable institutions. Debt fell below 90% of GDP in 2025, the government targets a fifth consecutive surplus in 2027, and corporate tax is on a legislated path down to 17% by 2028. The main points to monitor are the government's lack of a parliamentary majority, which has slowed some reforms, energy-led inflation of 3.8% in October 2026, and capacity constraints at investment hubs such as Sines. Climate events, including the January 2026 storms and summer wildfires, are a growing operational consideration best managed through site selection and insurance. Portugal is a FATF member with no grey-list concerns, and investors benefit from EU law, the euro and the Eurosystem framework.

Key counterpart on tax, fiscal and privatisation matters

Relevant for strategic projects and industrial location

Important for institutional stability and the legislative process

TAP Air Portugal

National airline in which Lufthansa is to acquire a 44.9% stake, with plans to expand the fleet and regional connectivity

Partner for aviation, tourism and connectivity investment

Business networks

The Portugal-UK Chamber of Commerce, the American Chamber of Commerce in Portugal, the Portuguese-German Chamber of Industry and Commerce and the Franco-Portuguese Chamber of Commerce and Industry are active bilateral business associations, alongside the Confederation of Portuguese Business (CIP) and the Portuguese Chamber of Commerce and Industry. AICEP runs investor services, trade missions and international promotion through its network of overseas offices. The European Investment Bank and EU recovery and cohesion programmes co-finance energy, transport and innovation projects, and EU solidarity funding supports storm reconstruction. Embassies of major partners maintain commercial sections that are a useful source of introductions.

Who this market suits

Portugal suits investors seeking a stable, lower-cost EU and euro-area base with a falling tax burden: battery, clean-tech and data-centre investors at Sines; export manufacturers in the North and Centre; technology, digital and shared-services companies in Lisbon and Porto drawing on skilled graduates and the IFICI regime; renewable-energy and green-hydrogen developers; and infrastructure, construction and housing investors responding to reconstruction and capacity needs. Its links with Brazil and Portuguese-speaking Africa add value for companies with Atlantic ambitions.

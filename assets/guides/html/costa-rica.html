Investor Entry Routes

  • CINDE (Costa Rican Investment Promotion Agency), a private non-profit body, provides free facilitation, site selection and sector guidance for foreign investors
  • PROCOMER (Foreign Trade Promoter) administers the Free Trade Zone Regime, offering 100% tax exemptions; large manufacturing projects of $10 million or more receive full income-tax exemption for eight years and 50% for a further four
  • Additional incentive programmes (inward processing, duty drawback and tourism development) available equally to foreign and domestic investors
  • Free capital movement with no foreign-exchange controls; the colón operates under a flexible exchange-rate regime overseen by the Banco Central de Costa Rica (BCCR)
  • Equal legal treatment of foreign and domestic investors, reinforced by OECD membership since May 2021 and adherence to the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention
  • An extensive trade-agreement network (15 FTAs covering more than 57 partners), allowing free zone operations to serve the US, EU, UK and regional markets
  • Sector-entry routes through concessions and public-private partnerships for infrastructure, and partnerships with state enterprises in energy and fuels

Market-Entry Observation

Costa Rica in October 2026 is one of Latin America's most consistent performers, combining OECD-standard institutions with a high-value export base. Real GDP grew 4.6% in 2025, led by free zone goods exports, and the BCCR projects 3.4% growth in 2026 and 3.5% in 2027. Exports reached a record $35.2 billion in 2025 (+11%), with medical devices alone exceeding $10.8 billion. Foreign direct investment totalled $5.12 billion in 2025, the second consecutive year above $5 billion, with reinvestment by established investors up 26% to $4.33 billion, a clear signal of confidence. Inflation turned positive in September 2026 (0.34%) after 16 months of falling prices, allowing the BCCR to cut its policy rate to 3.0% in July. S&P upgraded the sovereign to BB in October 2025, Moody's rates it Ba2, gross reserves reached a record $17.1 billion at end-2025, and an IMF Flexible Credit Line of about $1.5 billion provides an additional precautionary buffer.

Costa Rica's principal structural challenge is public security. Organised-crime activity has increased in recent years, and the country recorded 873 homicides in 2025. The response is gathering pace: homicides fell by around 20% in the first two months of 2026, specialised organised-crime courts and longer investigation periods have been introduced, and President Laura Fernández, who took office in May 2026, has made security a central priority. For investors the practical impact is concentrated in specific localities rather than in the free zone parks and business districts where most foreign operations are based, and standard security planning is sufficient for most projects. The improving trend, alongside a parallel fiscal-consolidation effort under the fiscal rule, also creates opportunities in security technology, logistics screening and port modernisation.

Investor Risk Note

Costa Rica's risk profile reflects a stable, rules-based democracy with OECD membership, strong institutions and a proven ability to attract and retain high-value foreign investment. The 2025 results, with 4.6% growth, record exports and more than $5 billion of FDI, demonstrate the resilience of the free zone model, and rating upgrades from S&P and Moody's, record reserves and a precautionary IMF Flexible Credit Line underline the improvement in macroeconomic credibility. The main areas to monitor are public security, where the trend has begun to improve and the new government has made enforcement a priority; US trade policy, where the 12.5% tariff applies only to some product lines; and public debt of around 61% of GDP, which is being managed under a fiscal rule. Investors should complete product-level tariff reviews, build standard security planning into site selection and work through CINDE and PROCOMER to manage administrative timelines; with these steps in place, Costa Rica remains one of the most predictable investment destinations in the Americas.

Market Access

Costa Rica is a founding member of the WTO and has 15 free trade agreements giving preferential access to more than 57 partners, including the CAFTA-DR agreement with the United States, the EU–Central America Association Agreement and the UK–Central America Association Agreement in force since January 2021, as well as agreements with Canada, Mexico, China and, since 2024, Ecuador and the UAE. In May 2026 CPTPP members welcomed the substantial conclusion of Costa Rica's accession negotiations; the government is consulting productive sectors before signature and legislative approval. Medical devices (31% of exports), business services, digital services, pineapples and bananas lead an export basket worth a record $35.2 billion in 2025, with the United States as the principal market. Caribbean ports at Limón and Moín and the Pacific port of Caldera connect Costa Rica to both oceans, positioning it as a nearshoring platform for North American and European supply chains.

Current International Business Channels

The American Chamber of Commerce of Costa Rica (AmCham), the Costa Rican Chamber of Exporters (Crecex) and the Free Zones Association (Azofras) are the principal business associations for foreign investors, alongside the Costa Rican Union of Chambers and Associations of the Private Business Sector (UCCAEP) for wider private-sector liaison. CINDE and PROCOMER run investor events, sector briefings and the Talent Costa Rica job fair, which has offered more than 8,000 vacancies. Established multinationals in the free zones, including several UK-linked businesses such as AstraZeneca, Diageo, Haleon and Smith & Nephew, are a valuable source of practical intelligence. The Inter-American Development Bank, IDB Invest, the World Bank and the Central American Bank for Economic Integration are active in infrastructure, energy and private-sector financing.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Costa Rica suits investors seeking a stable, high-skill base in the Americas with strong institutions: medical-device and life-sciences manufacturers drawn by a mature cluster and the Free Trade Zone Regime; business-services, shared-service and digital companies benefiting from bilingual talent and time-zone alignment with North America; nearshoring investors serving US and European supply chains through CAFTA-DR and the EU and UK association agreements; and tourism, real-estate and renewable-energy investors building on Guanacaste and the country's near-fully renewable grid. Agro-industrial exporters and infrastructure investors through concessions and public-private partnerships will also find attractive opportunities, particularly in regional free zones outside the Central Valley.

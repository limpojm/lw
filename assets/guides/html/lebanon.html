Investor Entry Routes

  • Investment Development Authority of Lebanon (IDAL), the national investment promotion agency, administering incentives under Investment Law No. 360 of 2001
  • Law 360 Package Deal Contracts for large projects (up to 100% corporate income tax and dividend tax exemption for up to 10 years, land registration fee exemption) and Investment Project by Zone incentives for SMEs, prioritising less-developed regions
  • Tripoli Special Economic Zone, a dedicated zone in the northern port city for export-oriented, logistics and light-industrial investment
  • Holding and offshore company regimes, exempt from corporate income tax and dividend withholding tax in return for capped or lump-sum annual taxes, suited to regional management and trading structures
  • Banque du Liban (BDL) currency framework: the economy now runs largely on new ('fresh') US dollars, kept separate from deposits frozen since 2019, with BDL Circulars 158 and 166 governing gradual withdrawals from legacy accounts
  • Upstream petroleum licensing through the Lebanese Petroleum Administration, with a fourth offshore exploration round being prepared on amended terms
  • EU–Lebanon Association Agreement (in force since 2006) and Agadir Agreement membership (since 2020), supporting preferential access for goods produced in Lebanon

Market-Entry Observation

Lebanon entered 2026 on a clear recovery path. The World Bank estimates real GDP grew 4.2% in 2025, the strongest since the 2019 financial crisis began, and the government recorded an overall fiscal surplus of 3.9% of GDP thanks to better tax, customs and VAT collection, a gain that continued into the first half of 2026. Foreign direct investment rose 15.7% to $2.04 billion in 2025, according to UNCTAD. The Aoun–Salam government elected in 2025 has advanced key reforms: in August 2026 Parliament passed amendments to the bank resolution law, which the IMF called a "major step", and Finance Minister Yassine Jaber is now seeking a new staff-level agreement with the IMF, with sources indicating it could come by the end of 2026. In January 2026 the government awarded offshore Block 8 to TotalEnergies, Eni and QatarEnergy, renewing upstream interest.

Lebanon's main challenge is completing the resolution of its 2019 financial crisis while recovering from renewed hostilities between Israel and Hezbollah that began on 2 March 2026. A US-brokered ceasefire took effect on 16 April and a US-sponsored framework followed in June, although Israeli forces remain in a self-declared zone in the south and strikes continue; the World Bank now projects a 6.4% contraction in 2026 and inflation of 17.5%. The economy has shown resilience: the Lebanese pound has stayed stable, BDL foreign reserve assets have held at around $11.4–11.7 billion through 2026, and fiscal discipline has been maintained. The sovereign has been in default on its Eurobonds since March 2020, about $30 billion remains outstanding, and the Financial Stabilisation and Depositor Recovery ('financial gap') law, which would allocate losses officially estimated in 2022 at about $70 billion, has yet to pass. Its passage, an IMF programme and a durable security settlement would open the way to debt restructuring and reconstruction, a significant opportunity for well-prepared investors.

Investor Risk Note

Investors should treat compliance as the first consideration in Lebanon. Lebanon itself is not subject to comprehensive country-wide sanctions, but Hezbollah is a US-designated Foreign Terrorist Organization, is proscribed in its entirety in the UK, and has its military wing listed by the EU, and OFAC continues to designate Hezbollah-linked officials, companies and financiers, most recently in June 2026. Lebanon's FATF grey-listing and its place on the EU high-risk third-country list mean banks apply enhanced due diligence, while legacy deposits remain frozen and the sovereign has been in default since March 2020. These are manageable with specialist legal and sanctions advice, thorough beneficial-ownership screening, new-money banking arrangements and careful site selection away from the south. Within this framework the direction of travel is encouraging: growth was the strongest since 2019 in 2025, the budget is in surplus, the bank resolution law has been amended with IMF backing, direct US-sponsored talks with Israel are under way and a new IMF agreement is being sought. Investors who build in compliance and security planning now are well placed for reconstruction and recovery.

Market Access

Lebanon's EU–Lebanon Association Agreement, in force since 2006, underpins its largest trading relationship: the EU accounted for 32.8% of Lebanon's goods trade in 2025, supplying 37.7% of imports and taking 15% of exports. Lebanon joined the Agadir Agreement with Egypt, Jordan, Morocco and Tunisia in 2020, has signed the Pan-Euro-Mediterranean Convention on rules of origin, and is a member of the Greater Arab Free Trade Area, which gives access to Gulf and wider Arab markets. Lebanon is not yet a WTO member; it has been negotiating accession since 1999. The ports of Beirut and Tripoli and Beirut's international airport connect the country to the eastern Mediterranean, the Gulf and a large global diaspora that remains a key source of demand, capital and trade links.

Current International Business Channels

The Chamber of Commerce, Industry and Agriculture of Beirut and Mount Lebanon and its counterparts in Tripoli and other regions are the main business associations, alongside the American Chamber of Commerce in Lebanon and the Association of Banks in Lebanon for banking-sector liaison. IDAL provides investor facilitation and sector information. The IMF, which has a resident representative in Beirut, and the World Bank, whose Lebanon Economic Monitor tracks recovery and reconstruction needs, are central reference points, while EU institutions and Gulf partners are expected to be major sources of reconstruction finance. Lebanon's large and well-connected global diaspora is also a valuable channel for partnerships, market intelligence and co-investment.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Lebanon best suits investors with strong compliance capability, regional experience and a medium-to-long-term horizon: diaspora and regional investors with local networks; contractors and suppliers positioned for donor- and IMF-supported reconstruction; energy investors in offshore exploration, power and renewables; services, education, healthcare and technology firms drawing on Lebanon's skilled workforce; and hospitality investors ready for tourism's recovery once security improves. Specialist distressed-debt and banking investors may find opportunities in an eventual debt restructuring and bank recapitalisation, subject to rigorous legal and sanctions advice.

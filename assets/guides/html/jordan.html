Investor Entry Routes

  • Ministry of Investment, the main reference authority for investors, offering a Comprehensive Investment Service through a joint electronic licensing platform; official entities must decide within 15 working days or the application is deemed approved
  • Investment Environment Law of 2022 guaranteeing equal treatment of Jordanian and foreign investors, protection against expropriation without fair market compensation, and arbitration under UNCITRAL or ICC rules where contracts allow
  • Incentives under the same law, including customs exemptions on fixed assets and inputs, income-tax reductions of at least 30% for projects in less-developed regions or employing 250+ Jordanians, and seven-year protection from adverse legal changes for projects of JD5 million or more
  • Aqaba Special Economic Zone, administered by ASEZA with the Aqaba Development Corporation as master developer, offering 100% foreign ownership and port and logistics concessions
  • Development and free zones (including King Hussein Business Park and Zarqa Free Zone) with no foreign-capital limits and tax and customs exemptions for export and transit activity
  • Dinar pegged to the US dollar under Central Bank of Jordan oversight, with the law guaranteeing conversion and transfer of funds in and out of the Kingdom without delay
  • Public-private partnerships and joint ventures with state-linked partners such as Jordan Phosphate Mines, Arab Potash and the Social Security Investment Fund, as used for the National Water Carrier and the Aqaba railway

Market-Entry Observation

Jordan in October 2026 offers a stable, reform-minded economy that is gaining momentum. Real GDP grew 2.8% in 2025 and accelerated to 2.9% in Q1 and 3.0% in Q2 2026, with agriculture (+7.8%) and manufacturing (+6.2%) leading, while inflation remains low at 2.0% in the first half of 2026. Foreign direct investment rose 25.1% to $2.02 billion in 2025, the highest since 2017, with Gulf investors providing almost a third, and national exports grew 9.9% to JD9.6 billion. The IMF completed the fifth review of Jordan's Extended Fund Facility in June 2026, noting that all end-2025 quantitative targets were met, and S&P affirmed Jordan at BB- with a stable outlook in August 2026. The Economic Modernisation Vision has entered its second phase (2026–2029), backed by the 2022 Investment Environment Law and a €3 billion EU package for 2025–2027.

Jordan's main structural challenge is its reliance on imported energy, which meets around 96% of needs, alongside chronic water scarcity and exposure to regional events. Both were tested from late February 2026, when the Iran war briefly halted Israeli gas supplies and slowed tourism. Jordan responded quickly: backup fuels kept power flowing, gas supply has since resumed, the Central Bank introduced a JD760 million liquidity and sector-support package, and the IMF expects the economic impact to remain limited. Reserves stood at $26.1 billion at end-June 2026, covering 8.6 months of imports. The disruption has also highlighted Jordan's strategic value: as Gulf trade rerouted, Aqaba's transit container traffic rose 160.8% in January–July 2026. Long-term solutions are now moving into delivery, notably the National Water Carrier, a $5.8 billion desalination PPP backed by $1.8 billion from the US DFC, which is expected to supply about 40% of drinking water from 2030, and the $2.3 billion UAE–Jordan railway to Aqaba.

Investor Risk Note

Jordan's risk profile is that of a well-managed, stable economy in a complex neighbourhood. Its macroeconomic anchors are strong: a long-standing dollar peg backed by $26.1 billion of reserves, low inflation, an IMF programme on track through five consecutive reviews, and a stable BB- rating from S&P. The 2026 regional conflict tested the economy through energy, tourism and trade channels, and the response — alternative fuels, targeted Central Bank support and continued fiscal discipline — kept growth rising to 3.0% in Q2 2026. Reduced US development aid has been partly offset by a new $354 million four-year US agreement and the EU's €3 billion package. Investors should plan for periodic regional volatility, but can take confidence from Jordan's institutional continuity, deep international partnerships and the investor protections of the 2022 Investment Environment Law.

Market Access

Jordan combines preferential access to Western and Arab markets. Its free trade agreement with the United States was reinforced by a July 2026 reciprocal trade agreement capping tariffs at 10% and setting zero tariffs on apparel and textiles, Jordan's largest export sector; exports to the US exceeded $800 million in Q1 2026. The EU–Jordan Strategic and Comprehensive Partnership, signed in January 2025, underpins preferential EU access, and exports to the EU rose 39.3% in 2025. Membership of the Greater Arab Free Trade Area gives tariff-free access to Saudi Arabia and other Arab markets, while Syria is re-emerging as a market, with bilateral trade rising to JD334 million in 2025 from JD116 million in 2024. Aqaba links Jordan to Red Sea shipping, and overland routes to Iraq and Syria, together with the planned national rail network, position the Kingdom as a logistics gateway for the Levant and Iraq.

Current International Business Channels

The American Chamber of Commerce in Jordan and the Amman Chamber of Industry are well-established channels for business liaison, and the Jordan–EU Investment Conference of April 2026 was designed as a platform for flagship infrastructure and connectivity deals. Development finance is deep and active: the EBRD invested €201 million across ten projects in Jordan in 2025, mostly in the private sector, under its 2025–2030 country strategy, while the World Bank Group, EIB, Islamic Development Bank, Proparco, JICA, the OPEC Fund and the US DFC are all financing the National Water Carrier. The EU's €3 billion package for 2025–2027 and Gulf investors, notably from the UAE and Saudi Arabia, provide further co-financing and partnership routes.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Jordan suits investors seeking a stable, well-connected base for the Levant, Iraq and the Gulf: logistics and transport investors positioned around Aqaba and the planned rail network; infrastructure, water and renewable-energy investors drawn by large, DFI-backed PPPs; manufacturers in apparel, pharmaceuticals, food and chemicals benefiting from US, EU and Arab market access and development-zone incentives; mining and fertiliser investors building on world-class phosphate and potash resources; and ICT and services investors seeking an English-speaking, skilled workforce. Construction-materials and trading firms can also use Jordan as a platform for Syria's and Iraq's reconstruction demand.

Investor Entry Routes

  • Investment Encouragement Act 2021, which provides equal treatment for foreign investors, protection against arbitrary measures, and expropriation only for public purpose with prompt and adequate compensation
  • Investment Organ and Private Sector Development Authority, overseen by the Ministry of Investment and International Cooperation, operating a Single Window for licensing and incentives such as profit-tax exemptions of up to five years and customs exemptions on capital goods
  • Central Bank of Sudan registration, required before operating; profits and loan repayments may be transferred in convertible currency subject to Central Bank rules, with multiple exchange rates in practice
  • Public-Private Partnership Act 2021 and specialist investment courts; Sudan is an ICSID contracting state, so arbitration is available where a treaty or contract provides consent
  • Development-finance and humanitarian procurement channels, including African Development Bank grants and the UN's $2.9 billion 2026 Humanitarian Needs and Response Plan
  • Sanctions-compliant structuring: there is no comprehensive US embargo, but UN, US, UK and EU targeted listings, US Chemical Weapons Act measures and the EU ban on Sudanese gold define what is permissible

Market-Entry Observation

Sudan in October 2026 is a market in early stabilisation where government-administered areas are rebuilding. After contracting 13.5% in 2024, the economy returned to growth of 1.2% in 2025, with the African Development Bank projecting 2.1% in 2026 and 3.2% in 2027, and inflation forecast to fall from 150.2% in 2025 to 78.3% in 2026; official data showed annual inflation at 44.5% in May 2026. Gold output recovered to 70 tonnes in 2025, up from 64 tonnes in 2024. The army-aligned government returned to Khartoum in January 2026, the Central Bank resumed operations in the capital and Khartoum airport reopened to domestic flights on 1 February 2026. The Investment Encouragement Act 2021, a Single Window and Sudan's ICSID membership provide a legal base for investment, and the African Development Bank signed an $83 million grant in May 2026 for Sennar, Kassala and Blue Nile.

Sudan's defining challenge is the war between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), under way since April 2023. At least 59,000 people have been killed, about 14 million have been displaced and 33.7 million people need humanitarian assistance in 2026, with famine conditions in parts of the country. The army holds central and eastern Sudan, including Khartoum and Port Sudan, while the RSF controls most of Darfur and parts of the south. The Quad (US, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Egypt) is pressing for a 90-day humanitarian truce leading to a permanent ceasefire, though the army leadership rejected negotiations on 6 October 2026. Resilience is visible in about 3 million returns and the restart of administration in Khartoum. For investors, the opportunity lies in lawful humanitarian supply, agriculture and power in government-administered areas today, and in positioning for reconstruction once a ceasefire holds.

Investor Risk Note

Sudan is a legally restricted, conflict-affected market, and investors should treat compliance and security as the starting point of any engagement. The war between the SAF and the RSF continues; the UN Darfur arms embargo and targeted sanctions have been renewed to 11 December 2026, the US has designated both SAF leader Abdel Fattah al-Burhan and RSF leader Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo, and the UK and EU maintain listings on both sides. Since July 2026 the EU has prohibited purchase, import and transport of Sudanese gold, and US Chemical Weapons Act measures deny US government credit and restrict sensitive exports. Sudan is not under a comprehensive US embargo, however, and lawful activity remains possible in humanitarian supply, agriculture, power and reconstruction in government-administered areas, where the economy returned to growth in 2025 and administration has resumed in Khartoum. Investors should obtain specialist sanctions and legal advice, screen counterparties continuously, avoid gold supply chains without expert guidance, and treat Sudan as a market where careful positioning today builds readiness for reconstruction once a ceasefire takes hold.

Market Access

Sudan's Red Sea coastline makes Port Sudan a natural gateway between the Gulf, Egypt and the African interior. Trade is strongly oriented to the Gulf and Egypt: in H1 2025 the UAE took about 58% of recorded exports, Saudi Arabia about 12% and Egypt about 11%, while China, Egypt, India and the UAE were the main import sources. Gold, livestock, sesame and gum arabic are the leading exports. Sudan is a member of the Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa (COMESA) and the Greater Arab Free Trade Area, and is negotiating WTO accession. Port Sudan's facilities have also handled South Sudanese crude, and the resumption of river transport with Egypt in early 2026 points to the corridor potential that a settlement would release.

Current International Business Channels

The most active channels are currently development and humanitarian partners: the African Development Bank, the World Bank through its Sudan Economic Monitor, UNDP and WFP, whose programmes and procurement offer a lawful entry point. The Quad (US, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Egypt), supported by the African Union, EU and UK, leads diplomacy on a truce and is the key forum to follow for reconstruction timing. Gulf and Egyptian business links are the deepest commercial relationships, and in 2025 the Prime Minister presented a $100 billion package of proposed joint projects to Saudi Arabia, including Red Sea resources. Investors are commonly encouraged to work with a Sudanese partner, which makes thorough due diligence on local counterparties essential.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Sudan currently suits experienced, risk-tolerant investors with strong compliance functions: humanitarian logistics and supply companies; agri-input, seed, fertiliser and food-processing firms building on sesame, livestock and gum arabic; off-grid solar and power-equipment providers; and construction-materials and engineering firms preparing for reconstruction. Regional investors from the Gulf and Egypt, and DFI-backed funds, are best placed for early engagement. Long-horizon investors can build relationships and pipelines now in readiness for a ceasefire, while gold-sector investment should be considered only with specialist sanctions and provenance advice.

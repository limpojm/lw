Investor Entry Routes

  • Kenya Investment Authority (KenInvest), the national investment promotion agency, providing licensing support, aftercare and a One-Stop Investment Centre that is due to be fully digital by end-2026
  • Investment Promotion Act (2004), with an Invest Kenya Bill in preparation to create a unified investment framework, and the Foreign Investment Protection Act guaranteeing repatriation of capital, dividends and interest
  • Special Economic Zones offering a 10% corporate tax rate for the first 10 years and 15% for the next 10, plus VAT exemptions; Export Processing Zones offering a 10-year corporate tax holiday and duty-free inputs
  • Nairobi International Financial Centre (NIFC), whose certified firms can access a reduced 5% capital gains tax rate on qualifying investments of at least KES 3 billion
  • Liberalised capital account with no restrictions on converting or transferring investment funds; the Central Bank of Kenya manages a floating shilling, stable at around KES 130 to the dollar
  • Public-Private Partnerships Act (2021) and the new National Infrastructure Fund, creating structured routes for private capital into roads, energy, water and ports
  • Privatisation and capital-market entry via the Nairobi Securities Exchange, demonstrated by the oversubscribed Kenya Pipeline Company IPO in March 2026

Market-Entry Observation

Kenya in October 2026 offers one of Africa's most diversified and resilient growth stories. GDP expanded 5.1% in the second quarter of 2026, up from 4.9% a year earlier, led by electricity and water (+7.7%), accommodation and food services (+7.4%), information and communication (+7.2%) and financial services (+6.1%), and the Central Bank of Kenya has raised its 2026 growth projection to 5.0% after 4.6% in 2025. Foreign direct investment reached a record $3.2 billion in 2025, up about 38%, driven by digital infrastructure and renewable energy. Macroeconomic stability has strengthened markedly: the shilling has held at around KES 130 to the dollar for over two years, reserves stand at $14.7 billion (5.9 months of import cover), and inflation of 6.8% in September remains within the target range with the policy rate at 8.75%. Moody's upgraded Kenya to B3 in January 2026, while the Kenya Pipeline Company IPO and the sale of a 15% Safaricom stake to Vodacom show growing investor appetite for Kenyan assets.

Kenya's main structural challenge is its public-debt profile. Total public debt stood at KES 13.2 trillion in July 2026, around 63% of GDP, and debt service absorbs a large share of revenue, limiting fiscal room. The government is addressing this through fiscal consolidation, liability management that has smoothed Eurobond maturities, and a deliberate shift from borrowing to private capital: proceeds from the Safaricom sale (about KES 204 billion) are being channelled into the new National Infrastructure Fund, and the Treasury is turning to PPPs and securitisation to close an estimated $5 billion annual infrastructure gap. The Treasury projects the debt ratio easing to 60.6% by 2030. For investors this creates a substantial opportunity: Kenya is actively seeking private partners for highways, power, water and ports, with a pipeline of investment-ready projects and a stronger capital market through which to exit or co-invest.

Investor Risk Note

Kenya's risk profile is that of a large, diversified economy with improving external buffers and a clear reform direction. A stable shilling, reserves of 5.9 months of import cover, inflation within target and a Moody's upgrade in January 2026 reflect strengthened external liquidity, while record FDI and the oversubscribed Kenya Pipeline Company IPO show investor confidence. The central issue is the public-debt burden, which the government is managing through fiscal consolidation, active liability management and a deliberate shift towards privatisation proceeds, the National Infrastructure Fund and PPPs; this shift is itself the main opening for private capital. Investors should factor in the higher global oil-price environment, FATF compliance requirements while Kenya completes its action plan, and the policy calendar ahead of the August 2027 election. Using SEZ, NIFC and PPP frameworks, international arbitration protections and DFI co-financing provides practical mitigation and Kenya's open capital account supports a positive medium-term outlook.

Market Access

Kenya is a member of the East African Community customs union and COMESA, and was among the first countries to ratify the African Continental Free Trade Area, giving access to EAC and COMESA markets of more than 330 million and 680 million consumers respectively. It is a WTO member and holds Economic Partnership Agreements with the United Kingdom (ratified 2021) and the European Union (signed December 2023), providing duty-free, quota-free access for its exports, and the government reports a newly finalised trade agreement with China. Africa took the largest share of Kenya's KES 1.12 trillion of exports in 2025, with Uganda the single largest market (14.5%), followed by Europe and Asia; the US market remains important for apparel under AGOA. Mombasa's port and the Northern Corridor make Kenya the main gateway for landlocked Uganda, Rwanda, South Sudan and eastern DRC, with Lamu and the LAPSSET corridor adding a second route northwards and to Ethiopia.

Current International Business Channels

The Kenya Private Sector Alliance (KEPSA) and the Kenya National Chamber of Commerce and Industry are the principal domestic business bodies, alongside the Kenya Association of Manufacturers for industrial investors. The American Chamber of Commerce in Kenya and the British business community are well established, and the UK–Kenya Strategic Partnership 2025–2030, backed by up to £1.5 billion, prioritises trade, climate and technology. The government's Kenya International Investment Conference (KIICO), held most recently in March 2026, showcases investment-ready projects. The World Bank, IFC, African Development Bank and British International Investment are active in energy, infrastructure and financial services, and the NIFC is increasingly a networking hub for regional financial-services investment.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Kenya suits a broad range of investors seeking a regional platform for East and Central Africa: technology, fintech and financial-services investors drawn by Nairobi's ecosystem and the NIFC; renewable-energy investors, particularly in geothermal, wind and solar, given Kenya's 80% renewable power mix and plans to expand generation; infrastructure funds and contractors able to participate in PPPs and National Infrastructure Fund projects; logistics investors positioned around Mombasa, Lamu and the Northern Corridor; and manufacturers and agro-processors using SEZ and EPZ incentives to serve EAC, AfCFTA, EU and UK markets. Portfolio investors also benefit from a deepening Nairobi Securities Exchange and an expanding privatisation pipeline.

The economy today

Iraq in October 2026 is in a strong recovery phase. As OPEC's second-largest producer, it has restored crude exports to more than 3.4 million barrels a day so far in October, close to the 3.3–3.5 million barrels a day shipped before the regional war, from about 32 million barrels a month in May and June. The IMF's October 2026 World Economic Outlook projects a double-digit contraction in 2026 followed by a double-digit expansion in 2027 as output and logistics recover, while inflation remains contained at 3.3% in August 2026. The government of Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi, confirmed by parliament in May 2026, has made economic diversification, investment promotion and banking-sector reform core priorities, and on 7 October 2026 the Central Bank of Iraq adjusted the dinar by about 15% to strengthen domestic industry and attract investment into non-oil sectors. Flagship projects, including the TotalEnergies-led Gas Growth Integrated Project, with 1 GW of solar and Ratawi output rising to 210,000 barrels a day by 2028, and the Grand Faw Port and Development Road, signal long-term international confidence.

Iraq's central structural challenge is its reliance on oil, which provides around 90% of government revenue, and on a single maritime route: about 80% of exports passed through the Strait of Hormuz before the conflict that began on 28 February 2026. Monthly oil revenue fell from $6.8 billion in February to about $2.3 billion in May and June, delaying some public salaries, and CBI reserves eased from $97.4 billion to $86.2 billion in the first half of 2026, still covering about 9.6 months of imports. Baghdad has responded practically: it extended the Iraq–Turkey pipeline agreement for a year with a 750,000 barrel-a-day target, began trucking crude through Syria, moved its own crude past Hormuz on a state-owned tanker and plans 5 million barrels a day of export capacity through new pipelines to Fishkhabour and Baniyas. This diversification drive, together with the push to broaden the non-oil economy, is creating sustained demand for investment in pipelines, ports, storage, power and manufacturing.

How do I get in?

National Investment Commission (NIC), a cabinet-level body with a One Stop Shop for licences, visas, customs and registration, supported by Provincial Investment Commissions

Investment Law No. 13 of 2006, guaranteeing national treatment and repatriation of capital and profits, with ten-year tax exemptions and three-year import-fee exemptions for licensed projects; foreign ownership is generally capped at 49% outside licensed projects, and government land can be leased for up to 50 years

Kurdistan Region Investment Law No. 4 of 2006, administered by the Kurdistan Board of Investment, permitting 100% foreign ownership, land ownership and 10-year tax holidays

Federal free zones at Khor al-Zubair (Basra), Nineveh, Baghdad and Fallujah for trading, logistics and manufacturing projects

Central Bank of Iraq oversight of foreign exchange: the official rate was adjusted on 7 October 2026 to IQD 1,510 per dollar for bank sales, domestic transactions must be in dinars, and cross-border dollar transfers run through the CBI's monitored platform

Energy-sector entry through Ministry of Oil contracts with state companies such as Basra Oil Company, as in the TotalEnergies–QatarEnergy Gas Growth Integrated Project

Investor protection through Iraq's membership of ICSID and the 1958 New York Convention, with a dedicated commercial court for foreign-investor disputes

Trade and market access

Iraq is OPEC's second-largest producer, giving it long-standing commercial relationships with major energy buyers. It is not yet a WTO member, but the government has revived its accession effort, and Iraq is expanding non-dollar trade settlement with China and the UAE. Crude moves mainly through the Basra terminals, supplemented by the Iraq–Turkey pipeline to Ceyhan, which provides direct access to Mediterranean markets, and by new trucking routes through Syria. Over the longer term, the Grand Faw Port and the Development Road to the Turkish border aim to position Iraq as a land bridge between the Gulf and Europe, while planned pipelines to Fishkhabour and Baniyas would widen export choices further. This combination of energy scale and new corridors gives Iraq a growing range of routes to market.

The overall risk picture

Iraq is not subject to comprehensive country-wide sanctions, but sanctions compliance is a central part of any engagement. The United States continues to designate Iran-aligned armed groups such as Kataib Hezbollah, affiliated companies including the Muhandis General Company, and individuals using Iraqi banks to move funds to Iran, and US sanctions on Iran itself apply to Iran-linked transactions. Dollar access is also a practical consideration, since Iraq's oil revenues are held at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York and the CBI restricts listed banks from dollar business. Investors should therefore take specialist legal and sanctions advice, screen counterparties and beneficial owners, and select banking partners carefully. Within this framework the outlook is constructive: exports are back near pre-war levels, the al-Zaidi government is pursuing a state monopoly on weapons and closer economic ties with Washington, Iraq has instructed its banks to enforce US designations, and the IMF expects a strong rebound in 2027. Investors who build in compliance and payment safeguards are well placed for Iraq's recovery and diversification.

Primary reference point for strategic direction and major project approvals

National Investment Commission (NIC)

Cabinet-level investment authority with a One Stop Shop and provincial commissions

Essential for currency, repatriation and banking-compliance matters

Business networks

The Iraq Britain Business Council and the US Chamber of Commerce's US–Iraq Business Council are the leading Western business networks, alongside the Federation of Iraqi Chambers of Commerce and the Erbil Chamber of Commerce for Kurdistan Region liaison. The National Investment Commission and the Kurdistan Board of Investment provide direct investor facilitation. Prime Minister al-Zaidi's July 2026 visit to Washington has given fresh impetus to US–Iraq economic engagement, and the US International Development Finance Corporation has more than $300 million of active investments in Iraqi energy and financial services. International energy companies such as TotalEnergies and QatarEnergy are a valuable source of practical intelligence on operating in the south.

Who this market suits

Iraq best suits experienced investors with strong compliance functions and a long-term horizon. Energy and energy-services companies can benefit from gas capture, power, field development and export-route diversification; infrastructure and logistics investors are well placed for the Grand Faw Port, the Development Road and new pipelines; and manufacturers, agribusiness and consumer-goods firms can serve a large domestic market with an exchange rate now set to favour local production. The Kurdistan Region offers an established entry point for real estate, tourism and services under a liberal investment law. All investors should budget for sanctions screening, careful choice of banking partners and secured payment structures.

    Build currency and continuity planning into project economicsModel the October 2026 exchange-rate adjustment, hedge dinar exposure and maintain contingency plans for regional disruption.

The economy today

Yemen in October 2026 is a market defined by conflict and divided authority, but the government-administered areas have shown real stabilisation. After the rial reached about 2,905 to the dollar in Aden in mid-July 2025, Central Bank of Yemen controls and Saudi support brought it back to about 1,600 from August 2025, where it has broadly held; the food basket fell 29.7% from its peak and the share of households with inadequate food consumption eased from 70% to 57% by February 2026. The current account deficit narrowed to 14.7% of GDP in 2025 from 26.6%, and remittances of about $1.98 billion, roughly a quarter of GDP, continue to underpin household incomes. The World Bank estimates real GDP fell 1.5% in 2025 on a nominal base of about $19.6 billion and expects a smaller 0.5% contraction in 2026. Politically, the Presidential Leadership Council contained the Southern Transitional Council's December 2025 takeover of eastern governorates within about two weeks, the STC announced its dissolution in January 2026, and a new 34-member cabinet under Prime Minister Shaya Mohsen al-Zindani took office in February 2026, backed by a SR1.3 billion Saudi grant for public salaries.

Yemen's central structural challenge is that the country remains divided and its main source of foreign currency is idle: oil exports have earned no foreign exchange since Houthi attacks on export terminals in late 2022, government revenue fell to 5.6% of GDP in 2025 and reserves at the central bank are about $1.2 billion, roughly one month of imports. The 2026 regional war added pressure, with the Houthis entering the conflict in March and declaring a blockade of Saudi-linked shipping in July. The mitigations are substantive: sustained Saudi grants and reconstruction finance, a central bank in Aden that has regained control of the exchange rate, remittance flows of about $2 billion a year and donor programmes that pay contractors in hard currency. For investors, the realistic opportunity today lies in lawful, donor-financed reconstruction, power, water, health and logistics in government-administered areas, and in building the relationships and compliance readiness that will matter when a political settlement allows oil exports and wider recovery to resume.

How do I get in?

Investment Law No. 15 of 2010, administered by the General Investment Authority, which approved 68 projects between 2015 and 2023 and publishes a guide to around 100 investment opportunities

Aden Free Zone, the country's first commercial and industrial free zone, with an industrial area of about 1,900 hectares next to the Port of Aden

Central Bank of Yemen (Aden) foreign-exchange regime: the National Committee for Regulating and Financing Imports prioritises foreign currency for essential goods, with caps on currency access, so investors should plan for staged repatriation

Saudi Development and Reconstruction Program for Yemen (SDRPY), which has completed 240 projects since 2018 and is delivering a new SR1.9 billion, 28-project package in health, energy, education and transport

World Bank-funded programmes implemented through UN agencies, including the proposed $120 million RESET electricity project for Aden and privately operated solar mini-grids (board date November 2026)

Humanitarian trade under OFAC General License 22A (agricultural commodities, medicine and medical devices) and General License 26A (port and airport operations, excluding refined petroleum products), for any unavoidable dealings in Houthi-controlled areas

Banking through institutions headquartered in Aden, as banks continue to relocate from Sana'a to reduce sanctions exposure

Trade and market access

Yemen has been a member of the World Trade Organization since 2014, when it became the organisation's 160th member. Its location on the Bab el-Mandeb strait between the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden places it on one of the world's principal shipping lanes, and the Port of Aden and the Aden Free Zone provide a natural sheltered harbour adjacent to international routes. Saudi Arabia is the most important economic partner, as the largest source of grants and reconstruction finance and the origin of about 60% of remittances. Non-oil exports were about 392,000 tonnes in 2025, well below pre-2022 levels, while Houthi-controlled Red Sea ports handled 67% of imports in 2025, down from 75% the year before, as more trade moved through government-administered ports. A restart of oil exports from Hadramawt and a durable Red Sea settlement would restore Yemen's principal export channel and its role as a regional transit point.

The overall risk picture

Yemen is a legally and physically constrained market, and compliance is the starting point of any engagement. The Houthis (Ansarallah), who control Sana'a and much of the north, are designated by the United States as a Foreign Terrorist Organization and a Specially Designated Global Terrorist; providing material support to them carries criminal and secondary-sanctions exposure, petroleum deliveries to Houthi-controlled ports are no longer licensed, and a Sana'a bank has been sanctioned. UN asset-freeze and travel-ban measures under resolution 2140 run to 14 November 2026, a targeted arms embargo applies under resolution 2216, and the UK applies its own designated-person regime. Shipping in the southern Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb remains exposed to Houthi attacks, which resumed in 2026, and in October Houthi missiles were fired at Aden airport and at Saudi Arabia. Within these limits, lawful opportunity exists: trade and investment with the internationally recognised government and businesses in Aden and the south and east are not subject to general sanctions, and Saudi, World Bank and UN programmes are financing power, water, health and transport. Investors should take specialist sanctions and security advice, screen all counterparties and logistics routes, work in government-administered areas, and treat Yemen as a reconstruction market for which early, careful preparation will pay off when a settlement is reached.

Business networks

The most active channels into Yemen today are its reconstruction financiers. The Saudi Development and Reconstruction Program for Yemen works closely with the government in Aden and contracts across health, energy, education and transport, while the World Bank channels grant finance through UN agencies such as UNOPS and UNDP, including the proposed RESET electricity project. UN humanitarian agencies, whose 2026 response plan seeks $2.16 billion, are significant buyers of food, medicine and logistics services. The Port of Aden has hosted delegations from Qatar, China and the United Kingdom to discuss investment opportunities, and established Yemeni family business groups and Gulf-based Yemeni investors remain the most practical local partners for international firms entering government-administered areas.

Who this market suits

Yemen suits a narrow but meaningful set of investors with strong compliance capability and long time horizons: engineering, construction and equipment suppliers bidding into Saudi, World Bank and UN-financed reconstruction; renewable-energy and mini-grid operators responding to acute power shortages; food, pharmaceutical and medical-device suppliers working within humanitarian licences; logistics, port and warehousing operators focused on Aden; and Gulf and diaspora investors with established local relationships. Oil and gas investors should treat Yemen as a restart opportunity contingent on security, and all investors should avoid any dealings that could benefit the Houthis or other designated persons.

Five steps from interest to engagement

    Obtain specialist sanctions and legal advice before any engagementMap US, UN, UK and EU obligations, including the FTO designation and applicable general licences, and set up continuous screening of counterparties, ports, vessels and banks.
  2. 2
    Focus on government-administered areas and engage the General Investment AuthorityUse the Investment Law, the Aden Free Zone and Aden-based banks as the foundation for any licensed presence.
  3. 3
    Bid into donor- and Saudi-financed reconstruction programmesRegister with SDRPY, World Bank-funded UN implementers and humanitarian procurement systems, which offer hard-currency payment and established safeguards.
  4. 4
    Build Red Sea security and logistics scenarios into planningSecure war-risk insurance, route cargo through Aden and the eastern ports, and plan for freight and fuel-cost volatility.
  5. 5
    Position for a political settlement and oil-export restartTrack the UN sanctions renewal in November 2026, the peace process and oil-terminal security so that relationships and approvals are in place when wider recovery begins.

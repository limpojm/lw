The economy today

Syria in October 2026 is one of the region's clearest reconstruction stories, and its legal position has changed fundamentally. The US revoked its Syria sanctions programme in June 2025, Congress repealed the Caesar Act in December 2025, and the State Sponsor of Terrorism designation was rescinded on 24 August 2026; the EU and UK have lifted most economic measures, and the UN removed President Ahmed al-Sharaa from its sanctions list in November 2025. The economy began to recover in 2025, when about 1.5 million refugees returned and the budget closed with a small surplus, and IMF staff now expect double-digit growth in 2026. Investor interest is substantial: some $25 billion of memorandums had been signed by October 2025, including a $14 billion infrastructure package, a $7 billion power partnership and $6.4 billion of Saudi agreements, while Investment Law 114 offers full foreign ownership and repatriation guarantees and the World Bank has returned with $493 million in grants.

Syria's central challenge is scale: the World Bank estimates reconstruction needs at $216 billion, and nominal GDP fell from $67.5 billion in 2011 to about $21.4 billion in 2024. Generation of about 3,000 MW meets less than a third of estimated demand of 10,000 MW, and the IMF describes rebuilding the banking system as of critical importance. These gaps are being addressed in a structured way: daily power supply rose from about 3 to 13 hours in 2025, new plants and port concessions are under way, the Central Bank has completed a currency changeover, and an IMF technical assistance programme covers new central bank and banking laws, tax reform and removal from the FATF grey list. Higher import prices linked to the regional conflict have lifted inflation in 2026, but the same conflict has raised Syria's value as an overland route to the Mediterranean, reflected in the plan to revive the Kirkuk–Baniyas pipeline. For investors with sound compliance, the size of the gap is the opportunity.

How do I get in?

Syrian Investment Authority licensing under Investment Law 114 of 2025 (amending Law No. 18 of 2021): up to 100% foreign ownership, repatriation of profits and capital, compensation guarantees, full tax exemption for agricultural and medical projects and up to 80% tax relief for exporters

Sanctions-clearance route: the US Syria sanctions programme was revoked by executive order on 30 June 2025, the Caesar Act was repealed in December 2025 and the State Sponsor of Terrorism designation was rescinded on 24 August 2026, but individual SDN designations and some export-licence requirements remain

EU and UK regimes: the EU lifted most economic sanctions in May 2025 and has renewed listings linked to the former Assad regime until 1 June 2027; the UK revoked its gold and luxury-goods bans in April 2026 while retaining designations and some security-related restrictions

Free zones run by the General Establishment for Free Zones (Damascus, Latakia, Aleppo, Tartus, with Idlib being developed), where 800 investor contracts have been signed and around 90% of ready-built facilities are occupied

Concession and partnership route for infrastructure: ports via the General Authority for Land and Sea Ports, airports via the civil aviation authority and power plants via the Ministry of Energy, as used by DP World, CMA CGM and the UCC-led consortium

Currency and banking: the Central Bank of Syria has introduced a new currency and restored SWIFT transfers; the banking system is being rebuilt and Syria remains on the FATF grey list, so payment channels need enhanced due diligence

Donor-financed procurement: $493 million of World Bank IDA grants across electricity, water, health, public finance and financial-sector modernisation, alongside Gulf-backed packages such as the $6.4 billion Saudi–Syrian forum agreements

Trade and market access

Syria's Mediterranean ports at Latakia, Tartus and Baniyas, now operated or being upgraded by CMA CGM and DP World, make it a natural gateway between the Mediterranean, Iraq, Jordan and the Gulf. The country is a member of the Greater Arab Free Trade Area and has been engaged in WTO accession since members agreed in 2010 to open talks. Trade with Türkiye is growing fast: Turkish exports to Syria rose about 70% to $2.57 billion in 2025, led by grain and food products. The 2026 regional conflict, which has disrupted tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, has added interest in overland and pipeline routes, and Iraq and Syria agreed in June 2026 to rehabilitate the Kirkuk–Baniyas oil pipeline. With most Western sanctions lifted, re-established SWIFT links and Gulf and Turkish capital flowing in, Syria's market access is broadening quickly.

The overall risk picture

Syria's risk profile has improved markedly since 2025, and for most commercial sectors the main sanctions barriers have now been removed. US investors no longer face a country-wide embargo: the Syria sanctions programme, the Caesar Act and the State Sponsor of Terrorism designation have all ended, and the EU, UK and UN have eased or removed their principal measures. The compliance obligations that remain are targeted, not general. Individuals and entities linked to the former Assad regime, human-rights abuses, Captagon trafficking, proliferation, ISIS, al-Qa'ida and Iran remain designated, some US export-licence requirements still apply pending new Commerce rules, and Washington will review Syria's progress every 180 days over four years, with power to sanction individuals. Every transaction should therefore be screened by sanctions counsel in each relevant jurisdiction. Banking connectivity, power supply and security in some areas also need careful planning. With these safeguards in place, Syria offers early-mover access to one of the world's largest reconstruction programmes.

Business networks

The Saudi–Syrian Investment Forum, held in Damascus in July 2025, produced 47 agreements, and Saudi, Qatari, Emirati and Turkish companies are now the most active foreign investors, which makes them useful partners and sources of practical intelligence. The Syrian Investment Authority promotes opportunities at international events such as the World Governments Summit, and the Damascus and Aleppo chambers of commerce and industry are the main channels to local business. The World Bank, with $493 million in IDA grants, and the IMF, through its technical assistance programme, are engaged again, creating procurement and advisory openings. The Syrian diaspora in Europe, the Gulf and Türkiye is an important source of partners and capital.

Who this market suits

Syria best suits investors with a reconstruction focus, a tolerance for early-stage markets and strong compliance capability. Gulf and Turkish developers, contractors and infrastructure investors are leading on ports, airports, power and real estate. Logistics and transit investors can benefit from the Mediterranean ports and the pipeline revival. Agriculture, agri-food and healthcare investors benefit from full tax exemptions under the investment law, and export-oriented manufacturers from up to 80% tax relief. Engineering, equipment and advisory firms can take part in World Bank-funded programmes. Western investors in most sectors can now enter lawfully, provided they carry out rigorous counterparty screening and stay alert to residual designations and export-licence requirements.

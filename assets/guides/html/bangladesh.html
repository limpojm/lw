Investor Entry Routes

  • Bangladesh Investment Development Authority (BIDA), under the Prime Minister's Office, provides one-stop facilitation covering registration, approvals, visas and work permits, regulatory support and aftercare
  • Foreign Private Investment (Promotion and Protection) Act 1980, guaranteeing fair and equitable treatment, protection against expropriation and repatriation of capital, profits and dividends
  • Bangladesh Economic Zones Authority (BEZA) economic zones, with five prioritised for accelerated development, and BEPZA's eight Export Processing Zones offering ready-built industrial plots and incentives
  • Bangladesh Bank operates a flexible, market-determined exchange rate; routine dividend remittances are processed through authorised dealer banks, generally without prior central bank approval
  • Public-private partnership concessions, illustrated by the 30-year Laldia container terminal concession signed with the Chittagong Port Authority in November 2025
  • Preferential export platforms: duty-free access to Japan for 7,379 product lines under the 2026 Economic Partnership Agreement and zero US duty on garments made with US cotton under the February 2026 reciprocal trade agreement

Market-Entry Observation

Bangladesh in October 2026 is a $500 billion economy of strong long-term potential, entering a new phase under the government of Prime Minister Tarique Rahman, sworn in on 17 February 2026 after a decisive election victory. Growth has been steady through the political transition, at 3.7% in FY2025/26 on the latest Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics estimate, and the government is targeting 6.5% in FY2026/27 with a record budget focused on investment, deregulation and jobs. Investor interest is recovering: net FDI rose 45% to $1.78 billion in 2025 according to UNCTAD, led by sharply higher reinvested earnings, while record remittances of $35.6 billion (+17.3%) in FY2025/26 have lifted gross reserves above $37 billion. New trade agreements with Japan and the United States, landmark port concessions and a joint BIDA–BEZA–BEPZA action plan are strengthening the investment case.

Bangladesh's main structural challenge is the health of its banking sector, where non-performing loans stood at 32.8% of total lending in June 2026, concentrated in a group of Islamic and newer private banks. The authorities are addressing this directly: the ratio has fallen by almost 3 percentage points since September 2025, five shariah-based banks have been merged into Sammilito Islami Bank, and the government requested a new IMF-supported programme in June 2026 to anchor further banking and revenue reforms. Foreign commercial banks, with an NPL ratio of 6.3%, remain well capitalised partners for international investors. With inflation easing from its 2024 peak to 8.3% and a firm commitment to a flexible exchange rate, the clean-up is creating opportunities in bank recapitalisation, asset resolution and financial services, and should support stronger credit growth as reforms take hold.

Investor Risk Note

Bangladesh's risk profile is that of a large, young and resilient economy completing a political transition and now rebuilding its financial foundations. The February 2026 election produced a government with a clear mandate and a stated ambition of a $1 trillion economy, and early actions, including the US and Japan trade agreements, port concessions and a request for a new IMF programme, point to a pragmatic, investor-friendly direction. The principal risks are domestic and well understood: a high stock of bad loans concentrated in specific banks, inflation that remains above target, and the transition out of LDC status, which UN bodies have supported deferring to 2029. Each is being actively managed, and record remittances, reserves above $37 billion and the flexible exchange-rate regime provide meaningful buffers. Investors should choose banking partners carefully, hedge currency exposure and include arbitration provisions, while positioning for the growth that reform and infrastructure upgrades are expected to unlock.

Market Access

Bangladesh is a founding member of the WTO and currently enjoys duty-free, quota-free access as a least developed country to major markets including the EU, the UK and Canada, with the UK committed to maintaining access for three years after graduation. In February 2026 Bangladesh signed its first comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement, with Japan, giving 7,379 product lines duty-free entry, and a reciprocal trade agreement with the United States setting a 19% tariff and zero duty on garments made with US cotton and synthetic fibres. The United States is the largest single export market ($9.04 billion in FY2025/26), followed by Germany and the United Kingdom. Chattogram is the main maritime gateway, with the Laldia terminal and Matarbari deep seaport set to expand capacity and allow larger vessels to call directly. Merchandise exports rose 6.3% to $13.1 billion in the first quarter of FY2026/27, signalling renewed momentum.

Current International Business Channels

The Foreign Investors' Chamber of Commerce and Industry (FICCI) is the principal voice of international companies in Bangladesh, alongside the American Chamber of Commerce in Bangladesh, the Federation of Bangladesh Chambers of Commerce and Industry (FBCCI) and the Dhaka Chamber of Commerce and Industry. The Bangladesh Garment Manufacturers and Exporters Association (BGMEA) is the key gateway to the garment supply chain. BIDA hosts the Bangladesh Investment Summit and runs sector roadshows, while BEPZA has launched dedicated desks for East Asian investors. The World Bank, Asian Development Bank and Japan's JICA are long-standing development partners in infrastructure, energy and private-sector development, and the IMF is engaging with the government on a new reform programme.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Bangladesh suits investors seeking scale, competitive labour costs and access to a large domestic market of a young, increasingly urban population: export-oriented manufacturers in garments, textiles, footwear and light engineering using EPZ and economic-zone platforms and new US and Japanese trade preferences; logistics and infrastructure investors drawn by port concessions and PPP opportunities; consumer, ICT and fintech businesses serving rising middle-class demand; and financial investors with the expertise to participate in banking-sector restructuring. Investors should favour strong banking partners, hedge taka exposure and phase commitments in line with reform progress.

From Intelligence to Engagement

STEP 2: Structure around the 1980 Act and repatriation channelsConfirm protections under the Foreign Private Investment Act and set up dividend and fee flows through authorised dealer banks from the outset.
STEP 3: Select banking partners carefullyWork with foreign commercial banks or well-capitalised local banks, and apply standard currency hedging for taka exposure.
STEP 4: Plan exports around new trade preferencesMap products to the Japan EPA, the US agreement and the LDC graduation timetable to secure the most favourable market access.
STEP 5: Track infrastructure and reform milestonesFollow Laldia and Matarbari port progress, the IMF programme and banking reforms, which together will shape costs and financing conditions.

Investor Entry Routes

  • Agence Nationale pour la Promotion des Investissements (ANPI), the national investment promotion agency, receives approval applications under the Investment Code and organises the "Business in Komor" investor fair
  • Investment Code adopted by Law No. 20-035/AU of 28 December 2020, which the authorities present as among the most attractive in the region, with an approval regime for incentives
  • Euro-pegged Comorian franc (1 euro = 491.968 KMF) backed by an unlimited French convertibility guarantee, with transfers in principle free within the franc zone
  • Banque Centrale des Comores oversight of banking and foreign-exchange matters; policy rate cut to 2.5% in January 2026
  • Public-private partnerships and concessions in ports and aviation, as shown by the Moroni container terminal and the international airport concession
  • Procurement under donor-financed programmes, including the Maritime Corridor and Regional Trade Facilitation Project, whose tenders were scheduled from February 2026
  • OHADA business-law framework, giving investors a harmonised commercial and arbitration regime shared with other member states across Africa

Market-Entry Observation

Comoros in October 2026 is a small, stable and reforming island economy with a strong monetary anchor and growing international integration. Real GDP grew 3.8% in 2025, the strongest outturn in recent years, after 3.3% in 2024; the World Bank expects 3.2% in 2026 and 4.0% in 2027, while the IMF projected 4.1% for 2026. The euro peg, backed by France's convertibility guarantee, underpins price stability: inflation fell from a peak of 7.3% in March 2025 to 1.9% in October 2025, although higher energy and food prices are lifting it in 2026. Reserves stand at about $395 million, or 9.4 months of imports. Comoros became the WTO's 165th member in August 2024, applies the EU interim Economic Partnership Agreement and is on track under a $43 million IMF Extended Credit Facility running to 2027, with 13 of 15 structural benchmarks met in the latest review. FDI is modest, at a stock of $158 million in 2024, leaving significant room for first movers.

The main structural challenge is infrastructure, above all reliable electricity, water and port capacity, which the IMF identifies as a constraint on investment; fuel supply pressures in 2026 linked to Middle East disruption added to this. Comoros has shown resilience: diaspora remittances, worth around 18% of GDP in 2025, rose 13.5% in the first four months of 2026, and reserves remain well above the benchmark for small states. Mitigation is under way through the $137 million AfDB-led port programme, co-financed by the World Bank, Islamic Development Bank, AFD, EU and EIB, and through a World Bank portfolio of $454 million across energy, transport and water. For investors, the same gaps are the opportunity: renewable power, port and logistics services, hospitality ahead of the 2027 games, and higher-value processing of the islands' spices and essential oils.

Investor Risk Note

Comoros's risk profile is that of a small island state with a strong monetary anchor and an active reform programme. The euro peg and France's convertibility guarantee give investors currency predictability rare among peer economies, inflation has eased from its 2025 peak, and reserves cover more than nine months of imports. The IMF Extended Credit Facility, WTO membership since 2024 and a substantial pipeline of multilateral finance are steadily improving the policy and infrastructure base. Politically, President Azali Assoumani has provided continuity since his re-election in January 2024, a result the opposition disputed. The main practical considerations are energy supply, infrastructure, the depth of local banking and the IMF's high debt-distress rating, which make DFI partnership and careful counterparty selection advisable. Investors who plan for self-supplied power and structure projects with multilateral backing can take advantage of an early-stage market with limited competition.

Market Access

Comoros is a member of both the Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa (COMESA) and the Southern African Development Community (SADC), as well as the Indian Ocean Commission, the African Union and the Arab League, giving it a bridging position between eastern and southern Africa, the Indian Ocean islands and the Gulf. It became the WTO's 165th member on 21 August 2024 after 17 years of negotiations, and it has applied the EU interim Economic Partnership Agreement with Eastern and Southern African states since February 2019, providing duty-free, quota-free access to the EU market. Its main export markets are in Asia and the European Union, with cloves, vanilla and ylang-ylang the leading exports. Its location in the Mozambique Channel underpins the government's plan, backed by the AfDB-led port programme, to position the islands as a logistics link between Africa and Asia.

Current International Business Channels

ANPI is the main channel for investor contact and runs the "Business in Komor" investor fair, whose 2023 edition coincided with the annual conference of the francophone network of investment promotion agencies. France maintains business support in Moroni through its embassy's economic department, Business France and a bilateral chamber of commerce, and AFD is an active development partner. The African Development Bank, World Bank, Islamic Development Bank, European Union and European Investment Bank all finance current programmes, and their procurement notices are a practical route to early contracts. Gulf partners are present, notably through the airport concession, and the large Comorian diaspora in France is an important source of capital, market knowledge and local contacts.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Comoros suits investors seeking an early-stage, euro-anchored market with limited competition: renewable-energy and utility investors addressing the clear power gap; port, logistics and construction firms positioned for the donor-financed corridor programme and the 2027 games; hospitality and eco-tourism investors attracted by the islands' unspoilt coastline; and agro-industry and fragrance-sector investors seeking secure supplies of ylang-ylang, cloves and vanilla and higher-value local processing. Smaller, patient investors working alongside development finance institutions, and diaspora-linked enterprises, are especially well placed.

From Intelligence to Engagement

STEP 1: Engage ANPI and secure Investment Code approvalOpen direct dialogue with the national investment promotion agency to confirm the approval process and the tax and customs benefits available for the project.
STEP 2: Track donor-financed procurementMonitor tenders under the Maritime Corridor programme and World Bank and AfDB projects in energy, transport and water, which offer early, well-financed entry points.
STEP 3: Plan energy self-sufficiency from the outsetBudget for on-site generation or solar-plus-storage and explore utility-scale renewable opportunities with the authorities and development partners.
STEP 4: Structure with DFI partners and the euro peg in mindUse multilateral co-financing, guarantees and trade finance to offset limited local credit, and take advantage of the euro-pegged currency for pricing and transfers.
STEP 5: Position for the 2027 Indian Ocean Island GamesAssess hospitality, construction and tourism-services opportunities linked to the games and to the wider tourism agenda.

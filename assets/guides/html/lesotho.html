Investor Entry Routes

  • Lesotho National Development Corporation (LNDC), the national industrial development agency, provides investment promotion, facilitation and aftercare, including permits, site visits, joint-venture partner matching, factory shells and serviced industrial land
  • One Stop Business Facilitation Centre (OBFC), launched in 2016, consolidating business licences, permits and import/export clearance in one place
  • Competitive tax regime: 10% corporate income tax on manufacturing and commercial farming (25% standard), withholding-tax exemption on manufacturing dividends, a 125% deduction for training costs and zero-rated VAT on exports
  • Investor protections: property rights under the 1993 Constitution, membership of ICSID and the New York Convention, and bilateral investment agreements providing for international arbitration
  • Special Economic Zones framework under the SEZ Policy 2024–2029, with the SEZ Bill being finalised to provide a legislated regime for zone-based investment
  • Loti pegged at par to the South African rand within the Common Monetary Area, giving price stability and close integration with South Africa's financial system; the Central Bank of Lesotho oversees foreign-exchange matters
  • Energy-sector entry through solar, hydropower and mini-grid projects, supported by a M1.7 billion energy allocation in the 2026/27 budget and a new Ministry of Energy and Mining created in April 2026

Market-Entry Observation

Lesotho in October 2026 offers macroeconomic stability, sound buffers and a strengthening trade position. Output grew 3.9% year-on-year in the fourth quarter of 2025 and 2.8% in the first quarter of 2026, after growth of 4.2% in 2024/25 driven by construction on the Lesotho Highlands Water Project. Inflation eased from 4.3% in 2025 to 2.9% in July 2026, and the Central Bank of Lesotho raised its policy rate in May and again in September 2026, to 7.0%, as a precaution against higher global energy prices. Public finances recorded a surplus of 3.1% of GDP in 2025, public debt fell to 53.1% of GDP, and net international reserves stood at about $1.2 billion in May 2026, more than $200 million above target. The 2026/27 budget (M30.97 billion, of which M9.03 billion is capital spending) funds a Special Economic Zones Bill, a fiscal stabilisation fund and new solar and hydropower capacity, while the extension of AGOA to December 2028, signed in September 2026, restores long-term duty-free access to the US market.

Lesotho's main structural challenge is a narrow economic base: garment exports of around $300 million a year, split roughly evenly between South Africa and the US, together with SACU receipts and water royalties, account for a large share of activity and public revenue. The economy showed its resilience in 2025–26, when US tariffs and the lapse of AGOA prompted factory retrenchments: the government declared a national state of disaster on unemployment in mid-2025, placing 42,239 young people in work by May 2026, and pursued trade diplomacy that saw AGOA restored in February 2026, temporary US tariffs expire in July and a multi-year extension secured in September. With growth projected by the World Bank at a modest 1.2% in 2026–27, the government is broadening the base through the SEZ Bill, an IFC-supported textile sector strategy, and expanding solar and hydropower. Polihali Dam, due for completion in 2029, will increase water transfers to South Africa by more than 60%, underpinning future royalty income and creating opportunities in construction, energy and services.

Investor Risk Note

Lesotho's risk profile is that of a small, stable, rand-linked economy with sound buffers and a narrow but recovering export base. Fiscal surpluses, falling public debt, comfortable reserves and the loti's peg to the rand provide a solid macroeconomic anchor, while the extension of AGOA to the end of 2028 removes the most immediate risk facing the garment industry and restores a multi-year planning horizon for exporters. The main considerations for investors are modest headline growth, the economy's reliance on SACU revenues, water royalties and US apparel demand, high youth unemployment and partial dependence on imported electricity. Each is being addressed: the government is legislating for Special Economic Zones, building a fiscal stabilisation fund, expanding domestic solar and hydropower, and placing tens of thousands of young people in work under its unemployment emergency programme. Investors should monitor AGOA implementation and US trade measures, and benefit from the long-term visibility provided by LHWP Phase II and Lesotho's deep integration with South Africa.

Market Access

Lesotho is a member of the Southern African Customs Union (SACU), giving duty-free access to South Africa, Botswana, Namibia and Eswatini, and of the Common Monetary Area, which pegs the loti at par to the rand. It is also a member of the Southern African Development Community (SADC) and of the WTO. AGOA, now extended to 31 December 2028, provides duty-free access to the US for textiles, apparel and other eligible products, and Lesotho was excluded from the US Section 301 tariff round of July 2026. South Africa and the US each take roughly half of garment exports, while water is exported to South Africa's industrial heartland through the Lesotho Highlands Water Project, whose transfers will rise by more than 60% when Polihali is complete. Lesotho's position entirely within South Africa gives exporters direct overland access to South Africa's ports and logistics network.

Current International Business Channels

The Private Sector Foundation of Lesotho is the principal private-sector advocacy body and is actively shaping the SEZ Bill, while the Lesotho Textile Exporters Association represents the export garment industry. LNDC runs investor facilitation and aftercare, and the One Stop Business Facilitation Centre handles practical registration and licensing. The World Bank has ten active operations worth $453 million, including a Competitiveness and Financial Inclusion Project supporting SMEs, while the IFC is preparing a textile sector strategy and MIGA provides guarantee cover in the financial sector. The EU and EIB co-finance the Lowlands Water Development Project, and Japan and IFAD support smallholder agriculture. Established international operators such as Gem Diamonds at Letšeng are a useful source of practical insight into operating in the highlands.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Lesotho suits investors seeking a stable, rand-linked base with preferential access to both the South African and US markets: apparel and light-manufacturing investors able to use the 10% manufacturing tax rate, LNDC factory space and AGOA access through 2028; engineering, construction and services firms targeting LHWP Phase II and the Lowlands water programme; renewable-energy developers and independent power producers responding to the government's push for domestic generation; and mining investors drawn to high-value diamond deposits. Agro-processing and logistics investors can position for the forthcoming SEZ regime and Lesotho's deep integration with South Africa's supply chains.

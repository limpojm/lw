Investor Entry Routes

  • ApexBrasil (Brazilian Trade and Investment Promotion Agency) provides investor intelligence, tailored business agendas, an investment-opportunity portfolio and matchmaking
  • Registration of foreign capital with the Banco Central do Brasil under the modernised foreign-exchange framework (Law 14.286/2021), which underpins capital repatriation and profit remittance; the real is free-floating
  • Federal and state concession and public-private partnership (PPP) programmes, alongside the Novo PAC investment programme, offering long-term contracts in transport, energy, sanitation and social infrastructure
  • Manaus Free Trade Zone incentives (to 2073) and Export Processing Zones (ZPEs) offering tax and customs benefits for qualifying manufacturing and export projects
  • Eco Invest Brasil, a National Treasury programme providing blended finance and requiring currency hedging for green and ecological-transition projects funded with foreign capital
  • EU–Mercosur Interim Trade Agreement, provisionally applied since 1 May 2026, opening phased tariff reductions for EU–Brazil trade and supply chains
  • Acquisitions, joint ventures and capital-markets entry via the B3 exchange, noting sector-specific foreign-ownership limits in areas such as rural land, media and certain health and maritime activities

Market-Entry Observation

Brazil in October 2026 offers investors scale, diversified growth drivers and one of the world's deepest pools of foreign direct investment. GDP rose 0.5% quarter-on-quarter in Q2 2026 and 2.0% year-on-year, led by agriculture (+6.2% over twelve months), extractive industry (+3.4% in the quarter) and a 1.2% rise in fixed investment. Direct investment inflows reached $86.6 billion in the twelve months to August 2026, equal to 3.39% of GDP, while oil output hit a record 4.475 million barrels a day in June. Exports reached a record $348.7 billion in 2025, and the EU–Mercosur Interim Trade Agreement has applied provisionally since 1 May 2026. A landmark consumption-tax reform is moving into implementation, with IBS and CBS regulations issued in April 2026 and a phased transition from 2027.

Brazil's main structural challenge is fiscal: gross public debt stood at 82.9% of GDP in August 2026, and high interest costs keep the nominal deficit near 9.5% of GDP even though the primary deficit is a modest 0.62%. The policy framework is well equipped to manage this. The central bank has begun an easing cycle, cutting the Selic rate for a fifth consecutive time to 13.75% in September 2026, while keeping a firm stance as annual inflation of 4.58% sits just above the 4.5% tolerance ceiling, driven largely by electricity tariffs. External buffers are strong: international reserves of $372.6 billion and FDI inflows that comfortably exceed the 2.47%-of-GDP current account deficit. For investors, falling rates from a high base create attractive entry points in infrastructure, credit and local-currency assets, and fiscal consolidation will be a central theme for the government that takes office in 2027.

Investor Risk Note

Brazil's risk profile is that of a large, diversified, institutionally mature economy with strong external buffers and a fiscal challenge that is well understood and openly debated. Record oil output, a booming agricultural sector, record exports and FDI inflows of $86.6 billion over twelve months to August 2026 demonstrate the depth of investor confidence, while $372.6 billion in reserves and a floating exchange rate absorb external shocks. The main considerations are the high level of public debt and interest rates, which an easing cycle is now beginning to address, and the transition following the 25 October 2026 presidential run-off, which may bring adjustments to fiscal and sector priorities. US Section 301 tariffs affect a limited and declining share of exports, cushioned by the EU–Mercosur agreement and strong Chinese demand. Investors should build interest-rate and currency scenarios into project economics and monitor the incoming government's fiscal plans, while recognising that Brazil's long-term contract framework, regulatory agencies and capital markets provide continuity across political cycles.

Market Access

Brazil is the largest member of Mercosur, alongside Argentina, Paraguay and Uruguay, and a founding member of the WTO and of BRICS, giving it a genuinely multi-directional trade profile. The EU–Mercosur Interim Trade Agreement, signed in January 2026 and provisionally applied since 1 May 2026, phases out most tariffs over up to 15 years, with quotas for sensitive agricultural products such as beef, poultry, sugar and ethanol. China is the dominant export market, taking more than $100 billion, or about 28% of exports, in 2025, followed by the United States (10.8%) and Argentina (5.2%). Exports flow through Santos, Paranaguá and a growing network of northern-arc ports and grain corridors serving the Centre-West, while the pre-salt fields make Brazil a major crude exporter. This combination of regional integration, European access and Asian demand gives investors flexible routes to global markets.

Current International Business Channels

AmCham Brasil and the British Chamber of Commerce in Brazil (Britcham) are well-established foreign business associations, alongside the National Confederation of Industry (CNI), the São Paulo State Federation of Industries (Fiesp) and the China–Brazil Business Council (CEBC) for domestic and Asia-facing liaison. ApexBrasil hosts the Fórum Brasil de Investimentos and runs investor missions and webinars abroad. The Inter-American Development Bank, IFC, World Bank and BNDES are active co-financiers in infrastructure, energy transition and climate finance, with the IDB supporting Eco Invest Brasil. The B3 exchange and São Paulo's large professional-services community function as the main hub for deal origination and market intelligence.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Brazil suits a broad range of investors seeking scale and long-term growth in Latin America's largest economy: infrastructure and concession investors targeting transport, sanitation and energy auctions; energy investors in pre-salt oil and gas, offshore services and the country's abundant wind, solar and green-hydrogen potential; agribusiness and food-processing investors building on record exports; mining investors focused on critical minerals; and financial-services, fintech and consumer investors serving Latin America's largest consumer market. Investors with long horizons, local-currency hedging capacity and partnerships with established Brazilian groups or development banks are best placed to benefit as interest rates ease.

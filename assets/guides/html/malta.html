The economy today

Malta in November 2026 is one of the euro area's fastest-growing economies, offering an English-speaking EU base with a flexible tax system and strong services sectors. GDP grew 4.7% in 2025 and 4.5% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, led by domestic demand and services, and the government forecasts 4.1% growth in 2026. Unemployment is expected at about 3.3%, among the lowest in the EU, and inflation was 2.4% in September 2026, helped by stable regulated energy prices. Public finances are improving: the deficit fell to 2.1% of GDP in 2025 from 4.9% in 2023, and debt is forecast at 46.7% of GDP in 2026, well below the EU's 60% benchmark. The Labour Party won a fourth consecutive term in the snap election of 30 May 2026 with 51.8% of the vote, giving Prime Minister Robert Abela a fresh mandate and policy continuity.

Malta's main structural challenge is moving from growth based on population, construction and imported labour towards higher productivity, while adapting its tax and residence offer to new EU and global rules. The population has risen about 25% in a decade, adding pressure on infrastructure. Malta has deferred the Pillar Two minimum-tax rules until the end of 2029 and introduced an optional 15% regime, and the EU Court of Justice ended its citizenship-by-investment programme in April 2025. The government is responding with a focus on technology-driven, higher-value activity: Budget 2027 prioritises fintech, crypto-asset firms, family offices and aircraft leasing, alongside a second electricity interconnector with Sicily and a planned 300MW offshore wind farm. A major medical-technology investment, described by the economy minister as Malta's largest, signals growing interest in advanced manufacturing.

How do I get in?

Malta Enterprise, the national economic development agency, offers investment aid, tax credits and support for digitalisation, automation and higher-value investment

A full imputation tax system: a 35% headline corporate rate with shareholder refunds that can reduce the effective rate on trading profits to about 5%

An optional 15% final-tax regime (FITWI) for groups preferring a single rate, and a new 25% rate on retained profits for companies with turnover up to €1 million from Budget 2027

Malta Financial Services Authority licensing for funds, insurers, payment and crypto-asset firms, with EU passporting

Euro-area membership: the euro is the national currency, with free movement of capital under EU rules

EU single-market and Schengen membership, with English an official language and a common-law-influenced commercial system

Sector regimes for online gaming, aircraft registration and leasing, shipping and family offices

Trade and market access

As an EU member, Malta gives investors tariff-free access to the EU single market and to all the EU's free trade agreements, with WTO membership and the EU's common trade policy governing trade with third countries. Financial and gaming licences issued in Malta can be used to serve customers across the EU, and English as an official language makes Malta a natural base for UK, US and Commonwealth companies entering Europe. Its position in the central Mediterranean, between Sicily and North Africa, supports the Malta Freeport's transshipment role and close links with Italy, North Africa and the Middle East. Malta is also a member of the Commonwealth and operates a major international ship registry, underpinning its maritime services sector.

The overall risk picture

Malta's risk profile is that of a stable, fast-growing EU and euro-area member with full employment, falling deficits and debt below the EU benchmark. The May 2026 election returned the Labour government with a clear majority, providing continuity in economic policy. The main considerations are structural: the international tax environment, including Pillar Two rules that Malta has deferred to the end of 2029, pressure on infrastructure from rapid population growth, and continued EU scrutiny after the 2025 court ruling that ended the citizenship-by-investment programme. These are being addressed through an optional 15% tax regime, investment in energy and transport, and strong regulatory supervision. Inflation of 2.4% and an ECB deposit rate of 2.50% are moderate. There are no sanctions or legal restrictions specific to Malta beyond those applying across the EU, which gaming, financial and shipping firms must apply rigorously.

Business networks

The Malta Chamber of Commerce, Enterprise and Industry, the Malta Business Bureau and the Malta Employers' Association are the main business networks, with regular input into budgets and regulation. FinanceMalta promotes the financial centre internationally, while Malta Enterprise runs investor programmes and trade missions. Bilateral chambers, including the British Chamber of Commerce for Malta and the American Chamber of Commerce in Malta, support international firms. The European Investment Bank finances infrastructure and energy projects, and EU funds co-finance investment in transport, health and digital services. Embassies of major partners provide further introductions.

Who this market suits

Malta suits investors seeking an English-speaking, fast-growing base inside the EU and the euro area: online gaming, fintech, payments and crypto-asset businesses using Malta licences to serve the EU; fund managers, insurers and family offices; aviation and maritime companies using Malta's aircraft and ship registries and Freeport; pharmaceutical, medical-technology and precision manufacturers on industrial estates; and energy investors targeting the second interconnector, batteries and offshore wind. All investors should model the tax position under evolving international rules.

The economy today

Norway in November 2026 offers investors one of the world's strongest public balance sheets, a stable political system and a world-class energy and maritime industry. The Government Pension Fund Global is worth about $2.3 trillion, and the 2027 budget presented in October 2026 draws NOK 608.4 billion from it, equal to 2.7% of the fund's value and within the fiscal rule. Mainland GDP grew 2.6% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, and the government forecasts mainland growth of 1.1% in 2026 and 1.7% in 2027. Energy-transition investment is substantial: Northern Lights is expanding to more than 5 million tonnes a year of CO2 storage, and Norway has awarded its first floating offshore wind areas at Utsira Nord. Unemployment remains low by European standards at 5.0% in September 2026.

Norway's main near-term challenge is inflation, which has kept interest rates high. Consumer price inflation was 3.4% in September 2026, and Norges Bank raised its policy rate to 4.5% in September and held it on 5 November, saying the rate will likely need to stay elevated for a time. Over the longer term, Norway must manage the gradual transition away from oil and gas revenue. The mitigation is strong: the fiscal rule ties spending to the fund's returns, the government expects core inflation to ease to 2.8% in 2027, and the September 2025 election returned a Labour government under Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre, with Jens Stoltenberg as finance minister and the next election not due until 2029. Norway's energy expertise and state backing for CCS, offshore wind and green industry create opportunities for investors positioned for the transition.

How do I get in?

Invest in Norway, part of Innovation Norway, is the official investment promotion agency and provides free information, opportunity assessment, network introductions and support through the investment process

A 22% corporate income tax; upstream petroleum companies pay an additional 71.8% special tax, giving a 78% marginal rate within a stable, cash-flow-based regime

EEA membership through EFTA, giving access to the EU single market for goods, services, capital and people, together with Schengen membership; Norway is not an EU member

The Norwegian krone, with an independent central bank and free capital movement

The Security Act's investment control regime, under which acquisitions of 10% or more in businesses subject to the Act must be notified, with a standstill until clearance

State-supported energy-transition projects such as the Longship CCS programme and the Utsira Nord floating offshore wind tender, with up to NOK 35 billion of support

Priority sectors promoted by Invest in Norway, including aquaculture, batteries, CCUS, data centres, hydrogen, green maritime, offshore wind, life sciences and space

Trade and market access

Through the EEA Agreement and EFTA membership, Norway gives investors access to the EU single market for goods, services, capital and people, and it is a member of Schengen and the WTO. EFTA's free trade agreements extend preferential access to many markets beyond Europe. Energy and seafood are central to Norway's exports, and its long Atlantic and Arctic coastline and offshore expertise position it as a hub for North Sea energy, for carbon storage serving European industrial emitters through Northern Lights, and for green shipping. Norway aligns with EU sanctions on Russia, so trade with Russia is limited to what those rules permit.

The overall risk picture

Norway's risk profile is among the lowest in Europe: a stable, high-income democracy and NATO member with a sovereign wealth fund of about $2.3 trillion, a transparent fiscal rule and strong legal institutions. The main near-term issue is inflation of 3.4%, which has led Norges Bank to raise its policy rate to 4.5%, increasing financing costs; the government expects inflation to ease in 2027. Labour governs as a minority, but the fixed parliamentary term to 2029 and cross-party support for the fiscal rule provide continuity. Investors should note that acquisitions in businesses covered by the Security Act must be notified from a 10% stake, and that Norway applies EU sanctions on Russia, most recently the 21st package from October 2026, so sanctions and export-control advice is essential for any Russia-related business. Outside these areas, Norway offers a predictable, rules-based environment with full access to the EU single market through the EEA.

Leads the budget, tax policy and the fiscal rule governing withdrawals from the fund

Key counterpart on tax and public-finance matters

Essential counterpart for energy and transition projects

Business networks

The American Chamber of Commerce in Norway, national employers' and industry federations and bilateral chambers of commerce are the main business networks, with regular dialogue between business and government. Innovation Norway, through Invest in Norway, introduces investors to relevant networks and supports them through the investment process at no charge. Industry clusters in energy, maritime and aquaculture provide access to supply chains, and suppliers to the Northern Lights expansion and the Utsira Nord projects offer partnership routes for international firms. Embassies of major European partners and the United States in Oslo maintain commercial sections that are a useful source of introductions.

Who this market suits

Norway suits investors seeking a stable, high-income EEA market with world-class energy and maritime capabilities: energy and engineering firms in offshore wind, carbon capture and storage and oil and gas services; green-maritime, hydrogen and battery companies; aquaculture and seafood-technology investors; data-centre operators drawn by renewable hydropower; and life-sciences and space-technology companies. Investors targeting security-sensitive businesses should plan for Security Act screening, and any Russia-linked activity requires sanctions advice.

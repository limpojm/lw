The economy today

Bahrain entered 2026 with solid momentum and the Gulf's most diversified economic base. GDP grew 3.5% in 2025, above the government's 3.1% projection, with non-oil activity up 4.1% and fourth-quarter growth of 4.6%, the fastest since 2022. Non-oil sectors now generate around 90% of GDP, and even in the first quarter of 2026 they expanded 2.2%, led by financial and insurance services (+8.6%). The stock of foreign direct investment stands at BHD18 billion (around $48 billion) and inflows rose nearly 3% year on year in the first quarter. The Golden Licence attracted around $2.4 billion in its first year, the Bapco Modernisation Project has raised refinery capacity to 405,000 barrels a day, and strategic partnerships, including a £2 billion investment partnership with the UK and a $17 billion package of deals with the United States in 2025, deepen Bahrain's Western ties.

Bahrain's main structural challenge is fiscal: government debt reached about 147% of GDP in 2025 (Fitch), with interest costs of $2.8 billion, and the 2026 Iran war has added pressure. Iranian strikes on the Sitra refinery and Alba from March, together with restrictions on Strait of Hormuz shipping, cut oil activity by 37.2% and overall GDP by 3.8% in the first quarter; oil output averaged around 65,000 barrels a day in August against about 161,000 in late 2025. Bahrain has responded quickly and pragmatically. The CBB introduced loan-deferral and liquidity support in April, the UAE extended an AED20 billion currency swap, a $1 billion ten-year bond in June drew more than $3.2 billion of orders, and exporters such as Alba now route 40–60% of shipments overland through Saudi Arabia to Jeddah while meeting delivery commitments. Revenue reform, including the planned corporate income tax, is under way. For investors, the opportunity lies in Bahrain's resilient non-oil economy, Gulf-leading financial-services platform and the new demand for overland logistics, storage and supply-chain solutions.

How do I get in?

Bahrain Economic Development Board (EDB), the investment promotion agency, offering one-stop facilitation, incentive guidance and aftercare for foreign investors

Golden Licence for strategic projects, giving priority access to land, utilities and fast-track approvals; it attracted around $2.4 billion of investment in its first year

Wide scope for 100% foreign ownership across most sectors, with no personal income tax; a draft law would introduce a 10% corporate income tax from 2027, alongside the 15% domestic minimum top-up tax for large multinationals in force since 2025

Central Bank of Bahrain (CBB) licensing for banking, insurance, fintech and crypto-asset services, including a regulatory sandbox

Bahraini dinar pegged at about 0.376 per US dollar, with no restrictions on capital or profit repatriation

Industrial land and logistics access through Bahrain International Investment Park, Bahrain Logistics Zone and Khalifa Bin Salman Port

Joint ventures and supply arrangements with state-linked groups such as Bapco Energies, Alba and the sovereign investor Mumtalakat

Trade and market access

Bahrain is a founding member of the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) and its customs union, giving tariff-free access to the wider Gulf market, and has been a WTO member since 1995. It is the only GCC state with a bilateral free trade agreement with the United States, in force since 2006. The King Fahd Causeway connects Bahrain by road to Saudi Arabia, its largest trading partner and the source of around 22% of its cumulative inward investment, and in 2026 it has become a vital channel for trade to Saudi Red Sea ports. Bahrain's strategic partnerships with the US and UK under C-SIPA, and its planned second link to Saudi Arabia via the King Hamad Causeway, strengthen its position as a gateway to the wider Gulf.

The overall risk picture

Bahrain's risk profile reflects an open, diversified economy that is managing a significant regional shock from a position of deep institutional strength. Non-oil sectors, which generate around 90% of GDP, continued to grow in early 2026, financial services expanded strongly, and FDI inflows rose despite the conflict. The principal risks are external: the 2026 Iran war has brought strikes on Bahraini energy and industrial facilities and continuing restrictions on Strait of Hormuz shipping, which have sharply reduced oil output and export revenue; the IMF projects an 11.3% contraction in 2026 and a 13.9% rebound in 2027. These pressures add to high public debt, now rated 'B' by Fitch and S&P, and to rising interest costs. Bahrain's mitigants are substantial: GCC solidarity, illustrated by the UAE currency swap; continued access to international bond markets; a credible dollar peg; and the Saudi overland route, which is keeping exports moving. Investors should conduct thorough security, insurance and supply-chain due diligence and monitor ceasefire talks, while recognising that Bahrain's non-oil and services economy has continued to perform through the disruption.

Business networks

The Bahrain Chamber of Commerce and Industry is the principal channel for engagement with the local private sector and is consulted on new legislation, including the draft corporate income tax law. The American Chamber of Commerce in Bahrain supports US companies under the bilateral free trade agreement, and the UK–Bahrain Strategic Investment and Collaboration Partnership provides a framework for British investors. The EDB organises investor delegations, sector roadshows and an annual Saudi–Bahraini Investment Forum, reflecting the depth of Saudi investment in the kingdom. The Bahrain Bourse, Bahrain FinTech Bay and the large community of regional and international banks headquartered in Manama provide useful networks for financial-services investors.

Who this market suits

Bahrain suits investors seeking a cost-effective, well-regulated base for the Gulf: financial-services, Islamic finance, insurance and fintech firms drawn by the CBB's single-regulator model and sandbox; technology and data-centre investors building on Bahrain's cloud and digital infrastructure; logistics and supply-chain operators positioned for overland trade with Saudi Arabia; and downstream manufacturers using Alba aluminium and Bapco feedstock. Energy-services firms may find opportunities in restoration and maintenance once conditions allow, while all investors should build regional-security and supply-chain contingencies into their planning.

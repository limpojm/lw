The economy today

Burundi in October 2026 is moving from stabilisation toward recovery, with clear progress on its macroeconomic fundamentals. Inflation has fallen from a peak of 45.5% in April 2025 to 8.4% in August 2026, close to the central bank's 8% target, after the government stopped central bank financing of the deficit in mid-2025 and adopted a Macroeconomic Stabilisation Plan in January 2026. The IMF estimates growth at 4.2% in 2025 and 3.9% in 2026, averaging 4.3% over 2027–31, while public debt fell to about 42% of GDP at end-2025 from 53% a year earlier and the fiscal deficit is narrowing from 5.5% to 3.4% of GDP. Exports are gaining momentum: goods export volumes rose 45.3%, gold output tripled to 1.2 tonnes and coffee earnings roughly doubled in 2025. FDI remains modest at $31.2 million in 2025, but the IMF projects it to double to $63.6 million in 2026 as electrification advances, supported by the completion of the 49.5 MW Jiji–Mulembwe hydropower scheme, which lifts national capacity from about 60 MW to more than 109 MW, and by the $2.15 billion Uvinza–Musongati railway to Tanzania.

Burundi's main structural challenge is foreign-exchange availability. The official rate of about 3,002 francs to the dollar coexists with a parallel-market premium of around 100%, and gross reserves stood at $213.9 million, or 1.6 months of imports, at end-2025; in practice, currency is allocated by priority sector and some firms have faced delays in repatriating profits. The direction of travel is positive: the IMF judges this an opportune moment to phase out the dual rate under a clearly sequenced roadmap, reserves are projected to rise to $296.8 million in 2026 and toward $500 million over the medium term, and rising gold and coffee receipts are adding to export earnings. Investors with hard-currency revenues, export-oriented models or development-finance backing are best placed today, and early entrants stand to benefit most as exchange-rate unification proceeds and a stronger macroeconomic track record opens the way for deeper engagement with international lenders.

How do I get in?

Burundi Development Agency (ADB), the official single point of contact for investors, running a one-stop shop for company registration, licences and permits, and mediating disputes with state bodies

Investment Code (Law No. 1/19 of 17 June 2021) giving equal treatment to foreign and domestic investors, protection against expropriation and, for certified projects of at least $500,000 (half that outside Bujumbura), reduced profit tax of 5–25% for five years and VAT and customs exemptions on equipment

International dispute resolution through ICSID, the New York Convention (since 2014) and the CEBAC arbitration and mediation centre

Special economic zones: a legal framework is in place and a pilot SEZ bill passed the National Assembly in January 2025, positioning early investors for a new incentive regime

Banque de la République du Burundi (BRB) manages the Burundian franc; the law permits free transfer of after-tax profits, although foreign currency is currently allocated by priority sector while a roadmap toward a unified exchange rate is prepared

Mining entry under the 2023 Mining Code, with a state stake of 16% in operating companies, alongside gold-sector formalisation and progress toward EITI membership

Energy and infrastructure participation through co-financing with the African Development Bank, EIB, EU and World Bank, including Mission 300 electrification programmes

Trade and market access

Burundi has been a member of the East African Community (EAC) since 2007, giving preferential access to its partner-state markets, and is also a member of COMESA, the Economic Community of Central African States (ECCAS) and the Economic Community of the Great Lakes Countries (CEPGL). It has been a WTO member since 1995, ratified the African Continental Free Trade Area in June 2021 and the COMESA–EAC–SADC Tripartite Free Trade Area in July 2024, and aims to complete technical integration with the East African Payment System by December 2026. Exports of $406.8 million in 2025 were led by gold (41%), coffee (23%), other minerals, beer and tea, with gold volumes to Dubai rising markedly over five years. The Central Corridor through Tanzania to the port of Dar es Salaam is the principal trade route, to be strengthened by the Uvinza–Musongati railway, with Lake Tanganyika providing additional regional links.

The overall risk picture

Burundi's risk profile is that of a frontier economy that has made measurable progress on stabilisation since 2025. Inflation has fallen by more than 35 percentage points, central bank financing of the budget has ended, public debt has fallen to about 42% of GDP and export volumes are rising, all underpinned by a Macroeconomic Stabilisation Plan and continuing IMF surveillance through the 2026 Article IV consultation. The IMF still assesses debt as sustainable but at high risk of distress, and the key practical consideration for investors is foreign-exchange access: currency is rationed while the dual exchange rate persists, and the earlier IMF lending arrangement lapsed in January 2025 without the rates being unified. Regional security in eastern DRC and the closed border with Rwanda should be monitored as part of routine planning. These factors are best managed through hard-currency or export-linked business models, co-investment with development finance institutions, political-risk insurance and early engagement with the ADB and BRB, while the delivery of exchange-rate reform would be a significant positive catalyst for investment.

Business networks

The Burundi Development Agency is the principal channel for investor facilitation and for resolving issues with public bodies, while the CEBAC arbitration and mediation centre provides a local dispute-resolution forum. Development finance institutions are highly active and are the most effective co-investment partners: the African Development Bank, which is financing the Uvinza–Musongati railway, the World Bank Group, including MIGA for political-risk cover, and the European Investment Bank and European Union, whose Global Gateway programme has committed €2 billion over two years to African renewable energy. Regional platforms, including EAC investment forums and the Central Corridor Transit Transport Facilitation Agency, offer further networking and intelligence on trade and logistics.

Who this market suits

Burundi best suits patient, frontier-market investors with a long-term horizon and strong partnership networks: energy investors in hydropower, solar and grid projects co-financed by development banks; mining and minerals investors in gold formalisation, processing and nickel prospects; agribusiness investors in specialty coffee, tea and food processing with dollar-denominated export revenues; and logistics and construction firms positioned around the Tanzania railway and Lake Tanganyika links. Investors whose revenues are in hard currency, or who partner with DFIs, are best placed while exchange-rate reform is completed.

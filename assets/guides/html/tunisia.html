The economy today

Tunisia in October 2026 offers a steady recovery, a deep industrial base closely integrated with Europe and growing foreign investor interest. GDP grew 2.5% in 2025, up from 1.6% in 2024, and 2.4% in the first half of 2026, led by agriculture, services and construction. Foreign investment rose 30.3% in 2025 to 3.5 billion dinars (about $1.2 billion), with industry taking 62.6% and France, Germany and Italy the leading sources, and international investment rose a further 11.5% to about 1.93 billion dinars in the first half of 2026. Some 4,296 foreign companies now operate in the country, concentrated in automotive components, aeronautics, electronics, pharmaceuticals and technical textiles. Investment Law No. 2016-71, a planned Investment Code revision targeting 24-hour company creation, and more than 2.3 GW of renewable-energy tenders in 2026 point to a widening opportunity set.

Tunisia's main structural challenge is fiscal and external financing. Public debt stood at 82.1% of GDP at end-2025 and the government has relied substantially on domestic and central-bank financing rather than an IMF programme, while energy imports have widened the trade deficit. Tunisia has managed this with discipline and resilience: Fitch affirmed its B- rating with a stable outlook in September 2026, reserves stood at 23.6 billion dinars (92 days of imports) in early October, and tourism receipts and workers' remittances of more than 13.2 billion dinars to end-September covered 142% of external debt service. The energy gap is itself a major opportunity, with the ELMED link to Italy and a national goal of 35% renewable electricity by 2030 creating demand for private investment in generation, storage and grids.

How do I get in?

Foreign Investment Promotion Agency (FIPA) provides free facilitation, sector guidance and aftercare, with the Tunisia Investment Authority (TIA) supporting investors through administrative procedures

Investment Law No. 2016-71, allowing 100% foreign ownership in most sectors without prior authorisation, with partial corporate-tax exemptions, investment grants and social-security support in regional development zones

Investment Code revision under the 2026–2030 development plan, targeting company set-up within 24 hours, a unified digital investor portal and digitised land services

Bizerte and Zarzis free zones and the totally exporting enterprise regime, offering customs suspension and tax advantages for export-oriented manufacturing

Central Bank of Tunisia (BCT) foreign-exchange framework: the dinar is not freely convertible, but capital and profits can be repatriated where the investment was financed with imported foreign currency and declared through the BCT's non-resident investment platform

Private power-generation tenders cleared by the High Committee for Private Electricity Generation, supporting the target of 35% of electricity from renewables by 2030

Startup Act offering tax exemptions and easier access to foreign currency for qualifying young technology companies

Trade and market access

Tunisia's market access is anchored in its Association Agreement with the European Union, under which free trade in industrial goods has been fully in place since 2008; the EU took 70.4% of Tunisian exports in the first half of 2026, led by France, Italy and Germany. Tunisia has been a WTO member since 1995 and is also a party to the Agadir Agreement with Egypt, Jordan and Morocco, the Arab Maghreb Union and bilateral agreements with Algeria and Libya. Its ratification of COMESA accession in 2019 and of the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) in 2020 open routes into wider African markets, where exports to Egypt more than doubled in the first half of 2026. Proximity to southern Europe, supported in future by the ELMED power link to Sicily and the planned Enfidha deep-water port, makes Tunisia a natural near-shoring platform serving both Europe and Africa.

The overall risk picture

Tunisia's risk profile reflects an economy with a diversified, Europe-integrated industrial base, a skilled workforce and steady recovery, set against high public debt and a constrained external financing position. The government has chosen to finance itself largely from domestic sources rather than through an IMF programme, and Fitch's affirmation of a B- rating with a stable outlook in September 2026, alongside reserves of 92 days of imports and tourism and remittance inflows covering 142% of external debt service, indicates that this approach has so far been managed. Inflation of 5.6% in October 2026 and a 7% policy rate imply relatively high local borrowing costs, and foreign-exchange controls require careful structuring. Investors who bring capital in as declared foreign currency, earn export revenues, partner with development finance institutions and use FIPA and TIA support are well placed to benefit from Tunisia's competitive costs and proximity to Europe.

Who should I talk to first?

Presidency of the Republic (President Kais Saied) and Prime Minister Sarra Zaafrani Zenzri

Set national economic direction, including the 2026–2030 development plan and the emphasis on domestic financing

Primary reference point for understanding Tunisia's strategic and policy priorities

Foreign Investment Promotion Agency (FIPA)

National investment promotion agency and organiser of the annual Tunisia Investment Forum

Standard first point of contact for market entry, sector intelligence and aftercare

Tunisia Investment Authority (TIA)

Supports investors with administrative procedures and larger investment projects under the 2016 Investment Law

Key counterpart for incentive applications and project facilitation

Central Bank of Tunisia (BCT)

Sets monetary policy (7% policy rate) and administers the Foreign Exchange Code and investment declarations

Essential for structuring capital inflows and securing repatriation rights

Higher Investment Commission

Approves foreign participation above 50% in non-exporting service activities and other projects requiring authorisation

Relevant for service-sector entry and large or strategic projects

STEG (Tunisian Company of Electricity and Gas)

National utility, co-lead of the ELMED interconnector with Italy's Terna and offtaker for private renewable projects

Central partner for power, renewables and grid investment

Gafsa Phosphate Company (CPG) and Tunisian Chemical Group (GCT)

State companies leading phosphate extraction and processing and the expansion programme to 2035

Key counterparts for mining equipment, services and downstream processing partnerships

Business networks

FIPA's annual Tunisia Investment Forum is the leading investor gathering; its 22nd edition in June 2026, held with the African Development Bank, drew around 1,200 participants from more than 30 countries. The national employers' union UTICA and bilateral chambers of commerce, notably those linked to France, Italy and Germany as the largest investing countries, provide practical networks for market entry. Development finance institutions are active across infrastructure and energy: the European Union (through the Connecting Europe Facility), the World Bank, the European Investment Bank, the EBRD and KfW all support the ELMED interconnector, while the African Development Bank is a long-standing partner. The large community of European manufacturers already operating in Tunisia is a valuable source of practical intelligence on operating conditions.

Who this market suits

Tunisia best suits investors seeking a competitive, Europe-adjacent production base: automotive-component, aeronautics, electronics, pharmaceutical and technical-textile manufacturers using the free-zone and totally exporting regimes to serve EU customers; renewable-energy developers and storage and grid specialists targeting the 2.3 GW tender pipeline and ELMED; ICT and outsourcing firms drawing on a skilled engineering workforce; and infrastructure and mining investors engaging with the Enfidha port and phosphate expansion programmes. Export-oriented investors with foreign-currency revenues and development-finance partners are best placed to manage the foreign-exchange and financing environment.

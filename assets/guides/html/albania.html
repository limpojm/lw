The economy today

Albania in November 2026 combines steady growth, a record investment and tourism performance and the fastest-moving EU accession process in the Western Balkans. GDP grew 3.8% in 2025 and 3.6% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, with credit rising 15.5% in the third quarter. Foreign direct investment reached a record €1.64 billion in 2025, the fifth successive annual increase, led by real estate, finance and energy, and a record 12.4 million foreign visitors arrived in 2025. Inflation was 2.9% in September 2026, close to the Bank of Albania's 3% target, and the policy rate has been held at 2.5%. All EU negotiating chapters are now open and four were provisionally closed by October 2026, with the government aiming to conclude talks in 2027 and S&P and Moody's both rating the outlook stable.

Albania's main structural challenge is broadening its growth base beyond tourism, construction and real estate, while strengthening the rule of law that the EU process requires. Goods exports weakened in 2025, real estate absorbed 37% of FDI inflows in the fourth quarter, and agriculture contracted in the second quarter of 2026. The policy response is substantial: large energy projects including the Skavica hydropower plant, solar parks and a planned LNG hub at Vlorë, a new port at Porto Romano, reduced tax regimes for software and automotive components, and EU Growth Plan funding tied to reforms. Prudent public finances, with a 2025 deficit of 1.8% of GDP and public debt of 52.9% of GDP, leave room to finance this programme, creating opportunities in energy, logistics, manufacturing and ICT.

Capital and commercial centre; seat of government, the Bank of Albania and most foreign firms, and home to the TEDA Tirana technological and economic development area

Durrës and Porto Romano

How do I get in?

Albanian Investment Development Agency (AIDA) provides investor information, a one-stop shop for strategic investors, government introductions and aftercare

Strategic investor status, with assisted or special procedures for projects above set thresholds by sector (for example $5.75 million plus 80 jobs in tourism); the government is drafting a new strategic investments law

TEDA Tirana and Durana Tech Park offering serviced land, leases of up to 35 years and reduced or zero corporate tax for qualifying investors

A 15% standard corporate income tax, with reduced 5% rates for software development and automotive components

A freely floating lek, an inflation-targeting central bank and reserves of €7.3 billion at the end of 2025, equal to 7.6 months of imports

EU candidate status, with all negotiating chapters opened and four provisionally closed by October 2026, progressively aligning Albania's rules with the EU single market

EU Western Balkans Growth Plan funds, with €212.8 million released to Albania by May 2026 against reform milestones

Trade and market access

Albania is a WTO member and an EU candidate with a Stabilisation and Association Agreement giving duty-free access for most goods to the EU, its main trading partner and the destination for about two-thirds of goods exports, with Italy a key market. It is also a member of the Central European Free Trade Agreement (CEFTA), giving access to Western Balkan markets. Its Adriatic and Ionian ports at Durrës and Vlorë face Italy and connect to the Pan-European transport corridors, while the Trans Adriatic Pipeline crosses the country, bringing Azerbaijani gas to Italy. In April 2026 state gas company Albgaz signed a 20-year agreement with Aktor LNG USA for about 1 billion cubic metres a year of LNG from 2030, valued at about $6 billion, positioning Vlorë as a future regional gas gateway.

The overall risk picture

Albania's risk profile is that of a stable NATO member and advanced EU candidate with a growing economy, moderate public debt and a clear reform anchor. Prime Minister Edi Rama began a fourth term in September 2025 after his Socialist Party won a majority in the May 2025 election, giving policy continuity, and the government targets EU membership by 2030. Albania was removed from the FATF grey list in October 2023, and S&P (BB) and Moody's (Ba3) both rated the country stable in March 2026. The main issues for investors are rule-of-law reform, the transition to a new strategic investments law and the economy's reliance on tourism and real estate; each is being addressed through the EU accession process and a diversified project pipeline. The Bank of Albania expects inflation may temporarily exceed its 3% target before returning to it, so cost assumptions should allow some headroom.

Business networks

The American Chamber of Commerce in Albania, the Foreign Investors Association of Albania and bilateral chambers including Italian, German and British business groups are the main channels for dialogue with government, alongside the Union of Chambers of Commerce and Industry of Albania. AIDA organises investor events and sector presentations, and the Investment Council provides a forum between government and business. The European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, the European Investment Bank, the World Bank and the Western Balkans Investment Framework finance energy, transport and private-sector projects, while EU Growth Plan funding supports reform-linked investment. Embassies of EU partners and the United States maintain active commercial sections.

Who this market suits

Albania suits investors seeking an early position in a fast-converging EU candidate: tourism and hospitality groups targeting the Riviera coast; energy investors in hydropower, solar and the planned Vlorë gas hub; logistics and port operators around Durrës and Porto Romano; ICT, software and business-process outsourcing firms using reduced tax regimes and multilingual talent; and manufacturers, including automotive component suppliers, serving Italian and wider EU markets. Real-estate investors should undertake careful title due diligence.

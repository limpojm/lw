The economy today

North Macedonia in November 2026 offers investors a low-tax, euro-linked manufacturing base on the main corridors between Central Europe and Greece, with growth gathering pace. GDP grew 3.5% in 2025, according to the IMF, and accelerated to 4.3% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, the strongest rate since late 2024, as gross investment rose 16.5%, construction 21.9% and exports 10.4%. Inflation was 2.7% in September 2026 and the National Bank held its policy rate at 4.25% in November. Foreign exchange reserves reached €5.2 billion at the end of March 2026, and the IMF projects FDI to rise from 1.5% of GDP in 2025 to 3.8% in 2026. The ENKA–Bechtel motorway programme of about 111 km, the country's largest infrastructure project, is advancing on three sections with more than 1,550 people at work.

North Macedonia's main structural challenge is restoring momentum on EU accession and strengthening public finances. The country applied for membership in 2004 and has been a candidate since 2005, but progress depends on constitutional amendments to recognise Bulgarians as a minority, under a 2022 compromise adopted by the EU; these require a two-thirds majority that the government has not secured. The general government deficit was 4.0% of GDP in 2025, meeting its target, and the authorities aim for 3.5% in 2026; public and publicly guaranteed debt was 59.9% of GDP. The base remains solid: a flat 10% tax system, a stable currency, ample reserves and IMF-supported fiscal plans. Continued alignment with EU standards and DFI financing offer opportunities in manufacturing, energy and transport.

How do I get in?

Invest North Macedonia, the government's investment and export promotion agency, provides facilitation and guidance on incentives

Technological industrial development zones (TIDZs) offering a 10-year profit-tax exemption, customs and VAT relief, and up to €500,000 towards construction costs

A flat 10% corporate income tax, with a 1% turnover regime for small companies

A denar managed against the euro, giving euro-based investors a stable currency, supported by reserves of €5.2 billion in March 2026

EU candidate status since 2005 and a Stabilisation and Association Agreement giving preferential access to the EU market

Public infrastructure programmes, including the Corridor 8 and 10d motorways due for completion by December 2029

Trade and market access

North Macedonia is a member of the WTO and of the Central European Free Trade Agreement (CEFTA), and its Stabilisation and Association Agreement with the EU gives most of its goods duty-free access to the EU, its main export market. The denar's stability against the euro lowers currency risk for trade with the euro area. The country sits on Pan-European Corridor 10, linking Serbia and Central Europe with Greece and the port of Thessaloniki, and on Corridor 8, linking the Black Sea with the Adriatic; the new Corridor 8 and 10d motorways will improve links to Albania and Greece by December 2029. Manufacturers in the TIDZs supply European automotive and industrial supply chains.

The overall risk picture

North Macedonia's risk profile is that of a stable, NATO-member EU candidate with a flat-tax system, a currency managed against the euro and comfortable foreign exchange reserves. It is not on the FATF grey list and is not subject to sanctions. The main issues are the stalled EU accession process, which hinges on a constitutional amendment opposed by much of the public, and a fiscal deficit of about 4% of GDP, which the government is addressing with IMF-supported targets. Inflation has been moderate at 2.7% and growth has strengthened in 2026. Investors should plan for the possibility of slower EU integration, monitor budget measures and infrastructure costs, and use the strong incentives in the TIDZs, which offer one of the most attractive packages in the region.

Business networks

The Economic Chamber of North Macedonia, the American Chamber of Commerce in North Macedonia, the Foreign Investors Council and the German-Macedonian Chamber of Commerce are the main business associations, with regular dialogue with government. Invest North Macedonia promotes the country at international forums and supports investors in the TIDZs. The European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, the European Investment Bank, the World Bank and the EU's Western Balkans Investment Framework are active in energy, transport and private-sector finance, and a Hungarian loan facility has been on-lent to companies through commercial banks. Embassies of EU member states, the United Kingdom, Turkey and the United States maintain commercial sections that are a useful source of introductions.

Who this market suits

North Macedonia suits investors seeking a low-cost, low-tax base close to the EU: automotive-component and electrical manufacturers using TIDZ incentives to serve European supply chains; ICT and business-services companies in Skopje; logistics firms on Corridor 10 towards Thessaloniki; construction and engineering companies working on the motorway programme; and renewable-energy and agri-food investors. Investors should plan for a gradual rather than rapid EU accession path.

Investor Entry Routes

  • Enterprise Georgia's Investment Division (Invest in Georgia), under the Ministry of Economy and Sustainable Development, acts as a one-stop shop for foreign investors
  • Law on Promotion and Guarantee of Investment Activity, protecting foreign investors for 10 years against later legislation that changes the conditions of their investment, alongside ICSID membership (since 1992), the New York Convention and a US–Georgia Bilateral Investment Treaty
  • Four free zones (Poti, two in Kutaisi and the Tbilisi Free Zone) offering profit-tax exemption on permitted activities, duty- and VAT-free import of foreign goods and property-tax exemption
  • Distributed-profit tax model since 2017: 15% corporate tax applies only when profits are distributed, while International Company status offers a 5% rate for qualifying IT, software and certain shipping services
  • Free-floating lari with no restrictions on current international payments (IMF Article VIII); investors may convert and repatriate after-tax income freely
  • Development Fund of Georgia (formerly the Partnership Fund) offering co-investment through equity and subordinated loans in early-stage projects
  • Anaklia Deep Sea Port landlord model, under which international terminal operators are expected to invest an estimated $350–400 million in terminal infrastructure

Market-Entry Observation

Georgia in October 2026 is one of the fastest-growing economies in the wider region. GDP grew 7.5% in 2025 and 7.6% in January–August 2026, led by manufacturing, information and communication, finance and transport, and the World Bank projects 7.0% growth for 2026. Foreign direct investment rose 19.3% to $1.9 billion in 2025, with the United Kingdom the largest source ($426.6 million), followed by Azerbaijan and Türkiye. Macroeconomic buffers have strengthened markedly: gross international reserves reached a record $8.14 billion in August 2026, up 56.4% year-on-year and 128.2% of the IMF adequacy metric, while public debt stood at a moderate 34.3% of GDP in 2025. The National Bank of Georgia is holding its policy rate at 8.25% to bring inflation (5.6% in September, driven mainly by Middle East energy prices) back to its 3% target, and multilateral lenders remain active, including the $752 million TC-GATE transport programme approved in June 2026 to strengthen Georgia's Middle Corridor role.

Georgia's main structural consideration is its relationship with the European Union. Its accession process has been de facto halted since the government announced in November 2024 that it would not open talks before the end of 2028; the EU suspended visa-free travel for holders of Georgian diplomatic and official passports in March 2026 and may consider wider measures, with a decision expected around January 2027. The economy has nonetheless continued to grow strongly, and the core commercial architecture remains in place: the EU Deep and Comprehensive Free Trade Area (in force since 2014), free trade agreements with China, EFTA, Türkiye and others, and continued financing from the World Bank, EBRD, ADB and AIIB. For investors, this means focusing on trade, transit and services opportunities that do not depend on the pace of accession, while building in strong governance and compliance processes and positioning for the upside should EU relations improve.

Investor Risk Note

Georgia's risk profile combines strong macroeconomic performance with a political and diplomatic environment that investors should follow closely. Growth of 7.5% in 2025 and 7.6% in the first eight months of 2026, record reserves of $8.14 billion, moderate public debt and a sound banking sector give the economy real resilience, and the World Bank, EBRD, ADB and AIIB continue to commit significant financing. The main considerations are the de facto halt in EU accession, the possibility of wider EU visa measures in early 2027, and targeted US and UK sanctions on Georgian individuals, including the founder of the governing party. These are not country-wide sanctions, and lawful investment in Georgia remains broadly open, but counterparty and beneficial-ownership screening should be standard, supported by sanctions counsel. Russia's occupation of Abkhazia and South Ossetia means investment should be limited to government-controlled territory, and trade businesses should apply export-control checks to re-export flows. With these safeguards in place, investors can benefit from Georgia's growth, low-tax regime and expanding transit role, while positioning for further upside if relations with the EU improve.

Market Access

Georgia has been a WTO member since June 2000 and offers one of the broadest free trade networks in the region: a Deep and Comprehensive Free Trade Area with the EU (provisionally applied since September 2014, with the Association Agreement in force since 2016), a free trade agreement with China (in force since January 2018), and agreements with EFTA, Türkiye, CIS countries, Hong Kong and the United Arab Emirates. EU–Georgia trade reached about €5.2 billion in 2025. In January–August 2026, Georgia's largest export markets were Kyrgyzstan ($577.6 million), China ($543.7 million) and Russia ($500.7 million), with passenger cars, petroleum products and precious-metal ores the leading exports. Georgia is a key link on the Trans-Caspian International Transport Route (Middle Corridor), connecting Central Asia and Azerbaijan with Black Sea ports at Poti and Batumi, the Baku–Tbilisi–Kars railway to Türkiye and, from 2029, the Anaklia deep-water port.

Current International Business Channels

The American Chamber of Commerce in Georgia (AmCham Georgia) is the leading international business association, with members including Bank of Georgia, TBC Group and PwC, while the British Georgian Chamber of Commerce, whose founder members include BP Georgia, Bank of Georgia and EY, supports UK–Georgia business links from London and Tbilisi, and the Georgian Chamber of Commerce and Industry is the main domestic business counterpart. Development finance institutions are deeply engaged: the EBRD has invested almost €6 billion across 322 projects since 1992, the World Bank Group's active portfolio totals $1.3 billion, the ADB and AIIB are co-financing the TC-GATE transport programme, and the US Development Finance Corporation and its predecessor have committed more than $780 million in Georgia since 1993. These institutions are valuable sources of co-financing, political-risk cover and practical market intelligence.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Georgia suits investors seeking a fast-growing, low-tax and open economy on a key Eurasian trade route: port, logistics and rail operators drawn by Anaklia terminal concessions, Poti and the Middle Corridor; infrastructure investors co-financing alongside the World Bank, EBRD, ADB and AIIB; financial-services investors in a sector that attracted a third of 2025 FDI; IT and software companies using International Company status; tourism and hospitality investors in an economy where inbound travel income is over 12% of GDP; and export-oriented manufacturers using the free industrial zones and wide free trade network. UK investors, already Georgia's largest source of FDI, are well placed. All investors should apply robust sanctions screening and governance standards and monitor developments in EU–Georgia relations.

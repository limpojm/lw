The economy today

The United Kingdom remains one of the world's most open and attractive investment destinations, ranking second in Europe for foreign direct investment projects in 2025 with 730 projects and the highest number of FDI-related jobs, 28,867, of any European country. The United States accounted for 25% of projects and India 10%, and the UK led Europe for technology investment. GDP grew 0.5% quarter-on-quarter and 1.4% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, led by information and communication and professional services, and the Office for Budget Responsibility forecasts growth of 1.2% in 2026. Trade policy is opening new markets: the UK–India agreement entered into force on 15 July 2026, and a UK–Gulf Cooperation Council deal concluded in May 2026 is expected to add £3.7 billion a year to the economy in the long run.

The UK's main structural challenge is combining fiscal discipline with stronger growth as higher energy prices push up inflation. CPI inflation rose to 3.5% in September 2026, and the Bank of England raised Bank Rate to 4% on 5 November 2026. Andy Burnham became Prime Minister in July 2026, and Chancellor John Healey's first Budget on 28 October 2026 kept corporation tax at 25%, raised the first-year capital allowance to 50% and introduced a new Founders' Relief, while the OBR cut fiscal headroom to £15 billion and projects borrowing falling from 4.2% of GDP in 2026 to 2.1% in 2029/30. A stable tax roadmap, independent institutions, deep capital markets and a resetting of relations with the EU underpin long-term resilience and create opportunities in energy, technology and infrastructure.

How do I get in?

The Office for Investment, working with the Department for Business and Trade, supports major foreign investments with dedicated account management, and regional agencies provide local support

National Security and Investment Act 2021: mandatory notification for acquisitions in 17 sensitive sectors, reviewed by the Cabinet Office's Investment Security Unit; 95.6% of notifications in 2025/26 were cleared without further action

Corporation tax at a main rate of 25% (19% for small profits), confirmed in the October 2026 Budget, with full expensing for plant and machinery and a first-year allowance rising to 50% from January 2027

Freely convertible pound sterling and no capital controls, with free repatriation of profits and capital

Listings on the London Stock Exchange and access to one of the world's deepest capital markets

Freeports and Investment Zones in England, Scotland and Wales offering tax and customs incentives for qualifying investments

Over 70 trade agreements, including the UK–India Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement in force since 15 July 2026 and membership of the CPTPP

Trade and market access

The United Kingdom is a WTO member outside the EU, with tariff-free trade in goods with the EU under the Trade and Cooperation Agreement, and a reset of EU relations under way, including talks on linking emissions-trading systems ahead of a UK–EU summit later in 2026. The UK has over 70 trade agreements, is a member of the CPTPP, and its trade agreement with India entered into force on 15 July 2026; a UK–GCC agreement was concluded in May 2026 and awaits signature. The United States, the EU, China and India are major trading partners, and the 2025 UK–US Economic Prosperity Deal reduced some US tariffs on UK goods. Northern Ireland offers unique access to both the UK and EU goods markets under the Windsor Framework, and ports such as Felixstowe, Southampton and Liverpool connect the UK with global markets.

The overall risk picture

The United Kingdom offers investors deep capital markets, the rule of law, an independent central bank and a well-established framework for foreign investment. The main near-term issues are inflation, which reached 3.5% in September 2026, a higher Bank Rate of 4%, and tight public finances, with the OBR expecting net debt to peak at around 97% of GDP in 2028/29. Investors in sensitive sectors must notify acquisitions under the National Security and Investment Act; the regime is transparent and the vast majority of transactions are cleared, but early legal advice is essential. The UK applies extensive sanctions against Russia and other jurisdictions, and holds the FATF Presidency, with strong anti-money-laundering standards. Overall the UK remains a low-risk, highly liquid market, and a new Prime Minister and Chancellor have maintained the commitment to stable business taxes.

Licensing and conduct requirements for financial-services investors

National Wealth Fund and British Business Bank

Co-investment partners for major projects

Business networks

The Confederation of British Industry, the British Chambers of Commerce, TheCityUK and techUK are the main business and sector bodies, with regular dialogue with government on tax, trade and regulation. Bilateral chambers such as BritishAmerican Business and the UK India Business Council support investors from key source markets, and regional growth and investment agencies, including Scottish Development International and Invest Northern Ireland, provide local support. Government-led events such as investment summits, and the UK's trade envoy and embassy networks, are useful for introductions, while the National Wealth Fund and British Business Bank offer co-investment.

Who this market suits

The United Kingdom suits a wide range of investors: financial-services, fintech and asset-management firms using London's capital markets; technology, AI and life-sciences companies drawing on world-class universities in London, Oxford, Cambridge, Manchester and Edinburgh; energy investors targeting offshore wind, grids and nuclear; and manufacturers and logistics firms using Freeports, Investment Zones and new trade agreements with India and the Gulf. Investors in sensitive sectors such as defence, energy, data and advanced technology should plan for National Security and Investment Act notification.

The economy today

Myanmar enters late 2026 with a new political structure and early signs of economic stabilisation. Following elections held in phases in December 2025 and January 2026, Min Aung Hlaing was sworn in as President on 10 April 2026, handing command of the armed forces to General Ye Win Oo. The World Bank estimates the economy contracted by 2.0% in FY2025/26, with activity improving towards the year-end, and projects growth of 2% in FY2026/27; the ADB's September 2026 outlook forecasts 2.2% in 2026 and 2.7% in 2027. Inflation rose to 24.6% in April 2026 as higher global fuel prices fed through. The Myanmar Investment Commission continues to approve projects, and approved FDI reached $690 million in FY2024/25, led by oil and gas ($357 million) and manufacturing ($183 million), with Singapore, China and Thailand the leading cumulative sources.

Myanmar's central challenge is the combination of internal conflict and international restrictions. The civil war that followed the 2021 coup has displaced more than 3.5 million people, and the March 2025 earthquake caused an estimated $11 billion of direct damage, around 14% of GDP. The military has regained around 27 towns and key trade roads in 2026, including the Mandalay–Lashio and Myawaddy–Kawkareik corridors, and 13 ethnic armed organisations have joined talks, although fighting continues in central Myanmar. The US, UK, EU and Canada maintain targeted sanctions, and Myanmar remains on the FATF call-for-action list. The US has delisted several individuals since 2025 and has reportedly opened talks with the new government, signalling that the external environment can evolve. For prepared investors, reconstruction needs, a population of around 55 million and Myanmar's position between China, India and ASEAN offer longer-term opportunity within lawful limits.

How do I get in?

Myanmar Investment Commission (MIC) and its secretariat, the Directorate of Investment and Company Administration (DICA), which approve permits and endorsements and register companies

Myanmar Investment Law 2016, providing MIC permits, endorsements, tax incentives and land-use rights for qualifying projects

Special Economic Zones, led by the operational Thilawa SEZ near Yangon, offering a separate one-stop regime for licensing, customs and incentives; Kyaukphyu and Dawei remain at planning stage

Central Bank of Myanmar oversight of foreign exchange; the CBM reference rate has been used again for customs and trade calculations since January 2025, and conversion and repatriation should be planned with local banks well in advance

Sanctions-compliant structuring: US, UK, EU and Canadian measures are targeted rather than comprehensive, so non-sanctioned activity is lawful but requires screening of every counterparty and avoidance of restricted sectors

Partnership with established regional (Singaporean, Thai, Japanese, Chinese) operators already present in power, manufacturing and logistics

Trade and market access

Myanmar is a member of ASEAN, with tariff-free access to most goods across the ASEAN Free Trade Area and participation in ASEAN's free trade agreements with China, Japan, Korea, India and Australia–New Zealand, and has been a WTO member since 1995. Its main trading partners are China, Thailand, Bangladesh and India, with natural gas flowing by pipeline to Thailand and China and agricultural, fisheries, mineral and garment exports making up much of the rest. Overland trade runs mainly through Muse to Yunnan and Myawaddy to Thailand, both on corridors that reopened to government-controlled traffic in 2025–26, while Yangon and Thilawa handle most seaborne trade. The China–Myanmar Economic Corridor, a planned 1,700 km link from Kunming to the Indian Ocean via Kyaukphyu, gives Myanmar long-term potential as a land bridge between China, South Asia and ASEAN, subject to security conditions.

The overall risk picture

Myanmar is a legally restricted market, and compliance is the starting point for any engagement. Western governments have not accepted the 2025–26 elections as restoring democratic rule, and the US, UK, EU and Canada maintain targeted sanctions: the US blocks the Ministry of Defense, the two state banks handling foreign exchange (MFTB and MICB) and the gems and timber enterprises, bars US persons from providing financial services to MOGE and can designate jet fuel suppliers; the EU renewed its measures until April 2027, including the MOGE listing; and Canada bans aviation-fuel exports. Myanmar also remains on the FATF call-for-action list. These regimes are targeted rather than comprehensive, so lawful activity with non-designated private counterparties remains possible, and the US delistings of 2025–26 and reported talks indicate that the policy environment is not static. The economy is stabilising after the earthquake and conflict-related contraction, with modest growth forecast for 2026–27. Investors should obtain specialist sanctions and legal advice, screen counterparties continuously, avoid sanctioned sectors and conflict-linked resources, and treat Myanmar as a market where careful positioning today builds readiness for any future normalisation.

Who should I talk to first?

Office of the President (Min Aung Hlaing)

Sworn in on 10 April 2026 after leading the military government since 2021; sets economic and security policy, with General Ye Win Oo as Commander-in-Chief

Reference point for strategic direction; any engagement requires prior sanctions screening of officials and entities

Myanmar Investment Commission / DICA

Approves investment permits and endorsements and registers companies under the Investment Law 2016

Standard first point of contact for permits, incentives and company registration

Central Bank of Myanmar

Sets foreign-exchange rules and the reference rate, and supervises banks

Key for conversion, repatriation and banking-channel planning

Ministry of Planning and Finance

Sets fiscal, tax and customs policy, including exchange-rate rules for customs valuation

Relevant for tax incentives and import-duty matters

Myanma Oil and Gas Enterprise (MOGE)

State company holding interests in offshore gas fields and gas exports; subject to US financial-services restrictions and EU listing

No engagement for US or EU persons; others should take specialist advice before any energy-sector involvement

Thilawa SEZ Management Committee

Administers Myanmar's most developed special economic zone near Yangon

Practical entry route for manufacturing and logistics with established infrastructure

ASEAN and its Special Envoy on Myanmar

Leads regional diplomacy on Myanmar's political crisis and its relations with the bloc

Useful barometer of regional normalisation and future market-access conditions

Business networks

The Union of Myanmar Federation of Chambers of Commerce and Industry (UMFCCI) is the principal domestic business body, but it and many local conglomerates should be screened carefully, as some business groups have military links. Foreign chambers representing Japanese, Singaporean, Thai, Korean and European businesses in Yangon remain useful for practical intelligence on operating conditions. Multilateral lenders have restricted their programmes: the ADB has held sovereign disbursements and new contracts since February 2021, and the World Bank focuses on monitoring and analysis, whose Myanmar Economic Monitor is a valuable data source. Investors should also draw on specialist sanctions counsel, risk consultancies and human-rights due-diligence advisers.

Who this market suits

Myanmar suits a narrow group of investors with high risk tolerance and strong compliance capability: regional manufacturing and logistics investors, particularly from Japan, Singapore, Thailand and China, operating in Yangon and the Thilawa SEZ; suppliers of food, medicines, construction materials and power equipment serving reconstruction demand with non-designated private counterparties; and agribusiness and consumer-goods companies with robust supply-chain tracing. Most Western institutional investors are best served by a watching brief, maintaining legal analysis, ASEAN-based relationships and scenario planning, so that they are ready to act quickly if sanctions, political conditions or the conflict change.

    Screen counterparties and ownership chainsCheck every partner, bank and supplier against sanctions lists and military-ownership links, and repeat screening throughout the relationship.

    Engage DICA and the Myanmar Investment CommissionClarify permit, endorsement and incentive options under the Investment Law 2016 once the compliance analysis is complete.
  4. 4
    Assess the Thilawa SEZ for manufacturing and logisticsConsider the zone's established infrastructure and one-stop regime as a lower-risk base for export-oriented operations.

    Build a normalisation watching briefTrack sanctions changes, the FATF review, ASEAN engagement and conflict trends to be ready to scale quickly if conditions improve.

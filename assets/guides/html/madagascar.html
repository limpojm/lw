The economy today

Madagascar in October 2026 offers investors world-class mineral, agricultural and tourism assets backed by continued engagement with international financial institutions. GDP grew 4.3% in 2024 and an estimated 3.1% in 2025, a year marked by a political transition, and the IMF projects a recovery to 3.8% in 2026 and 4.5% in 2027. On 30 September 2026 the IMF Executive Board completed the combined third and fourth reviews of Madagascar's $658 million Extended Credit Facility and Resilience and Sustainability Facility programmes, releasing about $155 million and noting that all end-December 2025 quantitative performance criteria were met. Foreign direct investment rose to $304 million in 2025 from $231 million in 2024, lifting the inward stock to $5.25 billion, while gross reserves reached $3.68 billion at end-June 2026, around seven months of imports, and the ariary appreciated about 7% against the dollar in the first half of the year. The 2023 Investment Code and Mining Code, the reopening of mining permits and a transition government focused on agriculture, energy, infrastructure and governance provide the framework for new entry.

Madagascar's main structural challenge is infrastructure resilience, above all reliable electricity and water and the capacity to absorb tropical cyclones. Cyclone damage in early 2026 temporarily halted the Ambatovy nickel mine, yet operations restarted within about three months, demonstrating the recovery capacity of the country's largest industrial assets. The authorities are addressing the underlying constraints: a recovery plan for the national utility JIRAMA, an automatic fuel-pricing mechanism to be fully applied by January 2027, Madagascar's first green budget and a National Climate Finance Strategy under the IMF's resilience programme, and a new World Bank energy programme targeting reliable power for more than 2.5 million people by 2030. For investors, the energy gap is itself the opportunity, with strong demand for hydropower, solar, mini-grid and storage capacity, and for climate-resilient ports, roads and agricultural supply chains.

Where are the opportunities?

Toamasina (east coast)

Principal deep-water port and main gateway for international trade, with an international container terminal under long-term concession and a port extension under way

Port logistics, nickel and cobalt processing, trade and warehousing

Moramanga / Alaotra-Mangoro

Site of the Ambatovy nickel-cobalt mine, which produced around 29,000 tonnes of nickel in 2025 and restarted after cyclone repairs in May 2026

Location of the Toliara (Vara Mada) mineral sands project, whose January 2026 feasibility study shows a 38-year mine life and a net present value of $1.8 billion

Vanilla, spices, agro-processing, traceability and export services

Nosy Be and Antsiranana (north)

Leading island tourism destination and a large natural deep-water bay in the far north

How do I get in?

Economic Development Board of Madagascar (EDBM), the investment promotion agency and one-stop shop for company registration, tax and social security numbers, investor visas and work permits

Investment Code (Law No. 2023-002 of 27 July 2023) permitting up to 100% foreign ownership in most sectors (telecoms capped at 66%) and providing for ICSID arbitration of investor-state disputes

Free Zone regime (Law No. 2007-037) for export-oriented companies exporting at least 95% of output, offering 2–15 years of tax exemptions and duty-free inputs; more than 225 companies use it, 46% in textiles

Mining Code (Law No. 2023-007) which ended the suspension of new mining permits in place since 2010, with a 5% royalty and Malagasy employment requirements

Banky Foiben'i Madagasikara (central bank) oversight of the floating ariary; export proceeds must be repatriated within 90 days (goods) or 30 days (services), and larger outward transfers require approval in practice

Long-term land access through renewable emphyteutic leases of up to 99 years, as foreign investors cannot hold freehold title directly

Power-sector entry through independent generation and renewable projects, including self-generators selling surplus power under regulator conditions and the planned 120 MW Volobe hydropower scheme

Trade and market access

Madagascar is a member of the Southern African Development Community (SADC), the Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa (COMESA) and the Indian Ocean Commission, a WTO member since 1995, and ratified the African Continental Free Trade Area agreement in November 2024. It applies an interim Economic Partnership Agreement with the EU as part of the Eastern and Southern Africa group, and the EU is its largest export destination, taking around 31.5% of goods exports in 2024, while the United States took 15.4%. AGOA, extended in September 2026 to December 2028, provides duty-free US access for eligible countries, and Madagascar's apparel, vanilla and titanium-ore exports have been its main beneficiaries. Toamasina is the main gateway to Indian Ocean shipping lanes, complemented by Ehoala and Toliara in the south, positioning Madagascar on routes linking Southern Africa, the Gulf and Asia.

The overall risk picture

Madagascar's risk profile combines a political transition with exceptional resource and agricultural endowments and continued international financial support. The October 2025 change of government led the African Union to suspend Madagascar, and investors should treat this as a material governance and compliance consideration, taking legal advice on counterparties, contract authority and the stability of agreed terms. At the same time, SADC has kept Madagascar engaged, the transition roadmap provides for a national consultation, a constitutional vote and elections in 2027, and the IMF completed its programme reviews in September 2026, releasing about $155 million. Reserves of around seven months of imports and an appreciating ariary in the first half of 2026 provide a buffer. The principal operational risks, namely power reliability, cyclone exposure, foreign-exchange procedures and the annual AGOA eligibility review, can be managed through captive power, resilient design, clear transfer arrangements agreed at entry and diversified export markets. Investors who establish relationships during the transition will be well placed as institutional normalisation proceeds.

Who should I talk to first?

Presidency of the Refoundation of the Republic (Col. Michael Randrianirina)

Leads the transition and its roadmap toward a constitutional vote and elections in 2027; large investment projects are referred to the Presidency

Primary reference point for strategic direction and for approval of major projects

Economic Development Board of Madagascar (EDBM)

Investment promotion agency and one-stop shop for registrations, visas and permits

Standard first point of contact for market entry and incentive guidance

Banky Foiben'i Madagasikara (central bank)

Sets monetary policy, manages the floating ariary and oversees foreign-exchange transfers

Key counterpart for repatriation, transfer approvals and banking matters

Ministry of Economy and Finance

Leads fiscal policy, the IMF programme, tax incentives and approvals of larger foreign-currency transfers

Relevant for fiscal terms, incentives and macroeconomic dialogue

Ministry of Mines

Administers the 2023 Mining Code, permits and large mining agreements, including the Toliara negotiations

Essential partner for any mining or critical-minerals investment

JIRAMA (national electricity and water utility)

State utility at the centre of the power-sector recovery plan supported by the IMF and World Bank

Key offtaker and counterpart for independent power and water projects

Fonds Souverain Malagasy (sovereign wealth fund)

State investment vehicle established in 2022 that holds interests linked to mining revenues

Potential co-investment partner in strategic projects

Business networks

The American Chamber of Commerce in Madagascar, the Chambre de Commerce et d'Industrie France Madagascar and the Groupement des Entreprises de Madagascar (GEM) are the leading business associations, alongside the Groupement des Entreprises Franches et Partenaires (GEFP) for export-processing and textile investors. The EDBM organises investor outreach and sector briefings. The World Bank, IFC, African Development Bank and the European Union maintain active programmes in energy, infrastructure, agriculture and private-sector development, and the IMF's ongoing programme provides a regular window on policy direction. Major mining operators at Ambatovy, QMM and Toliara are a useful source of practical intelligence on permitting, logistics and community engagement.

Who this market suits

Madagascar best suits investors with a long-term horizon and experience of frontier markets: mining and critical-minerals investors drawn by nickel, cobalt, ilmenite, zircon and rare earths; renewable-energy and infrastructure investors responding to a large and well-identified power gap; apparel and light-manufacturing investors using the Free Zone regime and preferential access to the EU and US; agribusiness investors in vanilla, spices and other high-value crops; and tourism investors in Nosy Be and coastal destinations. DFI-backed and blended-finance structures are particularly well suited during the transition, and all investors should build political-transition and climate scenarios into project planning.

Investor Entry Routes

  • Enterprise Armenia (formally the Investment Support Center), the national investment promotion agency, providing guidance on investment, company registration and incentives
  • Law on Foreign Investment (1994) granting national treatment and protection against expropriation, reinforced by bilateral investment treaties with 40 countries (including the US–Armenia BIT) and membership of the ICSID and New York Conventions
  • Four free economic zones (Alliance, Meridian, Meghri and Ecos) offering exemptions from VAT, profit tax, customs duties and property tax
  • Central Bank of Armenia oversight of a freely convertible dram under a managed float, with no restrictions on converting, transferring or repatriating capital, profits and dividends
  • TRIPP Development Company, the US–Armenia vehicle (74% US, 26% Armenia) for the TRIPP transit route, which is able to bring in partners for individual projects
  • Co-investment alongside development finance institutions, including the EBRD (€3.2 billion invested across 249 projects), the ADB ($2.1 billion committed) and the US DFC
  • Export-oriented entry using the EU Comprehensive and Enhanced Partnership Agreement (CEPA) and EU GSP+ preferences alongside tariff-free access to the Eurasian Economic Union

Market-Entry Observation

Armenia in October 2026 is one of the fastest-growing economies in the wider region. GDP grew 7.2% in 2025, led by construction (+21%), information and communications (+18.6%) and finance and insurance (+14.7%), and growth reached 6.7% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, with economic activity up 7.3% in January–August. Fundamentals are sound: public debt fell to 41.7% of GDP at end-July 2026 from 46.8% at end-2025, gross reserves stood at $6.2 billion (4.1 months of imports), and the budget recorded a surplus in the first half of 2026. Foreign direct investment into the real sector rose to about $605 million in 2025 from $132 million in 2024, led by investors from the United States, Luxembourg, Kazakhstan, Singapore, Switzerland and France. The June 2026 parliamentary elections returned the governing Civil Contract party with a majority of 64 of 105 seats, supporting policy continuity, while a precautionary IMF Stand-By Arrangement, deepening EU partnership and the US-backed TRIPP framework signed in June 2026 underpin the reform and connectivity agenda.

Armenia's main structural challenge is connectivity: two of its four land borders, with Azerbaijan and Türkiye, remain closed, and up to a quarter of foreign trade passes through Iran, which was affected by the regional conflict from February 2026. The economy has proved resilient: the Iranian border stayed open to cargo, a ceasefire took effect in April, and growth accelerated in the second quarter. Armenia is also turning the challenge into opportunity. The peace agreement with Azerbaijan was initialled at the White House in August 2025 and the government aims to sign and ratify it before 2031; the TRIPP route is moving through ratification; Armenia and Türkiye are preparing to reopen the Gyumri–Kars railway; and new southern road works are shortening the north–south corridor. Each of these opens the prospect of Armenia becoming a transit hub under the government's Crossroads of Peace initiative, with early-mover opportunities in logistics, infrastructure and services.

Investor Risk Note

Armenia's risk profile is that of a fast-growing, reform-minded economy whose principal constraints are geographic and regional rather than macroeconomic. Growth of 7.2% in 2025 and 6.7% in the second quarter of 2026, falling public debt, a budget surplus in the first half of 2026 and reserves of $6.2 billion show that the fundamentals are sound, and the IMF describes external and financial-sector buffers as strong. The closed borders with Azerbaijan and Türkiye and the reliance on Iranian routes are real considerations, but the direction of travel is positive: a peace agreement initialled in August 2025, the US-backed TRIPP framework, renewed work on the Gyumri–Kars railway and a decisive election result in June 2026 that supports continuity. Armenia is not subject to sanctions; however, its EAEU membership and Russian trade links mean that investors should apply rigorous counterparty, end-use and sanctions screening with qualified counsel, particularly for trade, dual-use goods and financial flows. With that discipline in place, investors can position for Armenia's emergence as a regional transit and services hub.

Market Access

Armenia has been a member of the Eurasian Economic Union (EAEU) since January 2015, giving tariff-free access to Russia, Belarus, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan, and benefits from the EAEU's free-trade agreement with Iran. It joined the WTO in 2003 and has a Comprehensive and Enhanced Partnership Agreement with the EU in force since March 2021, alongside EU GSP+ preferences giving duty-free access for around 66% of EU tariff lines; the EU Integration Act of 2025 and the first EU–Armenia Summit in Yerevan in May 2026 signal a deepening relationship, supported by the EU's €270 million Resilience and Growth Plan. A Trade and Investment Framework Agreement with the United States has been in place since 2015. Leading export markets include the UAE, Russia, Hong Kong and China. Trade currently flows north through Georgia to the Black Sea and south through Iran, and the TRIPP route, the planned reopening of the Gyumri–Kars railway and the Crossroads of Peace initiative are set to add east–west links that would connect Armenia more directly to Türkiye, Azerbaijan and the Middle Corridor.

Current International Business Channels

The American Chamber of Commerce in Armenia, founded in 2000 and representing more than 170 member companies, is the leading independent business association and an active voice in policy advocacy. Enterprise Armenia provides direct facilitation and incentive guidance. The EBRD, which has invested €3.2 billion across 249 projects and is implementing a 2025–30 strategy focused on private-sector competitiveness and green infrastructure, the Asian Development Bank ($2.1 billion committed) and the World Bank are all active in the country, and the US DFC has added a new channel through its backing of TRIPP. The EU's partnership framework, including the Resilience and Growth Plan, offers further entry points for European investors.

Investor Profile Best Suited

Armenia suits investors seeking exposure to a fast-growing, well-managed economy at an early stage of regional reconnection: technology and IT investors drawing on a high-tech workforce that has grown to more than 30,000 and on the Alliance and Ecos zones; infrastructure, logistics and construction investors positioning for TRIPP, the Gyumri–Kars railway and north–south road works; mining and mining-services investors in the Syunik belt; and export-oriented manufacturers and agro-processors using EAEU, CEPA and GSP+ access. Development-finance co-investors and diaspora-linked investors are well placed to move early, and all investors should build sanctions screening and routing flexibility into their plans.

The economy today

Oman in October 2026 stands out as the Gulf's most resilient economy. GDP grew 2.3% in 2025 and 2.6% in the first quarter of 2026, with oil activity up 4.6% and non-oil activity up 2.4%, and the IMF's October 2026 Regional Economic Outlook projects 4.1% growth this year, while the GCC as a whole contracts 4.8%, making Oman the region's standout performer. Fiscal discipline has transformed the public finances: government debt has fallen to about 36% of GDP from 68% in 2020, the first-half 2026 deficit narrowed to just RO17 million, and Fitch and S&P now rate Oman investment grade at BBB-. The stock of foreign direct investment rose 8.1% to RO31.4 billion (about $82 billion) at end-2025, committed investment in economic and free zones reached RO22.4 billion, and the India–Oman CEPA, in force since June 2026, opens a major new market for Omani-based producers.

Oman's main structural challenge is its position beside the Strait of Hormuz during the 2026 Iran war. Between late February and June, drone strikes hit Duqm, Salalah and Sohar and vessels in Omani waters, and in mid-October Hormuz traffic remained close to a standstill. Oman's geography has nonetheless become its principal advantage: its main ports open directly onto the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea, so its trade routes remain unobstructed (S&P), and Sohar, Salalah and Duqm are handling cargo diverted from inside the Gulf, while goods moving on the new Sharjah–Oman logistics corridor rose more than 66% in its first three months. Higher oil prices have strengthened revenues, liquid government assets exceed 40% of GDP, and Oman's long-standing diplomacy keeps it at the centre of talks on future Hormuz navigation. For investors, the opportunity lies in Oman's role as the Gulf's open-sea gateway, with demand for ports, storage, logistics and energy infrastructure set to outlast the conflict.

How do I get in?

Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Investment Promotion (Invest Oman) provides facilitation, sector guidance and online company registration for foreign investors

Foreign Capital Investment Law (in force since January 2020) permits up to 100% foreign ownership in most sectors and removes minimum-capital requirements, subject to a limited restricted-activities list

Public Authority for Special Economic Zones and Free Zones (OPAZ), covering Duqm, Sohar, Salalah and Al Mazunah plus industrial estates, with RO22.4 billion (about $58 billion) of committed investment and 325 new agreements in 2025

Omani rial pegged to the US dollar, with free repatriation of capital and profits; the Central Bank of Oman moves its policy rate in line with the US Federal Reserve

Competitive tax regime: 15% corporate income tax and 5% VAT, with a 5% personal income tax applying from 2028 only to individuals earning over RO42,000 a year

Sector-entry routes through Ministry of Energy and Minerals concessions, Hydrom green hydrogen land blocks and Nama Power and Water Procurement tenders for independent power projects

Preferential trade platforms, including the US–Oman Free Trade Agreement (2009) and the India–Oman CEPA (in force 1 June 2026), for export-oriented manufacturing

Trade and market access

Oman is a member of the Gulf Cooperation Council customs union and common market, giving access to a market of more than 60 million people, and has been a WTO member since 2000. It is the only GCC state apart from Bahrain with a bilateral free trade agreement with the United States, in force since 2009, and its Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement with India entered into force on 1 June 2026, granting duty-free access on about 98% of Omani tariff lines and preferential access to the Indian market; bilateral trade reached $11.2 billion in FY2025-26. Asia is the main destination for Omani crude and LNG exports, with oil and gas making up 65% of total exports in H1 2026. Because Sohar, Duqm and Salalah sit outside the Strait of Hormuz and the Sharjah–Oman corridor links Omani ports by road to the UAE, Oman has become a natural gateway for Gulf-bound trade.

The overall risk picture

Oman's risk profile is that of a well-managed, investment-grade economy navigating a major regional shock from a position of growing strength. Debt has roughly halved since 2020 to about 36% of GDP, the budget is close to balance, liquid government assets exceed 40% of GDP and gross foreign currency reserves are close to 20% of GDP, and S&P affirmed Oman at BBB- with a stable outlook in September 2026. The principal risks are external: the 2026 Iran war brought drone strikes on Omani ports and energy facilities between late February and June, and the Strait of Hormuz remained largely closed in mid-October with no ceasefire in place. Oman's open-sea ports, its unobstructed export routes and its standing as a trusted intermediary are significant mitigants, and the IMF expects Oman to be the only GCC economy to grow in 2026. Investors should commission security, insurance and supply-chain due diligence and monitor diplomatic developments, while recognising that Oman's geography positions it to benefit from the Gulf's search for resilient trade routes.

Business networks

The Oman Chamber of Commerce and Industry is the principal domestic business body and a useful partner for introductions and sector intelligence, alongside the American Chamber of Commerce in Oman and the British Business Group Oman. The United Kingdom is by far the largest foreign investor, accounting for more than half of Oman's FDI stock, and the United States is second, so Western energy majors and service companies offer deep in-country networks. Invest Oman and OPAZ hold regular investor events and signing ceremonies, such as the June 2026 Duqm round, while the India–Oman CEPA is generating new bilateral business-council and trade-mission activity. Future Fund Oman and the Oman Investment Authority are increasingly active co-investors and convenors for private-sector projects.

Who this market suits

Oman suits investors seeking a stable, investment-grade Gulf base with open-sea access: port, logistics and storage investors positioned to serve trade diverted from inside the Gulf; energy-transition investors in green hydrogen, renewables and power, building on the Duqm and Hydrom pipelines; industrial and export-oriented manufacturers using the free zones and the new India CEPA and US FTA; and food-security, tourism and services investors aligned with Vision 2040. Oil, gas and petrochemical investors benefit from established infrastructure and long-standing international partnerships, and should build regional security and insurance scenarios into project planning.

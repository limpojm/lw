The economy today

Suriname in November 2026 stands at the threshold of a transformational oil era, underpinned by a steady non-resource economy. The IMF estimates that non-resource GDP grew 4.4% in 2025 and projects overall growth of 3.9% in 2026, before offshore oil lifts growth to around 30% in 2028. Foreign direct investment rose to about $2.0 billion in 2025 from $675 million in 2024, according to UNCTAD data, driven by TotalEnergies' $10.5 billion GranMorgu project, which targets first oil in 2028, while Petronas plans a floating LNG development at Sloanea with first gas targeted for 2030. Suriname completed its IMF-supported programme in March 2025 and has legislated a strengthened Savings and Stabilization Fund (SSFS) and fiscal rules to manage future resource revenues, and inflation has eased to 7.3% in September 2026 from 13.0% at end-2025.

Suriname's main structural task is to consolidate macroeconomic stability in the years before oil revenues arrive. Fiscal and monetary slippages in 2025 widened the overall fiscal deficit to 10.0% of GDP and weakened the currency, while central government debt stood at an estimated 106% of GDP, mainly as a result of a liability management operation. The IMF has called for fiscal adjustment in 2026, projecting the deficit to narrow to 5.4% of GDP and debt to fall to about 96% of GDP, and gross reserves of $1.31 billion at end-2025 are projected to rise in 2026. The SSFS law, which channels all mineral revenue into an independently managed fund, together with the fiscal-rule framework provides the institutional base for converting oil income into lasting stability, and offers investors a clear policy anchor for the post-2028 period.

How do I get in?

Suriname Investment and Trade Agency (SITA), the national investment promotion agency operating under the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, International Business and International Cooperation, acts as a one-stop shop for foreign investors

Investment Law incentives including tax holidays of up to nine years (with a possible extension for large projects), import-duty exemptions on capital goods and accelerated depreciation

Production-sharing contracts with Staatsolie, which holds petroleum rights, under an open-door offshore licensing programme

Company registration through the Chamber of Commerce and Industry (KKF), with the N.V. (limited liability company) the most common structure

Central Bank of Suriname (CBvS) oversight of a flexible exchange-rate regime for the Surinamese dollar; investors should confirm current transfer procedures for dividends and capital with the CBvS and their bank

International protections including the New York Convention on foreign arbitral awards and MIGA membership, with Suriname pursuing the WTO Investment Facilitation for Development Agreement

Trade and market access

Suriname is a member of CARICOM and CARIFORUM and has been a WTO member, giving access to Caribbean regional markets and a framework for trade with Europe, alongside trade agreements with Brazil, Venezuela and Costa Rica. Gold dominates exports, accounting for about 80% of the total in 2023, with Switzerland (50.4%) and the United Arab Emirates (28.5%) the leading destinations, followed by Guyana and the United States. The Port of Paramaribo, Kuldipsingh Port and Johan Adolf Pengel International Airport are the main gateways, while land borders with Guyana and French Guiana link Suriname to a fast-growing regional energy economy and to the edge of the EU. From 2028, crude exports from GranMorgu will add a major new export stream and broaden the country's trading relationships.

The overall risk picture

Suriname's risk profile reflects a small economy on the verge of a major resource transition, with a sound non-resource growth base and a clear institutional framework for managing future oil wealth. The 2025 fiscal and monetary slippages raised debt and inflation, but inflation has since eased to 7.3% and the IMF projects narrowing deficits and falling debt from 2026. The SSFS law and fiscal rules adopted in December 2024 give investors visibility on how oil revenues from 2028 will be managed. Governance and institutional capacity remain areas of ongoing reform, so investors should conduct thorough due diligence and use international arbitration protections. For investors with a medium-term horizon, the period before first oil offers an opportunity to establish partnerships and supply-chain positions ahead of a sharp expansion in activity.

Business networks

The Chamber of Commerce and Industry (KKF) is the main channel for company registration and domestic business liaison, and the Suriname-Guyana Chamber of Commerce supports cross-border business between two of the region's emerging energy economies. The annual Suriname Energy, Oil and Gas Summit (SEOGS), which added a Local Content Forum in 2026, is the leading gathering for the energy sector and its supply chain. SITA organises investor outreach and export promotion, while international operators such as TotalEnergies, Petronas and Newmont are useful sources of practical intelligence on supply-chain and local content opportunities. The Inter-American Development Bank and the IMF remain closely engaged on Suriname's macroeconomic and development agenda.

Who this market suits

Suriname suits investors with a medium-term horizon who want to position ahead of an oil-driven expansion: oilfield service, marine logistics and engineering firms targeting GranMorgu and Block 52 supply chains; mining and mining-services investors in the established gold sector and in bauxite; and infrastructure, construction and logistics investors meeting rising demand for port, housing and power capacity. Agribusiness, tourism and renewable energy investors can benefit from Investment Law incentives and CARICOM market access. All investors should plan for currency movements during the pre-oil period and give due weight to local partnerships and community engagement.

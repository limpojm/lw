The economy today

Serbia in November 2026 combines a competitive cost base, low taxes and established manufacturing clusters with a new political chapter. The Student List won the 25 October 2026 early parliamentary election with 53% of the vote and about 140 of 250 seats on preliminary results, ending the long period of rule by the Serbian Progressive Party, and a new government is due to be formed after the National Assembly convenes. The economy grew about 2% in 2025, held back by a year of protests, and the GDP flash estimate showed 3.6% year-on-year growth in the second quarter of 2026. Goods exports rose about 8% in 2025, led by the automotive industry. Inflation was 2.2% in August 2026, within the central bank's target band, and the IMF projects foreign direct investment to recover from 2.6% of GDP in 2025 to 4.1% in 2026. Expo 2027, for which 118 countries and organisations have confirmed participation, is driving construction and infrastructure investment in Belgrade.

Serbia's main structural challenge is restoring investor confidence after the sharp fall in foreign investment in 2025 while managing energy-sector exposure and completing the political transition. The oil company NIS, majority-owned by Russia's Gazprom Neft, is under US sanctions; Hungary's MOL signed a binding agreement in January 2026 to buy the Russian stake, but completion still requires separate US approval. The starting position is sound: public debt is estimated at 44.8% of GDP in 2025, the deficit was 2.4% of GDP, and reserves cover about 6.7 months of imports. A new government with a strong anti-corruption and institutional-reform mandate, the Commission's view that Serbia is ready to open Cluster 3, and an IMF-backed reform programme create a clear opportunity for investors positioning ahead of renewed EU convergence.

How do I get in?

Development Agency of Serbia (RAS) provides investor facilitation, site selection and access to state incentives for job-creating investments

A 15% flat corporate income tax, with a 10-year corporate tax exemption for companies that invest at least RSD 1 billion (about €8.5 million) and employ at least 100 new workers

Fifteen licensed free zones, including Belgrade, Novi Sad, Subotica and Kragujevac, offering exemption from customs duties and VAT on imported equipment and materials for export production

EU candidate status: accession negotiations began in January 2014 and 22 of 35 chapters are open, with the Commission assessing Cluster 3 (competitiveness and growth) as ready to open

Dinar monetary framework under the National Bank of Serbia, with the key policy rate at 5.75% and an inflation target of 3% ±1.5 percentage points

IMF Policy Coordination Instrument, a non-financial programme whose third review was completed in June 2026, anchoring fiscal and energy-sector reform

Expo 2027 Belgrade procurement and its related transport and urban infrastructure programme

Trade and market access

Serbia's trade is closely tied to the EU, its largest market, under its relationship as a candidate country negotiating membership since 2014, and it sits at the heart of the Western Balkans on the Pan-European corridor linking Central Europe with Greece and Turkey. Free zones near the Hungarian, Romanian, Bulgarian and North Macedonian borders allow duty-free import of inputs for export production, and goods exports grew about 8% in 2025, led by vehicles and tyres. The Danube and Sava rivers provide inland waterway links to Central Europe and the Black Sea, and the Expo 2027 programme is adding rail and river infrastructure in Belgrade. Opening Cluster 3 of the accession talks would deepen alignment with EU single-market rules and strengthen Serbia's role as a nearshoring base for European supply chains.

The overall risk picture

Serbia's risk profile is that of an EU candidate country with moderate public debt, a stable monetary framework and a low-tax, export-oriented manufacturing base. The October 2026 election produced a clear result and a peaceful handover is under way, which should end the uncertainty created by more than a year of protests; investors should allow time for the new government to set out its programme. The most important legal consideration is that NIS, the oil company majority-owned by Gazprom Neft, is subject to US sanctions; the OFAC licence issued to MOL in November 2026 covers only preparatory steps and does not authorise completion of the sale, so any dealings with NIS require specialist sanctions advice. Outside the energy sector, Serbia offers investors cost advantages, free-zone benefits and an IMF-anchored fiscal framework, and progress in EU accession talks would bring further gains.

Business networks

The American Chamber of Commerce in Serbia, the Foreign Investors Council, the German-Serbian Chamber of Commerce, the British-Serbian Chamber and the Chamber of Commerce and Industry of Serbia are the main business associations and maintain regular dialogue with government. The Development Agency of Serbia promotes the country at international forums and supports investors, while Expo 2027 Belgrade is creating new platforms for business events and country partnerships. The European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, the European Investment Bank, the World Bank and the IMF are active in energy, transport and reform programmes, and EU pre-accession funds support infrastructure and institution-building. Embassies of EU member states, the UK and the US maintain commercial sections that are a useful source of introductions.

Who this market suits

Serbia suits investors seeking a low-cost, low-tax manufacturing and services base close to the EU: automotive, electronics and metals manufacturers using free zones in Vojvodina, Šumadija and southern Serbia; IT and business-services companies drawing on talent in Belgrade and Novi Sad; construction, hospitality and infrastructure firms targeting Expo 2027 and its legacy projects; and long-term investors positioning for faster EU convergence under a new reform-minded government. Investors in oil, gas and fuel should take sanctions advice on NIS before any engagement.
